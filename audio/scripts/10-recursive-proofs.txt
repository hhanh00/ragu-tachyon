Chapter ten. Recursive proofs.

Chapter two set a goal in which a node's proof covers the node's own step and also the proofs of its children, and it left open how one proof can contain a check of another. The previous chapter then built a complete proof and listed what its verifier pays. This chapter connects the two. We write the verifier as gates, place those gates inside the circuit of the next step, and measure the result.

The measurement shows that the previous chapter's verifier cannot work in this role, and it pins down the reason. We begin with a chain of steps and then move to trees, base cases, and initial states. The chapters that follow repair the verifier, so this one ends with a precise list of what they have to deliver.

A chain of steps.

We start with a sequence of states in which each state comes from the previous one. We call the rule the step function. A step function may also take a private input at each step. The Ragu Book's example is a blockchain virtual machine, whose state is the chain's state and whose private input is the next batch of transactions. Proving such a sequence one step at a time is called incrementally verifiable computation, the term chapter two introduced.

For numbers we reuse the cubic from the arithmetization chapter, now applied repeatedly modulo forty-three. The step function takes a value, cubes it, adds the value, and adds five, with no private input. Starting from three, the states are thirty-five, then one, seven, eleven, and fourteen. The first transition is the statement the previous chapter proved.

Now suppose a verifier wants to know the fifth state and doesn't want to run the steps itself. The tools we have so far offer two ways to arrange the proving. One option puts every step into one large circuit. Each application of the step takes two gates, and a circuit with n gates needs four n generators, where n is a power of two. So one thousand twenty-four steps take two thousand forty-eight gates and eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators. The generators grow with the whole computation, and the prover must know the number of steps in advance and hold every private input at once.

The other option gives each step its own proof. That avoids those problems, but one thousand twenty-four steps then leave the verifier with one thousand twenty-four proofs to check. The target of recursion is one proof to check however long the chain becomes, with a prover who handles one step at a time.

A proof about a verifier.

Consider the prover at some step in the chain. It holds the current state and a proof that this state is correct. It could check that proof itself, compute the next state, and then prove that it did both of those things. A verifier of the new proof would then have no reason to look at the old proof, because the new proof already says that the old one passed its check.

This idea only requires us to treat the verifier as one more computation to be proved. The new relation has the next state as its public input. Its witness holds the current state and the old proof. It checks two things, that the step function maps the current state to the next state, and that the verifier accepts the old proof for the current state.

The old proof now sits in the witness, in the same place that a secret input usually sits. The old state sits there as well, so the verifier of the new proof sees neither of them. The same value of the state must feed both checks, or else the prover could verify a proof about one state and apply the step to a different one. In a circuit, this means that both checks read one wire. The book writes this relation as the plainest way to build incrementally verifiable computation from a succinct proof system.

Unwinding the relation shows what a single proof covers. The second proof says that one is the step applied to thirty-five, and that the first proof verifies for thirty-five. The first proof in turn says that thirty-five is the step applied to three. So a verifier holding the state one and the second proof runs one check, and that check covers both applications of the step. Everything now depends on writing the verifier as gates, so we do that next.

Writing the verifier as gates.

A circuit, in the sense of the arithmetization chapter, is a list of multiplication gates and linear constraints among the wires. Multiplying a wire by a constant or adding two wires costs no gate, because linear constraints can absorb those operations. Each other multiplication costs one gate. An inversion also costs one gate, since proving that one wire inverts another takes a single multiplication that must equal one.

We take the short proof from the previous chapter, since it has the smallest verifier we've seen, and call it the level-zero proof. It covers the state thirty-five, with that chapter's hand-picked challenges. The level-one circuit has to establish that the next state is correct and that the level-zero proof verifies.

Let's look at a few of its gates, with the wires holding the values from that run, modulo forty-three. The step part multiplies thirty-five by thirty-five to get twenty-one, and then multiplies twenty-one by thirty-five to get four. A linear constraint adds four, thirty-five, and five to get one, the public next state. The verifier part multiplies thirty by forty-two to get thirteen, which is the public input value of the inner proof. Three more gates produce the values three, one, and two, and a linear constraint carries out the split check, requiring one plus two to equal three. Another linear constraint requires the proof's target value of thirteen to equal the computed thirteen.

The thirty in that verifier gate is where the two checks meet. It is the state thirty-five minus five, read from the same wire that the step's gates read. In a standalone run, the state was a known number and the public input value was a matter of arithmetic. Inside this circuit the state is a wire, so this multiplication needs a gate of its own.

The level-one circuit is our own teaching construction and not a Ragu circuit. We only build and check its gates, and we don't prove it, because the toy curve's forty-three points are too few to serve as distinct generators for a circuit of this size.

Point operations.

The verifier also adds and scales points, and those operations reduce to gates in the same way. To add two points, the prover supplies the slope of the line through them as a witness. Three gates then check the slope and build the result, assuming the two points have different horizontal coordinates, and a circuit must deal with the other case separately. Doubling takes a fourth gate, because it computes a square for the tangent slope. Ragu's point gadget uses these same formulas.

A scalar multiplication repeats these operations once per bit of the scalar. In the plainest form, each lower bit doubles the running point, adds the base point, and selects one of the two results according to the bit, which costs two more gates. Each bit also needs one gate to show that it is zero or one.

Here the toy curve's fields don't line up. The polynomials in our proof have coefficients modulo forty-three, so the circuit's wires are modulo forty-three. The commitments are points whose coordinates are modulo thirty-one, and a wire modulo forty-three can't directly do arithmetic modulo thirty-one. Emulating the other field is possible but costs far more than the counts below. We set this aside until the chapter on curve cycles, which removes the mismatch. To count gates at their native cost, the next example runs on the toy curve's partner curve, whose coordinates are modulo forty-three.

Take a point on that partner curve and the scalar twenty-three, which has five bits. The circuit spends five gates to show that the digits are bits, sixteen on doublings, twelve on additions, and eight on selecting by each bit, for forty-one gates in total. Its output wires hold the same point as twenty-three times the base point computed outside the circuit. In general, a scalar of some number of bits costs ten gates per bit, minus nine. That is fifty-one gates for the six-bit scalars of our toy field, and two thousand five hundred forty-one gates for a two hundred fifty-five bit scalar.

This count describes our plain construction and not an optimized one. Real circuits lower the cost per scalar multiplication, and the endoscalars chapter covers Ragu's main technique. We'll see shortly that this kind of saving doesn't change the conclusion.

The hash.

A non-interactive verifier gets its challenges by hashing the transcript, so the level-one circuit has to do the hashing too. It may look tempting to save those gates by giving the circuit its challenges as inputs and leaving the hash to whoever verifies the outer proof. The trouble is that the outer verifier doesn't hold the inner transcript, and the prover fills in every witness wire.

Here is the resulting attack. Take the false trace with the input four and the statement thirty-five. Of the one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of challenges, forty-three satisfy the revdot equation for this trace. All of them have the challenge y equal to zero, because the gates of this trace hold and only the constraint weighted by y cubed fails. If the circuit read its challenges from the witness, the prover could set y to zero, and every field check in the level-one circuit would pass. At the hand-picked challenges from before, the same false trace fails.

So a circuit recomputes each challenge from the inner proof's messages, using gates. This is the reason for choosing a hash like Poseidon, whose only nonlinear operation is a small power. On the toy sponge, replaying the transcript for eight coefficients takes seventeen permutations. Each permutation has thirty-two S-boxes, and each S-box computes a fifth power with three gates. So the replay costs one thousand six hundred thirty-two gates.

What the verifier costs.

We now have a gate price for every step of the previous chapter's verifier, so we can measure it. Start with the toy proof, using six-bit scalars. The step itself took two gates, and verifying the proof of that same step takes two thousand seven hundred forty-one. Most of those come from replaying the transcript, at one thousand six hundred thirty-two gates, and from twenty scalar multiplications at fifty-one gates each. The field rows come from running the gates on the values of the actual proof, and a check confirms that the trace and the formula agree.

At this tiny size the fixed costs dominate, so these counts say little about growth. To see the growth, we move to the size Ragu uses. Its production rank gives polynomials two to the thirteen coefficients and circuits at most two to the eleven gates. We keep the same plain gadgets with two hundred fifty-five bit scalars, and we assume a larger circuit has the same density of wiring as our toy. Both choices are ours and not Ragu's.

At this size the verifier takes about twenty-one million gates. Three rows grow linearly. Computing the wiring value takes twenty thousand four hundred seventy-four gates. Computing the coefficients of the challenge polynomial takes eight thousand two hundred three. Computing the combined generator, the point the inner product argument reduces its generators to, takes about twenty point eight million. All the other rows together are small, led by about eighty-one thousand gates for the other scalar multiplications and three thousand five hundred fifty-two for the hash.

The linear rows account for ninety-nine point six percent of the gates. A circuit at this size has room for two thousand forty-eight gates. The coefficients of the challenge polynomial alone need more than that. And the combined generator would exceed the limit even if a scalar multiplication cost one gate, because there are eight thousand one hundred ninety-two of them.

These are the two costs the previous chapter identified, computing the wiring value and computing the combined generator, with the coefficients counted as part of the second. The last comparison needs no cost model, because it only compares two counts. A verifier that performs four n scalar multiplications cannot fit in a circuit of n gates.

Why the linear steps cannot be dropped.

Since these steps are the whole problem, it's natural to ask what happens if the circuit leaves them out and reads the two values from the proof. Each attempt opens a specific attack.

Start with the wiring value, which is the verifier's only contact with the circuit it thinks it is checking. The input four doesn't satisfy our statement, since it gives thirty, not thirty-five. It does satisfy a different circuit, where the cube plus thirteen times the input equals thirty, and that circuit differs from ours in one wiring coefficient. A prover who builds its proof from that other circuit passes the split and target checks if the verifier reads the wiring value from the proof, which would be twenty-four. When the verifier computes the value from our circuit it gets twenty-three, and the split check fails. So without its own wiring value, the verifier cannot tell which circuit a proof belongs to.

The combined generator plays a comparable role in the inner product argument, because it connects the final equation to the public generators. Suppose the prover lies about the batched value and claims thirty-seven where the truth is thirty-six, keeping every round point and the final scalar from the honest run. If the verifier computes the combined generator itself, then of the forty-three possible values, only thirty-six passes the final equation with this final scalar. If the verifier accepts the combined generator from the proof, the prover can solve that equation for the point. This takes only point additions and scalar multiplications, and it succeeds for all forty-three values.

A prover facing the honest combined generator could still look for a different final scalar, but that means solving a discrete log, which the toy curve permits and a real curve doesn't. The false generator needs no such thing. So both linear steps have to happen somewhere, and the question left is whether they have to happen inside every step's circuit.

Why the circuit cannot keep growing.

So far we have priced one level of recursion. Now consider many levels. A circuit at one level holds the step and a verifier for the level below, so its number of gates equals the step's gates plus the verifier's gates for the circuit below.

Suppose first that the verifier is linear, costing some constant times the size of the circuit it checks. Expanding the first few levels shows a pattern. The size at any level is that constant raised to the level, times the starting size, plus the step's gates times a growing sum of powers of the constant. If the constant is below one, the sum converges and the size settles. If the constant is one or more, the size grows without limit, and above one it grows exponentially with the level.

Our rows give the value of the constant. Per gate of the inner circuit, the wiring value costs ten gates and the coefficients cost four. The combined generator costs four scalar multiplications. With a scalar multiplication at two thousand five hundred forty-one gates, the constant is ten thousand one hundred seventy-eight. Even the impossible cost of one gate per scalar multiplication gives eighteen. No improvement to scalar multiplication can bring the constant below one, because the verifier has four scalar multiplications to perform per gate. This is why the optimized gadgets we mentioned don't change the conclusion.

Now compare a verifier without the linear steps. Its remaining rows grow with the logarithm of the circuit's size. A logarithm grows more slowly than its input, so there is a size at which a circuit is large enough to hold the verifier of a circuit of its own size.

We ran both recurrences from our two-gate step, rounding each circuit up to the power of two it needs. With the linear steps, level one takes fifty-two thousand five hundred forty-three gates, which needs a size of two to the sixteen. Level two needs two to the thirty, level three needs two to the forty-four, level four needs two to the fifty-eight, and level five needs two to the seventy-two. Without the linear steps, level one needs two to the fifteen, and level two needs two to the seventeen. Level three takes one hundred sixteen thousand eight hundred gates, and then the count stops changing. A circuit of two to the seventeen gates can verify a proof from a circuit of the same size.

Once the size stops changing, one fixed circuit serves every step, and that circuit verifies proofs made by itself. The prover's work per step is then constant, and the proof's history can be arbitrarily long. A growing circuit would also fail for a blunter reason, since a circuit can never exceed the size allowed by the public generators, which are fixed in advance.

The settled size of two to the seventeen is well above Ragu's limit of two to the eleven gates. That gap comes from our plain gadgets and our single large circuit. The later chapters close it with other techniques, and the chapter on recursion in Ragu assembles them.

What succinct verification has to mean.

The requirement from chapter two can now be stated precisely. The number of gates in the part of the verifier that runs inside the circuit must grow more slowly than the circuit it verifies. This concerns the in-circuit work and not the length of the proof. Ragu's proofs stay large while they are being fused, and this is allowed, provided that the circuit doesn't read all of a proof's contents.

There have been two main routes to a verifier like this. Earlier recursive systems used proofs with a constant-time verifier, so the verifier could be encoded directly and the circuit converged. The book describes the resulting systems as barely practical, and they needed very large curves, complicated assumptions, and trusted setups. Chapter one explained why Tachyon rules out a trusted setup.

The second route came from Halo. We could read only that paper's abstract, so our account of it follows the Ragu Book. Halo keeps the linear verifier, and it relies on something our attacks did not rule out, which is postponing the expensive steps. A false claim can still be recorded and caught later. In our example the prover's false combined generator differs from the true one, and one multi-scalar multiplication performed by anyone, at any later time, exposes the claim.

Halo's insight was that the circuit can carry such a claim forward in its instance, and that a cheap random check can merge two claims into one. Only the last claim in a long history then needs its multi-scalar multiplication, and that one stands for every earlier one. The relation gains an object called an accumulator, which holds the postponed claims. The checks become the step function together with a check that the accumulator was updated correctly, and the final verifier decides the last accumulator itself. The next chapter builds this for the combined generator, and the chapter on wiring and the registry does the corresponding work for the wiring value.

From a chain to a tree.

A chain makes each prover wait for the one before it, so independent parties need the tree form from chapter two. The change to the relation is direct. A node now has a left and a right input, each with its own state and proof, and the step function combines them. Following the book's two-input proof-carrying data, the circuit checks the step and runs the verifier twice, once per child.

Chapter two's tally is a computation of this form. Carol joined the range zero to four, with total twenty-five, and the range four to eight, with total twenty-two, into the range zero to eight, with total forty-seven. Her circuit checks that the two ranges meet at four and that twenty-five plus twenty-two is forty-seven, and it runs the verifier once on the proof of each input header.

Each verifier reads a child proof, and that proof may come from a leaf or from a large subtree. The verifier can't tell the difference, because a child proof looks the same at every depth. So the same circuit must serve every node, and this is why all four hundred twenty-nine tree shapes over chapter two's list can use it.

Having two verifiers doubles the verifier term in the recurrence. With the linear steps, level one already needs two to the seventeen gates, where the chain needed two to the sixteen, and level two needs two to the thirty-two. Without them the size again settles, now at two to the eighteen compared with the chain's two to the seventeen. So a second input is a small price once the verifier is sublinear, and it makes the linear case worse. Accumulation also has slightly more work to do in a tree, because a node holds two incoming accumulators and merges both into one.

Ragu takes this form for every node. As chapter two explained, a chain step declares a unit header for its right input, so a chain is a lopsided tree, and each node verifies two proofs whether or not both inputs carry data. For the eight-entry tally, a balanced tree has fifteen nodes and verifies thirty proofs inside circuits, while a chain has eight nodes and verifies sixteen. Both shapes end with one proof for the final verifier to check. The chain uses fewer nodes, while the balanced tree lets separate parties work at the same time, and a single circuit design supports both.

The base case.

The relations so far say that every state has a proof behind it, but the first state has no earlier proof to point to. The circuit needs some way to skip the verifier at the start. That skip is dangerous to add, since any prover who can trigger it escapes the check. To see how the design of the skip decides what a proof means, consider three versions of the rule for our chain. We computed everything each rule can produce modulo forty-three.

In the first design, the prover sets a flag, and the flag skips the verifier. The prover can raise the flag at any time, so it can claim any output of the step function without any history, at any claimed step. That allows twenty-nine of the forty-three values for the fifth state.

The second design adds a step counter to the header, and the flag works only at step zero. Now the proof really does cover five applications of the step. It still leaves the start value open, so the prover may begin wherever it likes, and fifteen of the forty-three values are reachable that way.

The third design carries the start value in the header, and the flag requires the header's current state to equal it. This gives forty-three possible headers overall. Once the verifier requires the start value three, exactly one remains, with fourteen as the fifth state. This comparison is the same one that chapter two required of any verifier. A valid proof shows only that the rules produced this header, so the verifier has to confirm that the header is the one it wanted.

The level-zero proof from the previous chapter has no check on its start at all. Its public input is thirty-five and its start value is secret, so it shows only that something maps to thirty-five. That is what the statement of that chapter said, and it is reasonable on its own, though a chain that needs a particular start would have to add one of these rules.

The base case in Ragu.

Ragu doesn't place a skip in each step's circuit. Chapter two described its arrangement, in which a single internal bootstrap step takes synthesized proofs and produces a unit proof that attests nothing, and every leaf uses that proof as an ordinary child. Two more details of the code explain how narrow this is. The code computes the condition from the input suffixes, and no application step can declare the reserved dummy suffix. What the condition releases is the final check on the folded revdot value, in a circuit called collapse and in its counterpart on the nested side. The application has no flag to set. A leaf's truth comes from the checks in the application's own step and from the verifier's final comparison, as in the third design.

The Ragu Book's two descriptions differ here, and the code matches one. The book's accumulation chapter says that at step zero, the accumulators take a placeholder value, the previous instance satisfies its checks by construction, and the fuse circuit skips the rest of its work. The book's chapter on proof-carrying data, and the code at the commit this site pins, place the base case in one internal step.

In the code, the dummy proofs don't verify on their own, and a setup function runs this step once when the application is set up. The circuits still run with the final check relaxed on each side of the cycle, as far as we can read them. Each later leaf is an ordinary fuse whose child claims are enforced. This site follows the code, and we read the accumulation chapter's version as a simplified account for teaching incrementally verifiable computation.

Tracking initial states.

The third design works for a chain because a chain has one start. A tree has one start for each leaf, and a verifier may care which leaves went into the result, yet the header must stay a fixed size. The book's answer is to keep a Merkle root of the initial states. Each leaf's header holds a hash of its start value, and each node's circuit hashes its children's roots to get its own. The cost is one hash per node, and the root stays one field element however many leaves it covers.

We can run this on the first four values of chapter two's list, four, eleven, three, and seven, using the toy sponge as the hash and a fixed tag to mark leaves. The four leaves give the roots eleven, twenty-two, twenty-six, and thirty-two. Hashing them in pairs gives eighteen and sixteen, and hashing those gives the top root, twenty-four.

A verifier who knows the four start values can recompute the root and compare it with the final header, and this check is sensitive to the start values. Replacing the start value of the third leaf with each of the other forty-two values changes the root in forty-one cases. The remaining case is a collision, which a hash with only forty-three outputs can't avoid, and a two hundred fifty-five bit output makes finding one infeasible.

Here the Ragu Book and the code differ. The book says the protocol maintains a root of initial states on each side of the cycle and updates it in each step. In the code at the commit this site pins, we found no such value. The instance shared by the internal circuits holds commitments, challenges, and two values from the batching and revdot checks, and the phrase appears nowhere else in the crates. This site follows the code, in which initial states belong to the application. Any record of where a computation began goes in the application's header, under rules set by its steps.

The next reading is reconstructed from the code, and the authors have not confirmed it. Read this way, the examples we already have fit the same pattern. Chapter two's tally records a range of positions, and its entry step checks each value against the list. The book's getting-started guide builds a Merkle tree in application headers and steps, which is the calculation we just did, carried out by an application. Tachyon's fee seed step leaves its anchor as a free witness, and the Tachyon book says that validators confirm it against the chain. In each case the application chooses what to record and what the verifier compares.

Why cheating fails.

The argument from chapter two walks down the tree from a false header to a step whose check rejects it. Recursion needs a slightly stronger property than that walk first suggests, because of what the relation claims about the old proof. Accepted proofs of false statements exist as mathematical objects, and they are only hard to find. On the toy curve, the previous chapter found one by using a relation among the generators. So the claim that some accepted proof exists says little on its own, since it is usually true.

What the walk needs is the claim that the prover knows such a proof. The K in NARK stands for this, and the chapter on statements and challenges gave the informal idea with Schnorr's protocol. A prover able to produce an accepted proof must be able to produce a witness for it. Here the witness contains the previous proof, so the same reasoning applies again at the level below, until the walk reaches the base case. The walk therefore ends at a step whose own check rejects the false header, unless the prover has broken one of the following assumptions.

The first assumption is that nobody knows a relation among the generators, which is the discrete log relation assumption. The second is that the transcript hash behaves as a random oracle, and Ragu makes this assumption about Poseidon. The assumption has to stretch further here than in a standalone proof, because the circuit contains the hash that the verifier replays, while an actual random oracle could not be written as gates.

The third assumption is that the circuit wires its checks together as we required. The state is one wire, each challenge comes from the hash, and the wiring value comes from the correct circuit. The fourth is that the steps are correct, and that the verifier compares the final header with what it wants to know.

Under these assumptions, a changed inner proof leaves the level-one circuit with no satisfying witness. Starting from the honest level-zero proof, we changed each of its five evaluations to each of the forty-two other values, which makes two hundred ten altered proofs. All of them left the circuit unsatisfied. We also kept the proof and replaced its public state with each of the forty-two other values, and all forty-two failed the target check. In these runs the challenges stay fixed, and a real transcript would also change them.

This is an informal argument. Formal treatments have to account for repeating the extraction over the depth of the recursion and for the hash appearing in the circuit, and this site leaves those out.

Recursion in Ragu.

The operation that builds these proofs is called fuse, and its signature names the pieces of the two-input relation. It takes a step and that step's witness, along with a left proof and a right proof, each carrying its header data. It returns the new header with its new proof.

The rest of this description is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. A step's circuit exposes the left, right, and output headers as its output. The collapse circuit computes each child's public input value and enforces it, which plays the part of the public input gate we built earlier. A circuit called hashes one starts the replay of the transcript inside a circuit, as we required.

No single verifier circuit exists. With at most two to the eleven gates per circuit, the verifier cannot fit in one, so Ragu divides its work among internal circuits, and the application circuit holds only the step. A Ragu proof is really a set of claims about several circuits, and the revdot folding chapter explains how they combine. The book's accumulation chapter describes one application circuit and one fuse circuit per curve, and we read that fuse circuit as a summary of those internal circuits.

Recursion is also often used to hide data, because an inner proof sits in the outer proof's witness and a zero-knowledge outer proof would conceal it. That benefit isn't available yet. As chapter two recorded, neither the Ragu Book nor the code at the commit this site pins establishes zero knowledge, and a proof still exposes child data. Tachyon, which has not activated on mainnet and whose design is still changing, depends on fusing in the way we described, through a different Ragu revision.

Where this leaves us.

Incrementally verifiable computation applies a step function repeatedly and keeps one proof, whose relation checks the step and also checks a verifier run on the previous proof. We can write that verifier as gates, with its field arithmetic, its group operations, and its transcript hash all inside the circuit, and a shared wire ties the inner proof's public input to the step's state.

The previous chapter's verifier has two linear costs, computing the wiring value and computing the combined generator. At Ragu's circuit size the second can't fit, whatever a scalar multiplication costs. Neither step can be dropped. Without its own wiring value, the verifier can't tell which circuit a proof belongs to, and without its own combined generator, a prover can pass with a false value.

With a linear verifier the circuit grows at every level, and with a sublinear one it settles at a fixed size, so one circuit can verify its own proofs. Trees use a relation with two inputs and two verifiers, and the settled circuit handles every tree shape. The base case has to be confined so that no prover can skip a verifier at will. Ragu confines it to one internal step and leaves initial states to the application, although the book describes a tracked root.

The two linear steps still have to happen somewhere. The next chapter postpones the computation of the combined generator by turning it into a claim that travels with the proof, and it shows how two such claims merge into one.
