Chapter ten. Recursive proofs.

Chapter two set a goal in which a node's proof covers the node's own step and also the proofs of its children, and it left open how one proof can contain a check of another. Chapter nine then built a complete proof and listed what its verifier pays. This chapter connects the two. We write the verifier as gates, place those gates inside the circuit of the next step, and measure the result.

The measurement shows that chapter nine's verifier cannot work in this role, and it pins down the reason. We begin with a chain of steps and then move to trees, base cases, and initial states. The chapters that follow repair the verifier, so this one ends with a precise list of what they have to deliver.

A chain of steps.

We start with the simplest shape, a sequence of states in which each state comes from the previous one. We call the rule the step function. A step function may also take a private input at each step. The Ragu Book's example is a blockchain virtual machine, whose state is the chain's state and whose private input is the next batch of transactions. Proving such a sequence one step at a time is the incrementally verifiable computation that chapter two named.

For numbers we reuse the cubic from the arithmetization chapter, now applied repeatedly modulo forty-three. The step function takes a value, cubes it, adds the value, and adds five, and it has no private input. Starting from the chapter's value three, the states are thirty-five, then one, seven, eleven, and fourteen. The first transition is the statement that chapter nine proved.

Now suppose a verifier wants to know the fifth state and does not want to run the steps itself. The tools we have so far offer two ways to arrange the proving. Each application of the step function takes the two gates of our circuit, and a circuit with n gates needs four n generators, where n is a power of two.

The first way puts every step into one large circuit. Three steps then take six gates and thirty-two generators, and one thousand twenty-four steps take two thousand forty-eight gates and eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators. So the generators grow in proportion to the whole computation. The prover must also know the number of steps in advance and hold every private input at once.

The second way gives each step its own proof. That avoids those problems, but the verifier's work then grows with the length of the chain, so one thousand twenty-four steps leave it one thousand twenty-four proofs to check. The target is one proof to check however long the chain becomes, with a prover who handles one step at a time.

A proof about a verifier.

Consider the prover at some step in the chain. It holds the current state and a proof that this state is correct. It could check that proof itself, compute the next state, and then prove that it did both of those things. A verifier of the new proof would then have no reason to look at the old proof, because the new proof already says that the old one passed its check.

This idea only requires us to treat the verifier as one more computation to be proved. The new relation has the next state as its public input. Its witness holds the current state and the old proof. It checks two things, that the step function maps the current state to the next state, and that the verifier accepts the old proof for the current state.

The old proof now sits in the witness, in the same place that a secret input usually sits. The old state sits there as well, which means the verifier of the new proof sees neither of them. The same value of the state must feed both checks, or else the prover could verify a proof about one state and apply the step to a different one. In a circuit, this means that both checks read one wire. The book writes this relation as the plainest way to build incrementally verifiable computation from a succinct proof system.

Picture the chain as a row of circuits. The first holds only the step, and each later one holds the step and a verifier. Those later circuits look alike, but they differ. The first verifier checks a proof from the circuit that holds only the step, while the second checks a proof from a circuit that itself contains a verifier. A later section asks whether these circuits eventually stop changing, which is what a single reusable circuit would need.

Unwinding the relation shows what a single proof covers. The second proof says that one is the step applied to thirty-five, and that the first proof verifies for thirty-five. The first proof in turn says that thirty-five is the step applied to three. So a verifier holding the state one and the second proof runs one check, and that check covers both applications of the step. Everything now depends on writing the verifier as gates, so we do that next.

Writing the verifier as gates.

A circuit, in the sense of the arithmetization chapter, is a list of multiplication gates and linear constraints among the wires. Multiplying a wire by a constant or adding two wires costs no gate, because linear constraints can absorb those operations. Each other multiplication costs one gate. An inversion also costs one gate, since proving that one wire inverts another takes a single multiplication whose result must equal one.

We take the short proof from chapter nine, since it has the smallest verifier we have seen, and call it the level-zero proof. It covers the state thirty-five, with that chapter's hand-picked challenges. The level-one circuit has to establish that the next state is correct and that the level-zero proof verifies.

Most of the verifier's gates belong to the split check, which is step seven of that chapter's run. On one side, the verifier multiplies the value of r at x by a sum of three evaluations, one of them the wiring value. On the other side, it adds the values of the two halves of the split polynomial, each scaled by a power of x. It also checks a target, that the first half's constant term equals the public input written as a polynomial in y.

The public input deserves a closer look, because it is where the two checks of the relation meet. The short proof uses chapter nine's circuit without the constant wire. So its last constraint says that the start value plus its cube equals the state minus five, because the step's constant moves to the public side. The public input is therefore the state minus five, times y cubed. For the state thirty-five, that is thirty times y cubed, as in chapter nine.

Now we can follow a few of the level-one circuit's gates, with the wires holding the values from that run, modulo forty-three. The step part multiplies thirty-five by thirty-five to get twenty-one, and then multiplies twenty-one by thirty-five to get four. A linear constraint adds four, thirty-five, and five to get one, the public next state.

The verifier part computes y cubed with two gates, giving forty-two, and multiplies thirty by forty-two to get thirteen, the public input value. Three more gates compute the products in the split check, giving three on one side, and one and two on the other. A linear constraint requires one plus two to equal three. Another requires the proof's target value of thirteen to equal the computed thirteen.

The thirty in that verifier gate is the state minus five, and forming it from the state's wire is linear, so it costs nothing. The important point is that it reads the same wire that the step's gates read. In a standalone run, the state was a known number and the public input was a matter of arithmetic. Inside this circuit the state is a wire, so the multiplication by y cubed needs a gate of its own.

These wires use chapter nine's hand-picked challenges, and a real level-one circuit would derive them with the hash gates we price shortly. The level-one circuit is also our own teaching construction and not a Ragu circuit. We only build and check its gates, and we do not prove it, because the toy curve's forty-three points are too few to serve as distinct generators for a circuit of this size.

Point operations.

The verifier also adds and scales points, and those operations reduce to gates in the same way. To add two points, the prover supplies the slope of the line through them as a witness. Three gates then check the slope and build the result. These formulas assume the two points have different horizontal coordinates, and a circuit must deal with the other case separately. Doubling takes a fourth gate, because it computes a square for the tangent slope. Ragu's point gadget uses these same formulas.

A scalar multiplication repeats these operations once per bit of the scalar. In the plainest form, each lower bit doubles the running point, adds the base point, and selects one of the two results according to the bit. Selecting costs one gate per coordinate. Each bit also needs one gate to show that it is zero or one.

Here the toy curve's fields do not line up. The polynomials in our proof have coefficients modulo forty-three, so the circuit's wires are modulo forty-three. The commitments are points whose coordinates are modulo thirty-one, and a wire modulo forty-three cannot directly do arithmetic modulo thirty-one. Emulating the other field is possible but costs far more than the counts below. We set this aside until the chapter on curve cycles, which removes the mismatch.

To count gates at their native cost, the next example runs on the toy curve's partner curve, whose coordinates are modulo forty-three. Take a point on that curve and the scalar twenty-three, which has five bits. The circuit spends five gates to show that the digits are bits, sixteen on doublings, twelve on additions, and eight on selecting by each bit, for forty-one gates in total. Its output wires hold the same point as twenty-three times the base point, computed outside the circuit.

When the top bit is one, the circuit starts the running point at the base point, so the top bit needs no doubling, addition, or selection. Every other bit costs four gates to double, three to add, and two to select, and every bit costs one gate for its bit check. That comes to ten gates per bit, minus nine.

For a two hundred fifty-five bit scalar that is two thousand five hundred forty-one gates. The toy proof's scalars are numbers modulo forty-three, which have six bits, so each of its scalar multiplications costs fifty-one gates. We need this stand-in price because the partner curve has only thirty-one points, so its own scalars have only five bits. A scalar whose top bit may be zero needs a few extra gates, which does not change how the cost grows.

This count describes our plain construction and not an optimized one. Real circuits lower the cost per scalar multiplication, and the endoscalars chapter covers Ragu's main technique. We will see shortly that this kind of saving does not change the conclusion.

The hash.

A non-interactive verifier gets its challenges by hashing the transcript, so the level-one circuit has to do the hashing too. It may look tempting to save those gates by giving the circuit its challenges as inputs and leaving the hash to whoever verifies the outer proof. The trouble is that the outer verifier does not hold the inner transcript, and the prover fills in every witness wire.

Here is the resulting attack. Take the false trace with the start value four and the statement thirty-five. Of the one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of challenges, forty-three satisfy the revdot equation for this trace, the same count as in chapter nine. All of them have the challenge y equal to zero, because the gates of this trace hold and only the constraint weighted by y cubed fails.

If the circuit read its challenges from the witness, the prover could set y to zero, and every field check in the level-one circuit would pass. At the hand-picked challenges five and seven, the same false trace fails. So a circuit recomputes each challenge from the inner proof's messages, using gates.

This is the reason for choosing a hash like Poseidon, whose only nonlinear operation is a small power. On the toy sponge, replaying the transcript for eight coefficients takes seventeen permutations. Each permutation has thirty-two S-boxes, and each S-box computes a fifth power with three gates. So the replay costs one thousand six hundred thirty-two gates.

What the verifier costs.

We now have a gate price for every row of chapter nine's cost table, so we can measure the verifier. Start with the toy proof, using six-bit scalars. The step itself took two gates, and verifying the proof of that same step takes two thousand seven hundred forty-one.

Most of that is the transcript replay, at seventeen permutations of ninety-six gates each, and twenty scalar multiplications at fifty-one gates each. The field rows come from running the gates on the values of chapter nine's proof, and the worked script confirms that each count from the run equals the formula for its row. A real circuit would share some powers between rows, which would save a handful of gates without changing how any row grows.

Three rows decide how the verifier grows, so it is worth seeing where their counts come from. The first computes the wiring value. The circuit needs six gates for the powers of x and two for the powers of y. Our circuit has eight wiring terms, and each multiplies a power of y by a power of x, except the two terms of the first constraint, whose power of y is one. That makes six more gates, for fourteen in all.

The second row computes the coefficients of the challenge polynomial from the inner product argument. The first coefficient is the product of the three inverted challenges, which takes two gates. Each of the other seven is an earlier coefficient times one squared challenge, which takes one gate each, so the row costs nine.

The third row computes the combined generator, the single point that the inner product argument reduces its generators to. It is a sum of eight generators, each scaled by one coefficient of the challenge polynomial. So it costs eight scalar multiplications at fifty-one gates each, which is four hundred eight.

At this tiny size the fixed costs dominate, so these counts say little about growth. To see the growth, we move to the size Ragu uses. Its production rank gives polynomials two to the thirteen coefficients and circuits at most two to the eleven gates. We keep the same plain gadgets with two hundred fifty-five bit scalars, and we assume that a larger circuit has the same density of wiring as our toy. Both choices are ours and not Ragu's.

The same counting then works for any size. The wiring value costs ten gates per n, minus six. The coefficients of the challenge polynomial cost about four n gates. The combined generator costs four n scalar multiplications, one per generator.

The other rows depend on the size only through the number of rounds of the inner product argument, which is thirteen at Ragu's size. The scalar multiplications come from three places. Forming the running point takes three, scaling a fixed point by the batched value takes one, each round scales its two points, and the final equation takes two more. That gives twice the rounds plus six, or thirty-two at thirteen rounds.

The transcript replay costs eleven permutations before the inner product argument, plus two for each round, because each round absorbs its two points and squeezes one challenge. The remaining field rows are tiny, two hundred six gates at Ragu's size.

Put together, the verifier takes twenty million nine hundred twenty-nine thousand six hundred nineteen gates at Ragu's size. The wiring value takes twenty thousand four hundred seventy-four. The coefficients of the challenge polynomial take eight thousand two hundred three. The combined generator takes twenty million eight hundred fifteen thousand eight hundred seventy-two. Of the rest, the other scalar multiplications take eighty-one thousand three hundred twelve, and the hash takes three thousand five hundred fifty-two.

The linear rows account for ninety-nine point six percent of the gates. A circuit at this size has room for two thousand forty-eight gates. The coefficients of the challenge polynomial alone need more than that. The combined generator would exceed the limit even if a scalar multiplication cost one gate, because there are eight thousand one hundred ninety-two of them.

These are the two costs that chapter nine identified, computing the wiring value and computing the combined generator, with the coefficients counted as part of the second. The last comparison needs no cost model, because it only compares two counts. A verifier that performs four n scalar multiplications cannot fit in a circuit of n gates.

Why the linear steps cannot be dropped.

Since these steps are the whole problem, it is natural to ask what happens if the circuit leaves them out and reads the two values from the proof. Each attempt opens a specific attack.

Start with the wiring value, which is the verifier's only contact with the circuit it thinks it is checking. The start value four does not satisfy our statement, since it gives thirty, not thirty-five. It does satisfy a different circuit, where the cube plus thirteen times the input equals thirty, and that circuit differs from ours in one wiring coefficient.

A prover who builds its proof from that other circuit passes the split and target checks if the verifier reads the wiring value from the proof, which would be twenty-four. When the verifier computes the value from our circuit it gets twenty-three, and the split check fails. So without its own wiring value, the verifier cannot tell which circuit a proof belongs to.

The combined generator plays a comparable role in the inner product argument, because it connects the final equation to the public generators. Suppose the prover lies about the batched value and claims thirty-seven where the truth is thirty-six. It keeps every round point and the final scalar, six, from the honest run. If the verifier computes the combined generator itself, then of the forty-three possible values, only thirty-six passes the final equation with this final scalar.

If the verifier accepts the combined generator from the proof, the prover can solve that equation for the point. This takes only point additions and scalar multiplications, and it succeeds for all forty-three values. A prover facing the honest combined generator could still look for a different final scalar, but that means solving a discrete log, which the toy curve permits and a real curve does not. The false generator needs no such thing.

So both linear steps have to happen somewhere, and the question left is whether they have to happen inside every step's circuit. They do not. The attacks above exploit a verifier that trusts the prover's value, and a verifier can avoid that without computing the value at once. It can record the prover's value as a claim and have the claim checked later.

A false claim stays false while it waits, so the later check still catches it. In our example, the prover's claimed generator is a different point from the true one for the same challenges. One multi-scalar multiplication, performed by anyone at any later time, exposes this claim.

This idea comes from Halo. We could read only that paper's abstract, so our account of it follows the Ragu Book. Halo's insight was that the circuit can carry such a claim forward in its instance, and that a cheap random check can merge two claims into one. Only the last claim in a long history then needs its multi-scalar multiplication, and that single one ends up standing for every earlier one.

The relation gains an object called an accumulator, which holds the postponed claims. The checks become the step function together with a check that the accumulator was updated correctly, and the final verifier decides the last accumulator itself. The next chapter builds this for the combined generator, and the chapter on wiring and the registry does the corresponding work for the wiring value.

Why the circuit cannot keep growing.

So far we have priced one level of recursion. Now consider many levels. A circuit at one level holds the step and a verifier for the level below, so its number of gates equals the step's gates plus the verifier's gates for the circuit below.

Suppose first that the verifier is linear, costing some constant times the size of the circuit it checks. Expanding the first few levels shows a pattern. The size at any level is that constant raised to the level, times the starting size, plus the step's gates times a growing sum of powers of the constant. If the constant is below one, the sum converges and the size settles. If the constant is one or more, the size grows without limit, and above one it grows exponentially with the level.

Our rows give the value of the constant. Per gate of the inner circuit, the wiring value costs ten gates and the coefficients cost four. The combined generator costs four scalar multiplications. With a scalar multiplication at two thousand five hundred forty-one gates, the constant is ten thousand one hundred seventy-eight. Even the impossible cost of one gate per scalar multiplication gives eighteen.

No improvement to scalar multiplication can bring the constant below one, because the verifier has four n of them to perform for a circuit of n gates. This is why the optimized gadgets we mentioned do not change the conclusion.

The previous section found a way to move the two linear steps out of the circuit by postponing them as claims, and the next chapters build it. For now we measure only the rows that would stay in the circuit, to learn whether moving the linear steps is worth the trouble. Those rows grow with the logarithm of the circuit's size. A logarithm grows more slowly than its input, so there is a size at which a circuit is large enough to hold the verifier of a circuit of its own size.

We ran both recurrences from our two-gate step, rounding each circuit up to the power of two it needs. With the linear steps, level one takes fifty-two thousand five hundred forty-three gates, which needs a size of two to the sixteen. Level two needs two to the thirty, level three two to the forty-four, level four two to the fifty-eight, and level five two to the seventy-two.

With the linear steps moved out, level one needs two to the fifteen, and level two needs two to the seventeen. From level three on, the count stays at one hundred sixteen thousand eight hundred gates, because a circuit of two to the seventeen gates can verify a proof from a circuit of the same size. At that size, the scalar multiplications and the transcript replay hold one hundred sixteen thousand five hundred eight of those gates. So what remains in the circuit is almost entirely group operations and hashing.

That settled size leaves out a cost. Moving a linear step out of the circuit does not make it disappear, because the circuit must then check that the postponed claim was handled correctly. The next chapter prices the verifier's side of the merge that postpones the combined generator. Adding it at every level moves the settled size from two to the seventeen to two to the eighteen, and whatever replaces the wiring value adds more.

So the settled size is a lower bound. What this section needs is only that some settled size exists, and it does, because each added cost is still logarithmic.

For a chain with one step function, once the size stops changing, one fixed circuit serves every step, and that circuit verifies proofs made by itself. The prover's work per step is then constant, and the proof's history can be arbitrarily long. A growing circuit would also fail for a blunter reason, since a circuit can never exceed the size allowed by the public generators, which are fixed in advance.

Even the lower bound is far above Ragu's limit of two to the eleven gates. That gap comes from our plain gadgets and our single large circuit. The later chapters close it with other techniques, and the chapter on recursion in Ragu assembles them.

What succinct verification has to mean.

The last section showed that the in-circuit part of the verifier has to grow more slowly than the circuit it verifies, which makes the requirement from chapter two precise. One point remains to add. The requirement concerns the work done inside the circuit and not the length of the proof. Ragu's proofs stay large while they are being fused, and this is allowed, provided that the circuit does not read all of a proof's contents.

There have been two main routes to a verifier like this. Earlier recursive systems used proofs with a constant-time verifier, so the verifier could be encoded directly and the circuit converged. The book describes the resulting systems as barely practical, and they needed very large curves, complicated assumptions, and trusted setups.

Those proofs were pairing-based SNARKs. A pairing is an extra map, available only on certain curves, that lets a verifier check a polynomial evaluation in constant time. Recursing on such proofs required a cycle of two curves that both have one, and the known cycles of that kind use large curves on which arithmetic is slow. Chapter one explained why Tachyon rules out a trusted setup.

The second route is Halo's, which we met at the end of the section on dropping the linear steps. It keeps the linear verifier, postpones the expensive steps as claims, and merges the claims so that one final multi-scalar multiplication settles all of them. Ragu follows this route.

From a chain to a tree.

A chain makes each prover wait for the one before it, so independent parties need the tree form from chapter two. The change to the relation is direct. A node now has a left and a right input, each with its own state and proof, and the step function combines them. Following the book's two-input proof-carrying data, the circuit checks the step and runs the verifier twice, once per child.

Chapter two's tally is a computation of this form. Carol joined the range zero to four, with total twenty-five, and the range four to eight, with total twenty-two, into the range zero to eight, with total forty-seven. Her circuit checks that the two ranges meet at four and that twenty-five plus twenty-two is forty-seven. It also runs the verifier twice, once on the proof of each input header.

Each verifier reads a child proof, and that proof may come from a leaf or from a large subtree. It may also come from a different step, since a join can take a header made by an entry step or by another join. So the verifier has to be the same at every node and accept a child proof whatever step and whatever depth produced it, while the step part of each circuit can differ.

A tree with several step functions thus has several circuits that share one verifier. The earlier picture of one circuit verifying its own proofs becomes several circuits verifying each other's proofs. Once the verifier is uniform in this way, all four hundred twenty-nine tree shapes over chapter two's list can use it.

A uniform verifier runs into the first attack from earlier. The wiring value depends on the circuit, so a verifier that accepts proofs from both the entry step and the join step needs the right wiring value for whichever circuit produced the child. That attack showed that the verifier cannot read this value, or the choice of circuit, from the proof. A prover would then pick a circuit that its false witness satisfies. The chapter on wiring and the registry explains how Ragu learns which circuit made a child without trusting the prover.

Having two verifiers doubles the verifier term in the recurrence. With the linear steps, level one already needs two to the seventeen gates, where the chain needed two to the sixteen, and level two needs two to the thirty-two. With them moved out, the size again settles, now at two to the eighteen compared with the chain's two to the seventeen. Doubling the verifier work added one to the exponent.

So a second input is a small price once the verifier is sublinear, and it makes the linear case worse. Accumulation also has slightly more work to do in a tree, because a node holds two incoming accumulators and merges both into one.

Ragu takes this form for every node. As chapter two said, a step that needs fewer than two inputs fills the others with the unit header, which carries no data. Supporting chains, leaves, and joins as separate cases would mean a separate mechanism for each, so Ragu gives every node two inputs instead. A unit input still needs a proof for the node's verifier to check, and the next section explains where that proof comes from. The proof tree chapter applies the same rule to Tachyon's steps.

The base case.

The relations so far say that every state has a proof behind it, but the first state has no earlier proof to point to. The circuit needs some way to skip the verifier at the start. That skip is dangerous to add, since any prover who can trigger it escapes the check. To see how the design of the skip decides what a proof means, consider three versions of the rule for our chain. In each one, a flag is a witness wire in the step circuit, and when the flag is set, that circuit skips its verifier.

In the first design the header holds only the state, and nothing restricts when the prover sets the flag. It can raise the flag at any step, skip the verifier there, and claim any output of the step function without any history. A header like this cannot even say how many steps happened.

The second design adds a step counter to the header and lets the flag work only at step zero. So a proof at step five really does cover five applications of the step. It still leaves the start value open, so the prover may begin wherever it likes.

The third design closes that gap by carrying the start value in the header. At step zero the flag requires the current state to equal the start value, and every later step copies the start value from its input header to its output header unchanged. Without the copying rule a step could replace the start value along the way, and the field would record nothing. With it, the verifier can compare the start value with the start it cares about.

This comparison is the same one that chapter two required of any verifier. A valid proof shows only that the rules produced this header, so the verifier has to confirm that the header is the one it wanted.

We confirmed this reasoning by running each rule over all forty-three field elements and counting the states a valid proof can claim, at step five for the two designs that count steps. In the first design a flagged step may take any input, and the step function maps the forty-three values onto twenty-nine. So a proof can claim any of those twenty-nine states.

In the second design the prover still picks any start, but then has to apply the step five times. Each application shrinks the set of reachable values, from forty-three to twenty-nine, twenty-two, nineteen, seventeen, and finally fifteen. The third design has forty-three headers at step five, one for each start. Once the verifier requires the start value three, exactly one remains, with fourteen as the fifth state.

The level-zero proof from chapter nine has no check on its start at all. Its public input is thirty-five and its start value is secret, so it shows only that something maps to thirty-five. That is what the statement of that chapter said, and it is a reasonable statement on its own, though a chain that needs a particular start would have to add one of these rules.

The base case in Ragu.

Ragu does not place a skip in each step's circuit. It performs the skip once, in an internal step called the bootstrap step. That step takes two placeholder proofs, skips their checks, and outputs a valid proof of the unit header, which attests nothing. Application steps cannot use this skip.

So each leaf starts by fusing its own step with the bootstrap proof as both children, and its circuit checks those children like any others. The application has no flag to set. A leaf's truth comes from the checks in the application's own step and from the verifier's final comparison, as in the third design.

The Ragu Book's two descriptions differ here, and the code matches one. The book's accumulation chapter says that at step zero, the accumulators take a placeholder value, the previous instance satisfies its checks by construction, and the fuse circuit skips the rest of its work.

The book's chapter on proof-carrying data, and the code at the commit this site pins, place the base case in one internal step. In the code, the placeholder proofs do not verify on their own, and Ragu runs the bootstrap step once when the application is set up. The bootstrap step's input type is private to Ragu, and application code cannot declare it.

A leaf's fuse then enforces the children's claims in the ordinary way. The chapter on recursion in Ragu shows which internal check the bootstrap step relaxes. This reading is reconstructed from the code, and the authors have not confirmed it. This site follows the code, and we read the accumulation chapter's version as a simplified account for teaching incrementally verifiable computation.

With unit inputs backed by the bootstrap proof, we can count the work for both shapes of the eight-entry tally. A chain needs a step of its own. Chapter two's chain combines the previous result with one new entry, but a join cannot do that directly, because the new entry would first need its own entry node.

A chain step instead reads the entry as a witness, and we call it append. Its left input is a header with a start, an end, and a sum, and its right input is the unit header. It checks that the list holds the witness value at the end position. Its output keeps the start, adds one to the end, and adds the value to the sum. For example, append takes the range zero to four with sum twenty-five, checks that position four holds six, and outputs the range zero to five with sum thirty-one.

A chain then starts with one entry node, whose two inputs are both unit headers, and continues with one append per remaining entry, each with a unit header on its right. So a chain is a lopsided tree. The balanced tree uses one entry leaf per entry and the join nodes above them. Every node verifies two proofs, and each unit input contributes a bootstrap proof to that count.

For the eight-entry tally, the balanced tree has fifteen nodes, eight entries and seven joins. Its circuits verify thirty proofs, sixteen of them bootstrap proofs behind unit inputs. The chain has eight nodes, one entry and seven appends, and its circuits verify sixteen proofs, nine of them bootstrap proofs. Both shapes end with one proof for the final verifier.

The chain uses fewer nodes, while the balanced tree lets separate parties work at the same time. The same verifier serves both, because it accepts a child proof from entry, join, or append alike.

Tracking initial states.

The third design works for a chain because a chain has one start. A tree has one start for each leaf, and a verifier may care which leaves went into the result, yet the header must stay a fixed size. The book's answer is to keep a Merkle root of the initial states. Each leaf's header holds a hash of its start value, and each node's circuit hashes its children's roots to get its own. The cost is one hash per node, and the root stays one field element however many leaves it covers.

We can run this on the first four values of chapter two's list, four, eleven, three, and seven, using the toy sponge as the hash. An entry leaf has no input state, so we treat the entry it checks as its start value. A leaf hashes its start value together with a fixed tag, eighteen. The tag separates the hashes at leaves from the hashes at joins, which take two roots instead.

The four leaves give the roots twenty, thirty-nine, forty-one, and fourteen. Hashing them in pairs gives eight and thirty-two, and hashing those gives the top root, ten, which the final header would carry.

A verifier who knows the four start values can recompute the root and compare it with the final header, and this check is sensitive to the start values. Replacing the start value of the third leaf with each of the other forty-two values changes the root in thirty-nine cases. The remaining three cases are collisions, which a hash with only forty-three outputs cannot avoid, and a two hundred fifty-five bit output makes finding one infeasible.

Here the Ragu Book and the code differ. The book says the protocol maintains a root of initial states on each side of the cycle and updates it in each step. In the code at the commit this site pins, we found no such value. The instance shared by the internal circuits holds commitments, challenges, and two values from the batching and revdot checks, and the phrase appears nowhere else in the crates.

Read this way, the examples we already have fit the same pattern. Chapter two's tally records a range of positions, and its entry step checks each value against the list. The book's getting-started guide builds a Merkle tree in application headers and steps, which is the calculation we just did, carried out by an application. In each case the application chooses what to record and what the verifier compares.

Why cheating fails.

The argument from chapter two walks down the tree from a false header to a step whose check rejects it. Recursion needs a slightly stronger property than that walk first suggests, because of what the relation claims about the old proof. Accepted proofs of false statements exist as mathematical objects, and they are only hard to find. On the toy curve, chapter nine found one by using a relation among the generators. So the claim that some accepted proof exists says little on its own, since it is usually true.

What the walk needs is the claim that the prover knows such a proof. The K in NARK stands for this, and the chapter on statements and challenges gave the informal idea with Schnorr's protocol. A prover able to produce an accepted proof must be able to produce a witness for it. Here the witness contains the previous proof, so the same reasoning applies again at the level below, until the walk reaches the base case. The walk therefore ends at a step whose own check rejects the false header, unless the prover has broken one of four assumptions.

The first assumption is that nobody knows a relation among the generators, which is the discrete log relation assumption. It rules out the accepted proof of a false statement that chapter nine built from a relation among the toy curve's generators.

The second is that the transcript hash behaves as a random oracle, and Ragu makes this assumption about Poseidon. It rules out a prover who learns the challenges before committing and solves for a false trace that passes at them, as in chapter nine. The assumption has to stretch further here than in a standalone proof, because the circuit contains the hash that the verifier replays, while an actual random oracle could not be written as gates.

The third is that the circuit wires its checks together as this chapter required. Because the state is one wire, a proof about one state cannot be paired with the step applied to another. Because each challenge comes from the hash, a false trace cannot pass by choosing y equal to zero. And because the circuit computes the wiring value and the combined generator itself, or carries each forward as a claim checked later, a proof for a different circuit fails, and so does a solved generator.

The fourth is that the steps are correct, the base case is confined, and the verifier compares the final header with what it wants to know. This rules out a flag the prover raises at will, and a chain that starts from a value nobody wanted.

The third and fourth assumptions point to checks that the level-one circuit or the final verifier performs, and removing any one of those checks reopens the attack that goes with it. The first two are different, because no check can enforce them. They hold for a real curve and a real hash, and the toy curve shows what happens when the first one fails. The walk down the tree needs all of them at once.

This is an informal argument. Formal treatments have to account for repeating the extraction over the depth of the recursion and for the hash appearing in the circuit, and this site leaves those out.

The operation that builds these proofs is called fuse, and its signature names the pieces of the two-input relation. It takes a step and that step's witness, along with a left proof and a right proof, each carrying its header data. It returns the new header with its new proof.

A Ragu proof is really a set of claims about several circuits, and the revdot folding chapter explains how they combine. The book's accumulation chapter describes one application circuit and one fuse circuit per curve, and we read that fuse circuit as a summary of those internal circuits. The chapter on recursion in Ragu names the internal circuits and shows where each check of this chapter takes place, such as the public input gate and the transcript replay.

Recursion is often used to hide data as well, but that benefit is not available yet. As chapter two recorded, a Ragu proof still exposes its children's data.

Where this chapter leaves us.

Incrementally verifiable computation keeps one proof, whose relation checks the step and also a verifier run on the previous proof, written as gates inside the circuit. A shared wire ties the inner proof's public input to the step's state.

Chapter nine's verifier has two linear costs, the wiring value and the combined generator, and at Ragu's size the second cannot fit. Neither can be dropped, because a prover would then switch circuits or pass with a false value. With a linear verifier the circuit grows at every level, and with a sublinear one it settles at a fixed size.

Trees use two inputs and two verifiers, and the verifier must accept a child proof from any step at any depth. The base case has to be confined so that no prover can skip a verifier at will. Ragu confines it to one internal step and leaves initial states to the application, although the book describes a tracked root.

So the later chapters have to deliver a verifier with five properties. First, the combined generator and the wiring value leave the circuit, each postponed as a claim that travels with the proof, and someone still computes it in the end. Second, the part that stays in the circuit grows more slowly than the circuit, so the circuit settles at a fixed size that fits Ragu's limit of two to the eleven gates.

Third, one verifier accepts child proofs from every step circuit of the application. Fourth, the base case stays confined, so that no application step can skip a child's check. Fifth, headers stay a fixed size, and the application records whatever a verifier needs to compare at the end.

The next chapter, Accumulation, starts with the first requirement. It postpones the computation of the combined generator by turning it into a claim that travels with the proof, and it shows how two such claims merge into one.
