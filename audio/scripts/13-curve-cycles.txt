Chapter thirteen. Curve Cycles.

The recursive proofs chapter set a problem aside. A circuit that verifies a proof has to add and scale commitments, and the coordinates of those commitments live in a different field from the circuit's wires. The accumulation chapters ignored this too, and now we deal with it.

We first measure what it costs to compute in the wrong field, and the cost rules that route out. We then introduce a second curve whose coordinates match the first curve's scalars, and we divide each fold between two circuits so that each does only native arithmetic. This creates two new problems, because the circuits have to see the same data and use the same challenges. The chapters on staging and endoscalars give the full mechanisms for these, so here we explain why each is needed and show the idea on the toy cycle.

The split fold in the middle of this chapter is reconstructed, and the authors have not confirmed it. It is this site's own small assembly of what the Ragu Book describes, checked by a script. Its circuits are short lists of gates that the script runs and does not prove, because the toy curves have too few points to serve as generators for a full circuit.

We follow the book and say primary for the circuit over the first field and secondary for the circuit over the second. The code's names are native and nested. This site says fuse for the operation on a node, as chapter two does.

Two fields in one commitment.

Recall the fold from chapter eight, which a recursive step has to check. One line computes a new commitment as a weighted sum of commitments, with powers of beta as the weights. The other line computes a new value as a weighted sum of values, with the same weights.

These look alike, but they ask for different arithmetic. The polynomials have coefficients modulo forty-three, the scalar field of the toy curve. So the value line is a computation modulo forty-three, and a circuit over that field does it with one gate per multiplication. A circuit has to be over this field because its own trace is a polynomial committed on the toy curve.

The commitment line adds and scales points, and the addition formula works on coordinates, which are modulo thirty-one. If we add two commitments from chapter eight modulo thirty-one, the slope is twenty-one and the sum is a point on the curve. Run the same formulas on wires that reduce modulo forty-three, and the slope becomes twenty-nine, and the result lies on neither curve of the toy cycle.

The formula fails because each multiplication needs a remainder modulo thirty-one, and the wires produce a remainder modulo forty-three. The first gate multiplies twenty-one by ten. As integers that is two hundred ten, which leaves remainder twenty-four modulo thirty-one. A wire modulo forty-three holds thirty-eight, and that says nothing about the remainder modulo thirty-one. On real curves the same mismatch holds.

Emulating the other field.

A circuit can still check a multiplication modulo thirty-one if it treats the numbers as integers. The prover supplies a remainder and a multiple, and the circuit checks that the product equals the remainder plus thirty-one times the multiple. If this holds over the integers and the remainder is small, the remainder is correct.

The difficulty is that a gate only checks equations modulo forty-three, so a false witness can match. The honest witness for two hundred ten is remainder twenty-four and multiple six. But of the nine hundred ninety-two small pairs, twenty-three satisfy the gate, and twenty-two of those have the wrong remainder. One is remainder zero and multiple four, because one hundred twenty-four equals two hundred ten modulo forty-three.

The way out is to make every equation so small that it cannot wrap around, because an equation between small numbers modulo forty-three is also an equation between integers. So we split the numbers into pieces called limbs. On the toy field each limb is one bit. Expanding the equation and collecting by powers of two gives one column per power, as in long multiplication. Each column can carry into the next, so the circuit takes a witness carry for each column and checks the columns one at a time, with the last carry equal to zero.

Our multiplication of twenty-one by ten passes this check. The gates are twenty-five for the bit products, ten to show that the bits of the remainder and multiple are bits, and eighteen for the carries, which makes fifty-three gates for one multiplication. The site ran the check on every combination of inputs and witnesses, and it accepted only the true remainder for each input pair.

So one native multiplication has become dozens of gates, and the cost multiplies through every point operation. A six-bit scalar multiplication on the toy curve costs one thousand nine hundred eleven gates, where the same operation on the partner curve costs fifty-one. That leaves out the extra work to reduce sums and differences back into range.

A real field has room for larger limbs. The next estimate is this site's own plain design and not a gadget in Ragu, and better gadgets exist that cut these numbers by some factor. With four limbs of sixty-four bits, one multiplication costs about nine hundred ninety-five gates. A point addition needs three of these, or about two thousand nine hundred eighty-five gates, which already exceeds the two thousand forty-eight gates Ragu allows in one circuit. A full scalar multiplication costs about one million seven hundred sixty-nine thousand one hundred ten gates, where the native version costs two thousand five hundred forty-one.

The book calls non-native arithmetic prohibitively expensive, and these counts show why. The accumulator's whole purpose was to keep the verifier small, and this one requirement would undo it. So we need to avoid computing in the wrong field at all.

A curve for each field.

The recursive proofs chapter already used the way out when it counted gates on the partner curve, since that curve's coordinates are modulo forty-three. Adding two points there takes three gates, because every gate works in the circuit's own field.

We cannot move everything to the partner curve, though. It has thirty-one points, so its scalars are modulo thirty-one, and it cannot hold the polynomials of a circuit modulo forty-three. Those polynomials need the first curve, and the first curve's points need wires modulo thirty-one. A circuit modulo thirty-one would then have its own polynomials, which the partner curve can commit to, and those points need wires modulo forty-three.

So the two curves swap roles. The first curve has coordinates modulo thirty-one and scalars modulo forty-three, and the partner curve has the reverse. We call this a two-cycle of curves. It is a pair of prime fields, where each field carries a curve whose point count is the size of the other field. Each circuit can do the point arithmetic for the polynomials of the other. Ben-Sasson, Chiesa, Tromer and Virza introduced such cycles for recursive proofs.

A single curve whose point count equals its field size would avoid the need for a partner. Such a curve is called anomalous, though, and discrete log on it is easy by a known attack due to Smart, Semaev, and Satoh and Araki. So two curves are the smallest arrangement that works.

The Hasse bound limits how far a curve's point count can stray from its field size, to about twice the square root of that size, so the two primes of a cycle must be close together. Both toy curves fit within this bound. Such pairs are also uncommon. Among two hundred ten pairs of small primes the site examined, ten form a cycle, and thirty-one with forty-three is one of them.

The Pasta cycle.

Ragu uses the Pasta curves, which are already deployed in Orchard and in hardware wallets. Pallas and Vesta have the same equation, y squared equals x cubed plus five, over two different primes, with point counts swapped. Both primes have two hundred fifty-five bits, and their difference has only eighty-seven bits. The site tested the cycle property directly, by multiplying a point on each curve by the prime of the other curve's field and reaching the identity.

The main circuit's field is the first prime, and it commits its polynomials on Vesta, whose scalars are that prime. In the toy cycle, the same roles belong to the field modulo forty-three and the first curve. The other circuit uses the second prime and Pallas, or the field modulo thirty-one and the partner curve. The book numbers these one and two and calls the curves host and nested, and the code calls them circuit field, host curve, scalar field and nested curve. In the toy cycle the main circuit has the larger field, while in Pasta it has the smaller one, and this does not affect the construction.

The two fields are close in size, but their elements are not interchangeable. In the toy cycle, twelve of the forty-three values have no equal in the smaller field. In Pasta the fraction is near two to the minus one hundred sixty-seven, so a random value almost always fits. A protocol still cannot rely on that for values a prover can choose, and this returns when challenges cross between circuits.

Recursion on a cycle.

With two curves, every recursive step uses two kinds of circuit, and the book calls this a ping-pong pattern. Each circuit has its own polynomials, committed on its own curve, so each has its own accumulator. Where the last chapter had one accumulator, we now have a pair.

The rule for dividing the work follows from the arithmetic. Field elements are native to the circuit that made the polynomial, and points are native to the other circuit. So the primary circuit folds the first accumulator's value and the second accumulator's point, and the secondary circuit does the reverse. Each circuit updates half of each accumulator.

Timing matters as well. A circuit's commitments do not exist until its trace is complete, so a step cannot fold its own trace. The book's rule is that the instances produced in one step are folded only in the next. The primary circuit's trace becomes a commitment on the first curve, whose coordinates the secondary circuit can read, so the secondary circuit folds that point one step later, and the same holds in the other direction. Trees carry over, with each node folding two children's pairs of accumulators into one new pair.

A fold split across two circuits.

Here is one fold on the toy cycle, with two claims on each curve. The first side reuses chapter eight's polynomials, and the second uses two new polynomials modulo thirty-one. The prover runs chapter eight's batching on each side, with the same hand-picked challenges on both, which are eleven, thirteen and fifteen, each below sixteen for a reason we'll come to.

Now we assign each output to the circuit where it is native. For a value, the circuit does field arithmetic with inverses and powers of the challenges. For a point, it runs a double-and-add on the four-bit beta. The circuit modulo forty-three computes its value, fourteen, in eight gates, and the partner curve's point in sixty-eight. The circuit modulo thirty-one computes its value, nine, in eight gates, and the first curve's point in sixty-eight. A decider who commits to the two combined polynomials and evaluates them gets all four results.

The saving is what this arrangement is for. The first curve's point took sixty-eight gates on native wires. Emulated in the circuit modulo forty-three, the same steps would have cost at least two thousand six hundred twelve gates.

This result hides two assumptions. Nothing yet connects what the two circuits read, so a prover could give each a different set of inputs. The circuits also used the same challenges only because we typed the same numbers. The book names these problems input consistency and challenge consistency.

Committing to coordinates.

The primary circuit derives challenges by hashing the transcript, and the transcript has to contain the commitments the challenges test. For a commitment on the first curve, that means absorbing coordinates modulo thirty-one into a sponge modulo forty-three, which a circuit cannot do natively. The book also rules out hashing all the inputs on both sides and comparing digests, since that needs non-native arithmetic or bit decompositions of every value.

The cycle gives a neater route. The coordinates of a point on the first curve are numbers modulo thirty-one, which is exactly the partner curve's scalar field. So we place the coordinates in a list and make an ordinary Pedersen commitment on the partner curve. The result is one point with coordinates modulo forty-three, which the primary circuit can hold as wires and absorb. The book calls this a nested commitment.

One small point can therefore stand for any number of foreign points, and it binds them under the usual assumption, now for the second curve's generators. On the toy curve, changing one of our coordinates to each of its thirty other values gives thirty different points. Collisions must exist on the toy cycle, because there are far more lists than the thirty-one points, and the site found one by solving a discrete log.

Both circuits need to read all of the step's data. The book's encoding for the primary circuit has two layers. The values and points it can read go into a part of its own trace, which gives a commitment on the first curve. That commitment, the first curve's points and the values modulo thirty-one then form one list behind a nested commitment. The secondary circuit's encoding mirrors this.

The prover computes a nested commitment outside the circuit and supplies it as a witness. So at this stage the primary circuit knows that it hashed some point, and not yet that the point holds the coordinates the secondary circuit used.

Deferred operations.

Suppose the primary circuit's fold needs a scalar times a point on the first curve. It holds only the nested commitment, which binds the data without carrying over any operation on it. The secondary circuit has the coordinates as wires, so the work moves there, and we call this a deferred operation.

The wires the secondary circuit uses have to be the same values the nested commitment holds. The tool for this is a staged trace, which the staging chapter covers in full. The prover splits the secondary circuit's trace into a stage, holding the coordinates in reserved slots, and a later part holding everything else. It commits to each separately, and the stage's commitment is the nested commitment the transcript absorbed. The book then requires two checks. The commitments must add up to the commitment to the whole trace, and the parts must not overlap.

The sum check is native to the primary circuit, and on the toy cycle it takes three gates. It stops a prover who uses other coordinates. The site tried each of thirty other values for one coordinate while leaving the nested commitment alone, and all thirty fail.

A commitment sum cannot enforce the second check. Let the prover put fifteen in a slot of the later part reserved for the stage. The stage still says twenty-seven, but the sum holds eleven, so the circuit runs on a different point. That is a genuine curve point, because five cubed is one modulo thirty-one and the curve's equation only sees the cube of x. The sum check still passes, for all thirty nonzero values, because a commitment to a sum equals the sum of the commitments however the entries overlap.

So a separate check has to show that the later part is zero in the stage's slots. In our toy form, the verifier picks a random challenge and asks for a combination of the reserved slots, weighted by its powers, to be zero. Ragu expresses such a claim as a revdot product with a stage mask. A prover using two reserved slots can cancel at one challenge, and over all nine hundred pairs of entries, no pair passes at more than one value.

So each step leaves two kinds of work for the next, checking that partial traces are well formed and that deferred group operations were computed correctly. Both are claims about a circuit that the next step folds like any other, which is why deferring them does not make them pile up.

Sharing one set of challenges.

The two circuits must use the same challenges, because the value and the point only describe one polynomial if both use one beta. Running a second transcript would give different values. The book's answer is called transcript bridging, where only the primary circuit hashes and its challenges serve both circuits. That is safe only together with input consistency, because the secondary circuit's inputs reach the transcript only through nested commitments, and they have to be fixed before the challenges are.

The difficulty is that a challenge is a number modulo forty-three, and the secondary circuit has wires modulo thirty-one. Suppose the challenge is thirty-eight. A wire modulo thirty-one holds seven, and seven times a point is not thirty-eight times that point.

Two things fix this. The challenge is kept short enough that the same integer fits in both fields. Our run uses values below sixteen, and the code uses one hundred forty-three bit values called endoscalars. The circuit also computes with the challenge through its bits, using the curve's endomorphism, as the endoscalars chapter explains. In the code the short value maps to a scalar that is not the plain integer, and our toy run does not reproduce that map.

The secondary circuit still has to receive the right short value, and the primary circuit can check this from outside. The prover puts the values in a stage with no blinding, so the stage's commitment is a fixed combination of generators of the partner curve, and those points are native to the primary circuit. The primary circuit knows the challenges as wires, so it computes the same combination itself, in ninety-nine gates. Any of the thirty other values of beta makes the comparison fail.

A short challenge gives a prover a better chance of a lucky value. A cheater who aims its errors at a short value moves its chance from one in forty-three to one in sixteen. On a real curve that chance is the degree of the error polynomial divided by two to the one hundred forty-three, which remains negligible. The book notes that endoscalars are only for public challenges, since this shorter range would be unsafe for a secret.

Two steps of ping-pong.

We can now run the pattern for two steps. In the second step, each accumulator meets a new claim from a circuit's trace in the first step, using four wires as a stand-in polynomial because the toy curves cannot hold a full trace. Each commitment crosses to the other circuit for its point arithmetic. Each circuit takes seventy-six gates in both steps, which is the fixed circuit recursion requires.

At the end, one decider handles both curves, committing to the two final polynomials and evaluating them to get six and thirteen, as the accumulators say. If the first curve's accumulator had claimed fifteen at the start of the second step, the circuit would have derived thirty-nine, and the decider would have rejected, since the polynomial still evaluates to six.

Why cheating fails.

The division of the fold leaves new places to cheat. In each case a check the honest prover passes without effort catches the cheater, though the catch may come one step later or at the decider.

A prover cannot report a wrong result from a circuit, because the gates determine it. The site tried one thousand two hundred seventy-one combinations of wrong output and slope witness for one addition, and none satisfied the gates.

A prover cannot give the circuits different data, because the sum check and the overlap check catch it. Changing the nested commitment to match would change the transcript and every later challenge, so the prover has to commit to the secondary circuit's inputs before it learns the challenges.

A prover cannot give the circuits different challenges, because the primary circuit recomputes the challenge stage's commitment from its own transcript.

A prover might hope to skip the deferred work, because each of these checks happens in the next step, and the last step has no next step. That is why the decider has to examine both accumulators and the root proof's stages. If a false point or a false stage survives to the end, one of the claims on the two curves is false, and the decider rejects it. A verifier that checks only one curve leaves half of every fold unconfirmed, since that half happened on the other side.

The argument is informal, and it rests on these assumptions. The discrete log relation assumption has to hold for the generators of both curves, which is one more reason an anomalous curve cannot serve. The transcript hash has to behave as a random oracle. Challenges have to come after the data they test, and short challenges have to be long enough for the degree of the claims. The decider has to run on both sides.

Cycles in Ragu.

The book describes the design as inspired by CycleFold. The site could read only CycleFold's abstract, which says that the second curve merely represents a single scalar multiplication in a tiny circuit. Ragu's application also lives on one side only, but it has several internal circuits on the other side, and this comparison is the site's own.

The following is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. Polynomials on the nested side that hold native commitments are called bridge stages. The children's Vesta commitments go in a stage over the second prime, committed on Pallas, and fuse absorbs that point and squeezes the first challenge. The transcript absorbs only such bridge commitments, and Pallas commitments ride inside them, so they too are fixed before the challenges that test them.

Each side has a walk that performs the fold's point sum for the other side. A walk does not fit in one circuit of at most two to the eleven gates, so the native one spreads across twenty-three circuits, each doing four endoscalings. The nested side squeezes nothing and reads each challenge as a lift of a native one. Its challenge stage has no blinding, and the bind challenges circuits recompute its commitment on Pallas. The uncompressed verifier checks both sides and also rederives the root's challenge stage, because the root has no parent to bind it.

The book and the code differ in several places. The book has an application circuit on each side, while in the code every nested circuit is internal and the application lives only on the native side. This site follows the code, which is also the closer match to the book's CycleFold description.

The book says the partial trace commitments add up to the trace commitment, checked in-circuit. The site found no such addition in the code. A proof keeps a commitment for each stage and none for the whole trace, and a circuit's claim uses the sum of its stage polynomials. So the site reads the code as establishing the sum through polynomial claims, and this reading is the site's own. Our example teaches the book's form because it shows the purpose of the check on small numbers, and the site otherwise follows the code. The book's other description of which circuit runs the checks also differs. It places both checks in the next step's secondary circuit, while the first passage places the sum in the primary circuit.

The book's transcript bridging section makes the next challenge an endoscalar. In the code's native side, only beta is extracted to an endoscalar, because it is the one that scales points, and the nested side lifts every challenge from its low bits. This site follows the code, and our toy run uses short values on both sides to keep one set of numbers. Earlier chapters recorded related differences, in the base case, the root of initial states, and the book's single fuse circuit per curve, which corresponds in the code to many internal circuits.

All of this applies to the Ragu revision this site pins. Tachyon, which has not activated on mainnet and whose design is still changing, depends on this design through a different Ragu revision.

Where this leaves us.

A circuit over a commitment's scalar field can fold values directly but cannot fold points, and emulating the other field in our plain design makes one point addition exceed a Ragu circuit. A two-cycle of curves gives each field a curve whose points are native to it, and Pallas and Vesta form such a cycle. Each step has two circuits, each folding its own accumulator's values and the other's points from the previous step. Nested commitments place foreign data behind one absorbable point, and staged traces tie the other circuit's wires to it. Only the primary circuit hashes, and challenges cross in a short form. Every check depends on the next step, and the decider finishes both curves' claims at the end.

So far our accumulators have folded evaluation claims, but a proof also holds the revdot claims of chapter five. The next chapter folds those, and the split of this chapter applies to them too.
