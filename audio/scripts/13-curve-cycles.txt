Chapter thirteen. Curve Cycles.

The recursive proofs chapter set a problem aside. A circuit that verifies a proof has to add and scale commitments, and the coordinates of those commitments live in a different field from the circuit's wires. The accumulation chapter ignored the same problem, and the split accumulation chapter worked on one curve as if it did not exist. This chapter deals with it.

We first measure what it costs to compute in the wrong field, and the cost turns out to rule that route out. We then introduce a second curve whose coordinates match the first curve's scalars, and we divide each fold between two circuits so that each one does only native arithmetic. This division creates two problems, because the two circuits have to see the same data, and they have to use the same challenges. The chapters on staging and endoscalars later give the full mechanisms for these, so here we only explain why each is needed and show the idea on the toy cycle.

The split fold in the middle of this chapter is reconstructed from the Ragu Book and the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. It is this site's own small assembly of what the book describes, checked by the script behind the page. Its circuits are short lists of gates that the script runs and does not prove, because the toy curves have too few points to serve as generators for a full circuit.

For the general construction we follow the book and say primary for the circuit over the first field and secondary for the circuit over the second. In the toy cycle the primary circuit has wires modulo forty-three, and the secondary circuit has wires modulo thirty-one. The code's names are native and nested, and we come to them at the end. As in chapter two, we say fuse for the operation on a node.

Two fields in one commitment.

Recall the fold from chapter eight, which a recursive step has to check. It has two lines. One computes a new commitment as a weighted sum of the old commitments, with powers of beta as the weights. The other computes a new value as a weighted sum of the old values, with the same weights.

These two lines look alike, but they ask for different arithmetic. The polynomials have coefficients modulo forty-three, which is the scalar field of the toy curve. So the value line is a computation modulo forty-three, and a circuit over that field can do it with one gate per multiplication. A circuit has to be over this field, because its own trace is a polynomial that gets committed on the toy curve, and commitments there take scalars modulo forty-three.

The commitment line adds and scales points, and the addition formula from the recursive proofs chapter works on coordinates, which lie modulo thirty-one. Take two commitments from chapter eight. Modulo thirty-one the slope is twenty-one, and the sum is a point on the curve. Run the same three formulas on wires that reduce modulo forty-three, and the slope becomes twenty-nine, and the result lies on neither curve of the toy cycle.

The formula fails because each of its multiplications needs a remainder modulo thirty-one, and the wires produce a remainder modulo forty-three. The first gate of that addition multiplies the slope, twenty-one, by the difference of the x-coordinates, ten. As integers that is two hundred ten, which leaves remainder twenty-four modulo thirty-one. A wire modulo forty-three holds thirty-eight instead, and the remainder modulo forty-three says nothing about the remainder modulo thirty-one.

So a circuit over the scalar field handles the value directly but has no direct way to handle the point. On real curves the same mismatch holds, and the two fields have no useful relation between their remainders.

Emulating the other field.

A circuit can still check a multiplication modulo thirty-one if it treats the numbers as integers. The prover supplies a remainder and a multiple, and the circuit checks that the product equals the remainder plus thirty-one times the multiple. If this holds over the integers and the remainder is small, then the remainder is the product modulo thirty-one.

The difficulty is that a gate can only check equations modulo forty-three, and both sides are often larger than forty-three, so a false witness can match. The honest witness for two hundred ten is remainder twenty-four and multiple six. But among the nine hundred ninety-two small pairs of remainder and multiple, twenty-three satisfy the gate, and twenty-two of those have the wrong remainder. One is remainder zero and multiple four, because one hundred twenty-four equals two hundred ten modulo forty-three.

The way out is to make every equation the circuit checks so small that it cannot wrap around. An equation between small numbers modulo forty-three is also an equation between integers. So we split each number into pieces called limbs, and on the toy field each limb is one bit. We also write thirty-one times the multiple as thirty-two times the multiple, minus the multiple. In binary, thirty-one is five ones, so multiplying by it would put five shifted copies of the multiple into the columns, while the rewritten form puts in only two.

Expanding the equation and collecting by powers of two then gives one column per power, as in long multiplication by hand. Each column holds the bit products that land there, minus the remainder's bit, plus and minus the multiple's two shifted bits. The whole sum has to be zero, but each column need not be, because a column can carry into the next one, as in ordinary addition. So the circuit takes a witness carry for each column, and checks that each column plus the carry coming in equals twice the carry going out. The last carry must be zero.

Our multiplication of twenty-one by ten passes this check, column by column, with the last carry zero. The gates are twenty-five for the bit products, ten to show that the bits of the remainder and the multiple really are bits, and eighteen for the nine carries at two bits each. Two bits suffice, because an honest carry always lies between zero and three. That makes fifty-three gates for one multiplication modulo thirty-one.

The site ran this check on every pair of inputs against every pair of remainder and multiple. It accepted nine hundred sixty-one combinations, one for each pair of inputs, and each one had the true remainder. This count assumes the inputs arrive already split into bits. In a chain of multiplications that is mostly true, because each input is the remainder of an earlier multiplication, whose bits the circuit already checked. Inputs from outside the chain would need their own bit checks.

One native multiplication has become dozens of gates, and the cost multiplies through every point operation, since the formulas are built from these multiplications. A six-bit scalar multiplication on the toy curve does five doublings and five additions. A doubling takes four multiplications and an addition takes three, which makes thirty-five multiplications, or one thousand eight hundred fifty-five gates. Selecting between two points by each scalar bit adds fifty gates, and checking the six scalar bits adds six more.

The total is one thousand nine hundred eleven gates, where the same operation on the partner curve costs fifty-one gates in the recursive proofs chapter. As in that chapter, those fifty-one gates keep the six-bit width of the larger field as a stand-in, since the partner curve's own scalars have only five bits. Our count also leaves out the extra work to reduce the sums and differences in each formula back into range.

A real field has room for larger limbs, which lowers the count per bit. The next estimate is this site's own plain design and not a gadget that Ragu contains, and better gadgets exist that cut these numbers by some factor. With four limbs of sixty-four bits, one multiplication costs about nine hundred ninety-five gates. A point addition needs three of these, or about two thousand nine hundred eighty-five gates, which already exceeds the two thousand forty-eight gates that Ragu allows in one circuit.

A full two hundred fifty-five bit scalar multiplication would need one thousand seven hundred seventy-eight multiplications, or about one million seven hundred sixty-nine thousand one hundred ten gates, where the native version costs two thousand five hundred forty-one. The verifier of chapter nine has thirty-two such multiplications, apart from its large multi-scalar multiplication, which comes to about fifty-six million six hundred eleven thousand five hundred twenty gates.

The book calls non-native arithmetic prohibitively expensive, and these counts show why. The accumulator's whole purpose was to keep the verifier small, and this one requirement would undo it. We need a way to avoid computing in the wrong field at all.

A curve for each field.

The recursive proofs chapter already used the way out, when it counted gates on the partner curve, since that curve's coordinates lie modulo forty-three. Adding two points there computes the slope and sum modulo forty-three, so every gate works in the circuit's own field. The addition takes three gates, and the result lies on the partner curve.

It may look as if we could move everything to the partner curve, but that curve has thirty-one points, so its scalars lie modulo thirty-one. A commitment there holds a list of numbers modulo thirty-one, and it cannot hold the polynomials of a circuit over forty-three. Those polynomials need the first curve, and the first curve's points need wires modulo thirty-one. A circuit modulo thirty-one would then have its own polynomials, which the partner curve can commit to, and those points need wires modulo forty-three.

So the two curves swap roles. The first curve has coordinates modulo thirty-one and scalars modulo forty-three, and the partner curve has the reverse. We call this a two-cycle of curves. It is a pair of prime fields, with a curve over the first field whose number of points is the second prime, and a curve over the second field whose number of points is the first prime. Each circuit can then do the point arithmetic for the polynomials of the other. Ben-Sasson, Chiesa, Tromer and Virza introduced the use of such cycles for recursive proofs.

A single curve whose number of points equals the size of its field would avoid the need for a partner. Such a curve is called anomalous, though, and discrete log on anomalous curves is easy by a known attack due to Smart, Semaev, and Satoh and Araki. So two curves are the smallest arrangement that works.

The Hasse bound says that a curve's number of points differs from its field size plus one by at most twice the square root of the field size. So the two primes of a cycle must be close together. For the first toy curve the gap is eleven, against a bound of about eleven point one. For the partner curve the gap is thirteen, against about thirteen point one. Both fit.

Such pairs are uncommon. The site searched only curves with no x term, of the form y squared equals x cubed plus a constant, because Pasta has that form and the endoscalars chapter relies on it. It also kept only primes that leave remainder one when divided by three. For the other primes, every element has exactly one cube root, so every such curve has exactly one more point than the prime, which is never a prime itself.

That leaves twenty-one primes below two hundred. Among the two hundred ten pairs of these primes, ten form a cycle, and thirty-one with forty-three is one of them. The same count finds five primes with an anomalous curve, starting with seven.

The Pasta cycle.

Ragu uses the Pasta curves, which chapter one explained are already deployed in Orchard and in hardware wallets. Pallas and Vesta have the same equation, y squared equals x cubed plus five. Pallas lies over a prime we'll call p and has q points, and Vesta lies over q and has p points. Both primes have two hundred fifty-five bits, and their difference has only eighty-seven bits, which is below the size the Hasse bound allows.

The site tested the cycle property directly. A point on the curve over p reaches the identity when multiplied by q, and a point on the curve over q reaches the identity when multiplied by p. Only one multiple of each prime lies within the Hasse range, so the two point counts must be q and p.

The field of the primary circuit is p, and the code commits that circuit's polynomials on Vesta, whose scalars lie modulo p and whose coordinates lie modulo q. In the toy cycle, the same roles belong to the field modulo forty-three and the first curve. The secondary circuit uses q and Pallas, or the field modulo thirty-one and the partner curve. The book numbers these one and two, and calls the curves host and nested. The code calls them circuit field, host curve, scalar field and nested curve.

The toy cycle plays the same roles with one difference in size. In the toy cycle the primary circuit has the larger field, while in Pasta it has the smaller one. Most of the construction works the same way for either order, but the order matters when challenges cross from one circuit to the other, and we will see how.

Recursion on a cycle.

With two curves there are two kinds of circuit, and every recursive step now uses both. The book calls this a ping-pong pattern. Each circuit has its own polynomials, committed on its own curve, so each has its own accumulator. Where the split accumulation chapter had one accumulator, we now have a pair.

Each accumulator contains both field elements and points, and the rule for dividing the work follows from the arithmetic we just saw. Field elements are native to the circuit that made the polynomial, and points are native to the other circuit. The book states this split for the whole accumulator, including the revdot claims from chapter nine, which the next chapter folds.

For the batched evaluation claim, the split goes like this. The first accumulator holds polynomials modulo forty-three, or p, committed on the first curve, or Vesta. The primary circuit folds its value, and the secondary circuit folds its commitment. The second accumulator is the mirror image, so the secondary circuit folds its value and the primary circuit folds its commitment. Each circuit updates half of each accumulator.

Timing matters as well. A circuit's commitments do not exist until its trace is complete, so a step cannot fold its own trace. The book's rule is that the instances produced in one step are folded only in the next. The trace of the primary circuit becomes a commitment on the first curve, whose coordinates the secondary circuit can read, so the secondary circuit folds that point one step later. The same holds in the other direction, so each circuit's trace commitment crosses to the other circuit between steps.

Everything from the recursive proofs chapter about trees carries over. A node holds two children, each with a pair of accumulators, and it folds both into one new pair.

A fold split across two circuits.

Here is one fold on the toy cycle, with two claims on each curve. The first side reuses chapter eight's polynomials, and the second side uses two new polynomials modulo thirty-one, committed with four generators of the partner curve.

The prover runs chapter eight's batching on each side, with the same three challenges on both. They are eleven for alpha, thirteen for the evaluation point, and fifteen for beta. Each was picked by hand and each lies below sixteen, for a reason we will come to. On each side the prover forms its quotient combination, commits to it, and evaluates the original polynomials at thirteen.

Now we assign each output to the circuit where it is native. For a value, the circuit inverts the gap between the evaluation point and each claim's point, multiplies by the gap between the evaluated value and the claimed value, and accumulates with powers of alpha and then of beta. For a point, it runs the same weighted sum in Horner form, using the plain double-and-add from the recursive proofs chapter on the four-bit beta.

The results are as follows. The primary circuit computes its own side's value, fourteen, in eight gates, and the partner curve's point in sixty-eight gates. The secondary circuit computes its own side's value, nine, in eight gates, and the first curve's point in sixty-eight gates.

Let's follow the first side step by step. The first claim gives a quotient value of nine, and the second gives twenty, with every difference reduced modulo forty-three. The circuit combines them with alpha, so eleven times nine plus twenty gives thirty-three, which matches the quotient polynomial evaluated at thirteen.

Then it runs Horner's rule in beta. Thirty-three times fifteen, plus the first evaluated value, thirty, gives nine. Nine times fifteen, plus the second evaluated value, eight, gives fourteen, which is the folded value. Each claim costs three gates, to check an inverse, multiply by it, and scale by alpha, and the two Horner steps cost one gate each, so the total is eight.

The point follows the same Horner pattern on the first curve, with commitments in place of values. Fifteen times the quotient commitment, plus the first commitment, happens to return the quotient commitment itself. That is a coincidence of the toy numbers, and it makes the second line repeat the first scalar multiplication. Each line is a four-bit double-and-add of thirty-one gates followed by one addition of three gates, so the two lines cost sixty-eight.

As a check, a decider commits to the two combined polynomials and evaluates them at thirteen. It gets the same two points and the same values, fourteen and nine, so all four results match.

Compare this with the cost of emulation. Every gate in this run worked on values in its own circuit's field, and the first curve's point took sixty-eight gates on native wires. Suppose instead the primary circuit had computed that point by the limb method. Each Horner line would need twenty-four emulated multiplications at fifty-three gates each, plus gates for selections and the bits of beta, which comes to one thousand three hundred six gates. The two lines would cost at least two thousand six hundred twelve gates.

The result hides two assumptions. The secondary circuit computed the point from the commitments on the first curve, and the primary circuit computed the value from claims about those same polynomials. But nothing yet connects what the two circuits read, so a prover could give each a different set of inputs. The two circuits also used the same challenges only because we typed the same numbers. The book names these problems input consistency and challenge consistency, and the next three sections address them.

Committing to coordinates.

We start with input consistency. We need an object that both circuits can refer to, and that pins down the data each one used.

The transcript is the natural place to start. The primary circuit derives challenges by hashing the transcript, as the recursive proofs chapter required, and the transcript has to contain the commitments that the challenges test. For a commitment on the first curve, that means absorbing coordinates modulo thirty-one into a sponge modulo forty-three. On the toy cycle this is possible, because every number below thirty-one fits in a wire modulo forty-three unchanged.

On Pasta the direction is reversed. The primary circuit hashes modulo p, the coordinates of a Vesta point lie modulo q, and q is larger than p, so a coordinate may not fit in one wire. Chapter twenty-one meets the same problem when it hashes a Vesta point and has to split it into limbs.

Fitting is not the whole problem, though. Even on the toy cycle, if the primary circuit copied the coordinates into its own wires and absorbed them, nothing would tie those wires to the wires of the secondary circuit. That is a separate circuit with its own trace, so the prover could give the two circuits different coordinates, which is the problem we started with.

A second idea is to hash the inputs in both circuits and compare the digests, but the book rules this out. On Pasta, each circuit would have to hash values from the other field, which needs non-native arithmetic or bit decompositions of every value. Absorbing many coordinates one by one would also cost a sponge input for each coordinate.

The cycle gives a neater route. The coordinates of a point on the first curve are numbers modulo thirty-one, which is exactly the partner curve's scalar field. So we can place the coordinates in a list and make an ordinary Pedersen commitment to it on the partner curve. The result is one point on the partner curve, with coordinates modulo forty-three. The book calls this a nested commitment.

Take the two commitments from chapter eight. Their four coordinates form a list, and each entry is a valid scalar on the partner curve. Committing with four generators of the partner curve gives the point twelve, twelve. Its coordinates lie modulo forty-three, so the primary circuit can hold them as wires and absorb them, and the toy sponge squeezes the output two.

In this way one small point can stand for any number of foreign points, and both circuits can refer to it. The commitment binds them under the same assumption as any Pedersen commitment, now for the generators of the second curve. Changing the first coordinate to each of its thirty other values gives thirty different points, and twenty-eight of them change the sponge's output as well.

Of the other two, one is the point at infinity, which has no coordinates to absorb. The other is a different point whose hash happens to equal two, because the toy sponge has only forty-three possible outputs. Neither is a weakness of the commitment, since all thirty points differ from the original. Collisions must exist, because a list of four entries has nine hundred twenty-three thousand five hundred twenty-one possible values and the curve has only thirty-one points. The site found one by solving a discrete log.

A whole step has more data than two points, and each piece is native to only one of the two circuits. Field values need the same treatment with the curves swapped. The claimed values modulo forty-three are scalars of the first curve, so an ordinary commitment there can hold them, and its coordinates lie modulo thirty-one, where the secondary circuit reads them as wires. We extend the name nested commitment to this case too, meaning any commitment on one curve whose coordinates the other circuit absorbs.

Sorting the data of our fold by the rule from the start of the chapter gives a simple pattern. Values modulo forty-three and points on the partner curve are wires for the primary circuit. Values modulo thirty-one and points on the first curve are wires for the secondary circuit. Each circuit reaches the other half through a nested commitment.

The book's encoding of a step's inputs gathers everything a circuit cannot read behind a single nested commitment for that circuit, and the staging chapter builds that encoding. Here we follow only the two points from chapter eight.

The prover computes a nested commitment outside the circuit and supplies it as a witness. So at this stage the primary circuit knows that it hashed some point. It does not yet know that the point contains the coordinates the secondary circuit used, and the next section adds that connection.

Deferred operations.

A nested commitment binds the data without carrying over any operation on it. The primary circuit holds only the nested commitment in place of the points of the first curve, so it cannot add or scale those points. The folded point needed exactly such operations, since computing it takes beta times a point and additions on the first curve. The secondary circuit has the coordinates as wires and can do this arithmetic, so the work moves there, and we call it a deferred operation.

For this to mean anything, the wires the secondary circuit uses have to be the same values the nested commitment holds. The tool for this is a staged trace, which the staging chapter covers in full. The prover splits the secondary circuit's trace polynomial into two parts. The stage holds the coordinates in slots reserved for it, and the later part holds everything else. The prover commits to each part separately, and arranges for the stage's commitment to be exactly the nested commitment that the transcript absorbed.

The commitment to the whole trace is not new. By the timing rule, the secondary circuit's trace commitment becomes a claim on the partner curve that the next step folds. So the next step's primary circuit already holds it, along with the nested commitment and the commitment to the later part. All three are points on the partner curve, native to that circuit.

The book then requires two checks. The commitments to the parts must add up to the commitment to the whole trace, and the parts must not overlap. The code reaches the first of these by a different route, which we describe at the end.

Here is a shortened trace that stands in for a real one, since the toy curve has too few points for more. The secondary circuit adds the two points. Its stage holds their four coordinates in slots zero to three. The later part holds the slope, the difference of x-coordinates and the output in slots four to seven.

The stage uses the same four generators as before, so its commitment is the nested commitment, twelve, twelve. The later part uses the next four generators. The next step's primary circuit adds the two part commitments in three gates and compares the sum with the commitment to the whole trace. They match.

This check stops a prover who uses other coordinates. Suppose the prover traces the circuit with a different x-coordinate for the first point, while leaving the nested commitment alone. The site tried each of the thirty other values. Each one changes the whole trace's commitment, and all thirty fail the comparison.

A commitment sum cannot enforce the second check, however. Let the prover put fifteen in slot zero of the later part, which is reserved for the stage. The stage still says twenty-seven, but the sum holds eleven in that slot, so the secondary circuit runs on a different point. We chose fifteen so that the new point still lies on the curve and the run looks normal, though any nonzero value would do. The sum check still passes, for all thirty nonzero values, because a commitment to a sum equals the sum of the commitments however the entries overlap.

So a separate check has to show that the later part is zero in the stage's slots. In our toy form, the verifier picks a random challenge, and asks for a combination of the four reserved slots, weighted by powers of the challenge, to be zero. The entries lie modulo thirty-one, so the challenge does too. This is the later part evaluated at the challenge with its other slots masked out, meaning set to zero.

The verifier holds only a commitment to the later part, so it cannot compute this number itself. The prover has to claim that the number is zero and back the claim with a proof about the committed polynomial. The staging chapter builds that claim as a revdot product with a stage mask, which is also the book's form, and the next step folds it with the other claims on the partner curve. Like the other challenges, this one comes from the primary circuit's transcript, so it too has to cross into the smaller field.

The strength of the check comes from how few challenge values a cheater can survive. For the entry of fifteen above, the combination equals fifteen at every challenge, so all thirty-one values catch it. A prover that puts nonzero entries in two adjacent reserved slots gets a line in the challenge, and over all nine hundred pairs of nonzero entries, none vanishes at more than one value.

Other pairs of slots give the prover more room. Slots zero and three give a combination that vanishes where the challenge cubed hits one fixed value. Since three divides thirty, a nonzero number modulo thirty-one has either no cube root or three. With both entries equal to one, the combination vanishes at six, twenty-six and thirty. Over all six pairs of slots, the worst case is three values. That is the general bound, because the combination is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most three in the challenge, and such a polynomial has at most three roots.

To summarize, each step leaves two kinds of work for the next. It has to check that the partial traces are well formed, and that the deferred group operations were computed correctly. Both are claims about a circuit that the next step folds like any other, which is why deferring them does not make them pile up.

Sharing one set of challenges.

The two circuits must use the same challenges. The primary circuit folds the value and the secondary circuit folds the point, and these only describe one polynomial if both use one beta. Running a second transcript in the secondary circuit would give different values. The book's answer is called transcript bridging, where only the primary circuit hashes, and its challenges serve both circuits. That is safe only together with input consistency, because the secondary circuit's inputs reach the transcript only through nested commitments, and they have to be fixed before the challenges are.

The secondary circuit still has to receive the challenges, and the primary circuit has to be able to confirm that it received the right ones. The staging tool does this too. The prover puts the challenges in a stage of the secondary circuit with no blinding. That stage's commitment is then a fixed combination of generators of the partner curve, and those points are native to the primary circuit. So the next step's primary circuit can recompute the commitment from its own copy of the challenges and compare, in the same step as the sum check.

On the toy cycle the challenge stage holds eleven, thirteen and fifteen, on three more generators of the partner curve. The primary circuit knows the three challenges as wires, so it breaks each into bits and computes the same combination itself. This takes ninety-nine gates and gives the same point. If the prover hands the secondary circuit any of the thirty other values of beta, the stage commits to a different point, and the comparison fails.

This check compares scalars on the partner curve, which has thirty-one points, so it only sees each challenge modulo thirty-one. Whether that loses anything depends on which field is larger. In the toy cycle a challenge travels from forty-three into thirty-one, and twelve of the forty-three values are thirty-one or larger. So more than a quarter of the possible challenges have no equal in the smaller field. In Pasta a challenge travels from p into q, and p is smaller than q, so every Pasta challenge already fits unchanged.

So the toy cycle has a gap in the direction that challenges travel, and Pasta does not. A large toy challenge exposes it. Suppose beta is thirty-eight instead of fifteen. A challenge stage holding seven in place of thirty-eight commits to the same point, because the partner curve has thirty-one points, and thirty-eight times a generator equals seven times it. So the check passes, and the secondary circuit folds with seven. On the first curve, though, scalars are taken modulo forty-three, and thirty-eight times a point differs from seven times it.

The challenge stage holds a number modulo thirty-one, so it cannot carry the integer thirty-eight, and the check cannot tell that the circuits used different scalars. On a cycle like the toy one, where the primary field is the larger, the fix is to make every challenge short enough to be a valid element of both fields unchanged. That is why our run used values below sixteen.

Shorter challenges.

On Pasta this attack has no counterpart, since every challenge already fits. Ragu still shortens beta, the challenge that scales points, and the reason there is cost. Both circuits multiply points by beta, so its length sets the price of every point fold. With the plain double-and-add, multiplying a point by a full two hundred fifty-five bit challenge costs two thousand five hundred forty-one gates. That is more than a whole Ragu circuit, and a fold needs one such multiplication for every commitment it combines.

The code therefore uses one hundred forty-three bit values called endoscalars. It computes with them through their bits, using the curve's endomorphism to get more than one bit out of each point addition, and the endoscalars chapter explains that method and its cost. In the code the short value also maps to a scalar that is not the plain integer, and our toy run does not reproduce that map.

A short challenge gives a prover a better chance of hitting a lucky value, since there are fewer values to try. Take a cheater, as in chapter eight, that shifts both a claimed value and the matching evaluated value by the same amount. The quotient value uses only their difference, so it does not change. The error in the folded value is then the first shift times beta, plus the second shift, which vanishes at exactly one beta.

The cheater makes that beta the short value fifteen, by shifting the first claim by one and the second by minus fifteen. Among all forty-three challenges, one passes. Among the sixteen short challenges, the same one passes, so the cheater's chance moves from one in forty-three to one in sixteen.

On a real curve that chance is the degree of the error polynomial divided by two to the one hundred forty-three, which remains negligible. The book notes that endoscalars are only for public challenges, since this shorter range would be unsafe for a secret.

Two steps of ping-pong.

We can now run the fold for two steps, and then add the checks of the last three sections to the second step. In step one, each circuit does the work we just traced. In step two, each accumulator meets a new claim from the trace of a circuit in step one. The toy curves cannot hold a full trace, so we take four wire values from each circuit's gates as a stand-in polynomial, and commit to it on that circuit's curve.

The commitment made by the primary circuit goes to the secondary circuit in the next step for its point arithmetic, and the same holds the other way around. Step two uses new challenges, six, ten and nine. Each circuit's fold takes seventy-six gates in both steps. The gate count does not change between steps, which is the fixed circuit that recursion requires.

The run also has to respect when each step's challenges are fixed. Each step's transcript absorbs its nested commitment before it squeezes the challenges, in the same step that folds the commitments behind it. In step one the sponge absorbs the nested commitment twelve, twelve, and squeezes two, and only after that does it produce the challenges. The primary circuit replays this hash in gates, so its challenges are tied to the nested commitment. Our toy run then replaces the real challenges with hand-picked short ones. Step two does the same with its own inputs.

Our stand-in traces are too small to hold a stage. So for the checks in step two, we take step one's secondary circuit to be the staged trace and challenge stage from earlier. Step two's primary circuit then runs the sum check in three gates, recomputes the challenge stage in ninety-nine gates, and passes the overlap claim on to a later fold. With its own fold, that makes one hundred seventy-eight gates before the sponge's gates.

A real circuit runs these checks in every step, so they add a fixed number of gates. In step one there is no earlier secondary circuit, and the base case supplies placeholder proofs, so the same gates run on those.

At the end, one decider handles both curves. It commits to the two final polynomials and evaluates them at ten, getting six and thirteen, as the accumulators say. That single decision on each curve covers the two claims of step one and the two traces it produced. If the first curve's accumulator had claimed fifteen at the start of step two, the primary circuit would have derived thirty-nine, and the decider would have rejected, since the polynomial still evaluates to six.

Why cheating fails.

The division of the fold leaves new places to cheat. In each case a check that the honest prover passes without effort catches the cheater, though the catch may come one step later or at the decider.

A prover cannot report a wrong result from a circuit, because the gates determine it. The site tried every wrong output for the addition of the two points with every possible slope witness. That makes one thousand two hundred seventy-one attempts, and none satisfied the three gates.

A prover cannot give the circuits different data. The sum check catches a prover who uses other coordinates in a trace, and the overlap check catches one who hides a change in a reserved slot. Changing the nested commitment itself to match would change the transcript and every later challenge. So the prover has to commit to the secondary circuit's inputs before it learns the challenges, as in chapter three's discussion of omitted data.

A prover cannot give the circuits different challenges, because the primary circuit recomputes the challenge stage's commitment from its own transcript. That comparison sees each challenge only modulo thirty-one, as the example of thirty-eight showed, so on the toy cycle the shortness of the challenges is part of this defence. On Pasta every challenge already fits, and the comparison alone suffices.

A prover might hope to skip the deferred work, because each of these checks happens in the next step, and the last step has no next step. That is why the decider has to examine both accumulators and the root proof's stages. If a false point or a false stage survives to the end, one of the claims on the two curves is false, and the decider rejects it by the argument of the accumulation chapter. A verifier that checks only one curve leaves half of every fold unconfirmed, since that half happened on the other side.

The argument is informal, and it rests on these assumptions. The discrete log relation assumption has to hold for the generators of both curves, so both curves have to be secure, which is one more reason an anomalous curve cannot serve. The transcript hash has to behave as a random oracle. Challenges have to come after the data they test, and short challenges have to be long enough for the degree of the claims. The decider has to run on both sides.

Cycles in Ragu.

The book describes the design as inspired by CycleFold. The site's comparison uses only CycleFold's abstract, which says that the second curve merely represents a single scalar multiplication in a tiny circuit, and that the first curve folds invocations of that circuit. Ragu's code shares the main feature of that picture, because its application lives on one side only. It differs in having several internal circuits on the other side, and this comparison is the site's own.

The following is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The code's native side is the primary circuit, with commitments on its host curve, and its nested side is the secondary circuit. Polynomials on the nested side that hold native commitments are called bridge stages. A proof lists these three groups of polynomials, and one call to fuse produces all of them.

For nested commitments, the code places the children's Vesta commitments in a stage over q and commits to it on Pallas. Fuse then absorbs that point and squeezes the first challenge, and an internal hashing circuit repeats this with gates. The transcript absorbs only such bridge commitments. Pallas commitments go into native stages whose Vesta commitments in turn ride in the bridge stages, so they too are fixed before the challenges that test them.

Each side has a walk that performs the weighted point sum for the other side. The nested walk folds points on Vesta, and the native walk folds points on Pallas. A walk does not fit in one circuit of at most two to the eleven gates, so the native one spreads across twenty-three circuits, each doing four endoscalings.

The nested side squeezes nothing, and it reads each challenge as a lift of a native one. Its challenge stage is committed without blinding, and the bind challenges circuits, finished by a parent's bind beta circuit, recompute its commitment on Pallas. This is the check from the section on sharing challenges. The uncompressed verifier checks claims on both sides. It also rederives the root's challenge stage, because the root has no parent to bind it.

The book and the code differ in several places, and the site gives both. First, the book has an application circuit on each side, with an application state made of a pair. In the code every nested circuit is internal, a step's headers live over the circuit field alone, and the application lives only on the native side. This site follows the code, which is also the closer match to the book's own CycleFold description.

Second, the book says the commitments to the partial traces add up to the trace commitment, and the next primary circuit checks that in-circuit. The site found no such addition in the code. The only point additions in the internal circuits are in the two walks and the challenge bindings. A proof keeps a commitment for each stage and none for the whole trace, each stage commitment goes into the batch on its own, and a circuit's claim uses the sum of its stage polynomials.

So the site reads the code as establishing the sum through polynomial claims and not through a point addition, and this reading is the site's own. Our example teaches the book's form, because it shows the purpose of the check on small numbers, and the site otherwise follows the code. The book also describes in a second place which circuit runs these checks, and that description disagrees with the first. The staging chapter sets out both, along with the code.

Third, the book's transcript bridging section sets the next challenge to an endoscalar extracted from the hash output. In the code's native side, alpha and the evaluation point are used as full field elements, and only beta is extracted to an endoscalar, because it is the one that scales points. The nested side lifts every challenge from its low bits. This site follows the code, and our toy run uses short values on both sides to keep one set of numbers.

Earlier chapters recorded related differences, in the base case and the root of initial states. The book's single fuse circuit per curve corresponds in the code to many internal circuits, which the chapter on recursion in Ragu lists. All of this applies to the Ragu revision this site pins. Tachyon, which has not activated on mainnet and whose design is still changing, depends on this design through a different Ragu revision.

Where this leaves us.

A commitment's scalars and coordinates lie in different fields, so a circuit over the scalar field can fold values directly but cannot fold points directly. Emulating the other field with limbs works, but in our plain design one point addition already exceeds the size of a Ragu circuit. A two-cycle of curves gives each field a curve whose points are native to it. Pallas and Vesta form such a cycle, and a single anomalous curve is not an option.

Each recursive step has two circuits. Each one folds the values of its own accumulator and the points of the other, for the instances of the previous step. A nested commitment places foreign data behind one point that the transcript's field can absorb. Group operations on foreign points move to the other circuit as deferred operations, and staged traces with sum and overlap checks tie that circuit's wires to the nested commitment.

Only the primary circuit hashes, and it checks the secondary circuit's copy of the challenges through a commitment on its native curve. Short challenges are required when the primary field is the larger one, as in the toy cycle. On Pasta it is the smaller one, and Ragu shortens beta to lower the cost of point arithmetic. Every check in a step depends on the next step, and the decider has to finish both curves' claims at the end.

So far our accumulators have folded evaluation claims, but a proof also holds the revdot claims of chapter nine. The next chapter folds those, and the split of this chapter applies to them too.
