Chapter fourteen. Revdot Folding.

The chapter on split accumulation ended with a gap in its recursive relation. Its fold makes every opening sound, which means each commitment really opens to the value its proof states. But it does not check that a proof's revdot product equals its target, or that the two vectors behind that product are the ones inside the proof's commitments.

The verifier in the chapter on the complete proof did both checks, by reading the two vectors in full. A recursive verifier reads only instances, so it cannot do the same. This chapter closes the gap with a second fold, one that handles revdot claims.

A revdot claim contains a product of two unknown vectors, so it does not combine linearly the way an evaluation claim does. The split accumulation chapter already showed the first sign of this, a cross term that the prover has to supply. We start from that example and find that its single challenge stops working at three claims. Repairing it leads to the error matrix and to two challenges called mu and nu. We then remove the two costs this fold would place in a circuit, and we finish with the different fold that Ragu's code runs at each step.

The book and the code differ.

The Ragu Book and the code differ on this chapter's subject. The book's revdot chapter folds with an error matrix. The code's fuse, at the commit this site pins, uses a different fold with one challenge, which we derive near the end. We teach the matrix first, because it is the general construction and because the code's compression still uses it.

The claim that remains.

A step of the proof from the chapter on the complete proof ends up holding two commitments and a target value. It needs to know that the committed vectors have that revdot product. Recall that the revdot product pairs the first entry of one vector with the last entry of the other, and so on inward, then adds the products.

We'll call the two vectors the left vector and the right vector, and their commitments the left commitment and the right commitment. Following the book, a revdot claim has an instance and a witness. The instance holds the two commitments and the target. The witness holds the two vectors and a blinding factor for each.

The claim holds when three things are true. Each commitment opens to its vector with its blinding factor, and the revdot product of the two vectors equals the target. The first two conditions tie the vectors to the commitments, and the third is the product. The split accumulation relation left all three unchecked. A fold of revdot claims has to keep them together, so that the vectors whose product gets checked are the ones behind the commitments.

The proof from the chapter on the complete proof gives one such claim, with target thirteen and vectors of eight entries. Its two commitments recompute to the stated points, and the revdot product of its vectors does come to thirteen.

The earlier chapters offer two ways to check a claim like this, and each puts its cost in the wrong place. The direct check makes the verifier pay. For real vectors of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two entries, it takes that many field multiplications and two multi-scalar multiplications of that size, and both vectors have to travel. It cannot go in a circuit, since a recursive verifier reads only instances.

The split from the chapter on polynomial encoding makes the prover pay instead. Only two commitments and a few values travel, which suits a verifier. But the prover needs at least three fast Fourier transforms, at about one hundred fourteen thousand six hundred eighty-eight multiplications each, and it would run them at every step of a long computation. The book wants to confine that cost to the places in the tree where a short proof is needed.

The split accumulation chapter suggests a third route. We reduce many claims to one claim of the same form and leave the last one to a decider. The reduction has to handle many claims at once, because a fuse, the proof-carrying data operation that contains the folds, collects many revdot claims. The previous step's accumulators are among them, and the staging chapter lists the rest. As in the curve cycles chapter, the point operations of every fold go to the circuit on the other curve.

This chapter removes the split and its transforms from most steps, as the chapter on the complete proof promised. It does not check that the right commitment was built from the right circuit. The chapter on wiring and the registry handles that.

Two claims and one cross term.

Start with two claims, and weight the second by a challenge we'll call r, as the split accumulation chapter did. The commitments combine by linearity, so anyone can compute the first left commitment plus r times the second, and the same on the right.

The target needs more care, because the revdot product is linear in each of its two arguments separately. Expanding both arguments of the folded product gives four products. The first claim's own product carries weight one, and the second claim's own product carries weight r squared. The two mixed products each carry weight r.

The outer two products are the claimed targets, which the verifier already holds. The middle two pair one claim's left vector with the other claim's right vector, and no claim says anything about them. So the prover supplies their sum, which we call the cross term. The new target is the first target, plus r times the cross term, plus r squared times the second target.

Here is the example from the split accumulation chapter, rewritten as revdot products. The first claim has target twenty-nine and the second has target nine. The two mixed products are forty-two and three, so the cross term is two, modulo forty-three. At r equal to nine, the folded vectors have revdot product two, and the formula also gives two.

The new claim has the same form as the old ones. The prover has to send the cross term before it learns r, and a count shows why. Suppose the prover states the false target thirty in place of twenty-nine. The two sides of the check then differ by one, plus r times the gap between the cross term it sends and the true one.

We tried all forty-three possible cross terms against all forty-three values of r. The honest cross term never passes, and each of the other forty-two passes at exactly one value of r. But a prover that could choose its cross term after seeing r could solve this linear equation and pass every time. So a false claim survives only if r lands on one root. This is the root count from the chapter on statements and challenges, applied to a polynomial in r whose constant term is the false claim's error.

Three claims break the single challenge.

The natural extension gives claim number i the weight r to the power i, on both sides. Bilinearity then gives one product for every pair of claims. Pairing claim i's left vector with claim j's right vector gives an entry we'll call e i j. For a true claim, the diagonal entry e i i equals that claim's target. We write m for the number of claims. The book uses n, but on this site n remains the number of gates.

Expand the fold for three claims and collect the powers of r. Now look at the coefficient of r squared. It adds the middle claim's target to two cross terms, and the verifier can check only this sum. If the middle target is false, the prover can move the error into a cross term, which it controls, and the polynomial in r stays exactly right.

To see this we need real claims. We take three proofs of the step function from the split accumulation chapter's chain, which maps z to z cubed plus z plus five. They show that the function sends three to thirty-five, thirty-five to one, and one to seven, and their targets are thirteen, thirty-five and thirty-nine. The toy group has only forty-three points, so some commitments repeat across claims, which carries no meaning.

For the attack we need one false claim. The chapter on the complete proof built a false witness for the statement that the function sends three to thirty-five. It filled the trace with x equal to four, which does not satisfy the statement, since the function sends four to thirty. Its target comes from the statement, so it is thirteen, but its vectors have revdot product eighteen.

We call the following list the false list, and later sections reuse it. The honest proof for the input thirty-five sits in slot zero, the false witness in slot one, and the honest proof for the input one in slot two.

Now we attack the fold. The false claim sits in slot one, so its diagonal entry carries r squared, and it is false by five. With every cross term honest, the fold rejects at all forty-two nonzero values of r. It passes at r equal to zero, where only the true claim in slot zero keeps its weight.

The cross terms that share r squared have the honest sum eleven. The cheater sends sixteen instead, which is eleven plus five, and it sends this before it knows r. At r equal to nine both sides equal fifteen, and running all forty-three values of r gives forty-three passes.

The fold's output is a true claim, because the folded vectors really have the stated product, so the decider accepts it too. The harm is that this output certifies the statement that the function sends three to thirty-five. The only witness behind it was built for x equal to four and does not satisfy that statement. A verifier who trusts the chain would accept a computation step that never happened.

The challenge did nothing here, because the error and its cancellation carried the same power of r, whatever r was. With two claims, each diagonal entry had a power of r to itself, but that stops being true at three. So we need weights under which no cross term can share a monomial with a diagonal entry.

Two challenges and the error matrix.

Give the two sides independent weights, one family on the left vectors and another on the right vectors. Bilinearity then turns the folded product into a sum over every pair i, j, where each entry is multiplied by its row's left weight and its column's right weight. If the left weights are powers of one challenge and the right weights are powers of a second, independent challenge, then every cell gets its own monomial.

The book makes the same choice in a different form. It takes two challenges, mu and nu. It puts the weight mu to the minus i on left vector i, and mu times nu, to the power j, on right vector j. This is the plain choice with the two challenges renamed, since the left challenge is the inverse of mu and the right one is mu times nu. Each pair of plain challenges with a nonzero left challenge comes from exactly one pair mu and nu, so the two forms fold in the same way.

We follow the book because its form makes the diagonal easy to read. Multiplying a row weight by a column weight gives mu to the power j minus i, times nu to the power j. On the diagonal, mu cancels and the weight is nu to the power i. So the claimed targets depend on nu alone, and mu only spreads the cross terms apart. The two exponents determine the cell, so no two cells share a monomial.

The array of all the entries e i j is called the error matrix. The folded claim's left commitment is the sum of the left commitments, weighted by powers of the inverse of mu. Its right commitment is the sum of the right commitments, weighted by powers of mu times nu. Its target is the weighted sum of every cell. The verifier fills the diagonal of that sum with its own targets, and the prover supplies the other m squared minus m entries.

We can replay the cheat that just succeeded, with the same false claim and the same shift of five on a cross term in row zero, column two. The diagonal error now has weight nu, while the shift has weight mu squared times nu squared. The two cancel only where mu squared times nu equals one, or where nu is zero.

Across all one thousand eight hundred six pairs with nonzero mu, the fold passes at eighty-four, which is under five percent. Half of those have nu equal to zero. There the right side reduces to the first right vector, and the other claims drop out of the check. A later section counts the cheater's other options.

One fold on the toy curve.

The book's steps run in the order the argument requires. Both parties start with the list of instances. The prover computes every entry of the error matrix off the diagonal and sends these cross terms. Only then does the verifier send mu and nu. The verifier computes the new instance, and the prover computes the new vectors and both blinding factors.

Take the three true claims from before. The prover computes the error matrix, whose diagonal holds thirteen, thirty-five and thirty-nine. These match the targets in the instances, so the prover leaves them out and sends the other six entries.

The verifier sends mu equal to six and nu equal to thirteen. The inverse of mu is thirty-six, and mu times nu is thirty-five. Weighting all nine cells and adding them gives the new target, nineteen. The verifier applies the same weights to the commitments, and the prover applies them to the vectors and to the blinding factors.

The blinding factors follow the vectors because each commitment is linear in its vector and its blinding factor together, as the chapter on polynomial commitments showed. Meanwhile the verifier's work touches only instances and cross terms.

The decider now has one claim to check. The revdot product of the folded vectors is nineteen, equal to the new target, and both commitments recompute to the new instance. Three revdot products and six commitment checks have become one product and two commitment checks. An honest prover should pass whatever the challenges are, and it does, at all one thousand eight hundred six pairs.

An accumulator of revdot claims.

The output has the same form as each input, so it can serve as an accumulator, and the book says exactly this. Each step places the old accumulator in the list along with the step's new claims.

This explains why the chapter on the complete proof kept two names for what was then the same commitment, the trace commitment and the left factor of the revdot product. In recursion they differ, because the trace commitment holds one step's trace, while the left factor can hold an aggregate of many claims. This accumulator sits beside the one from the split accumulation chapter, and the book lists both in the accumulator of one curve.

Here is a chain over the first three proofs, folding one new claim at each step. It starts from the proof for the input three, with target thirteen. The first fold adds the proof for the input thirty-five, with two cross terms, and the target becomes thirty-two. The second fold adds the proof for the input one, and the target becomes twenty-five. Every vector still has eight entries, because each fold takes a linear combination of vectors of that length.

Suppose the chain stops here. The decider finds that the last accumulator's vectors have revdot product twenty-five, equal to the target, and both commitments recompute, so it accepts. Its work is two multi-scalar multiplications and one revdot product, once for the whole history.

The prover pays for the cross terms at each step. A fold of one accumulator and one new claim needs two cross terms. For dense vectors of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two entries, that is sixteen thousand three hundred eighty-four multiplications. The split needs at least three hundred forty-four thousand and sixty-four, and then the prover still has to commit to two more polynomials.

That second figure uses the usual estimate for a radix-two transform. The fold needs no product polynomial, since each cross term is one number computed in one pass over the vectors. So most steps never run a transform. The split comes back only in compression, where a proof leaves the tree.

Replacing the commitment sums.

The verifier of the toy fold computes the new left and right commitments, which takes two m scalar multiplications. Inside a circuit, those are operations on points whose coordinates lie in the wrong field. So the curve cycles chapter would defer each one to the circuit on the other curve, where each still costs a scalar multiplication. The book reduces their number by reusing a mechanism we already have.

The spot check.

The relation between the commitments says that the polynomials behind them satisfy the same weighted sum, as long as the commitments are binding. An identity between polynomials can be tested at a random point. So the prover names the new left commitment without the verifier computing it, and the verifier sends a random point.

The book calls this point beta, but the chapter on batched openings already uses beta for its batching challenge. So we call the point theta. The prover reports the value of each input left polynomial at theta, and the value of the new one there. The verifier checks that the new value equals the weighted sum of the input values. The right side works in the same way.

This check is field arithmetic, which a circuit handles natively. It only means something if the reported values are true. So each reported value becomes an evaluation claim. That gives one claim for every input and output polynomial on both sides, which is twice the number of claims, plus two. They join the list that the split accumulation chapter's accumulator folds anyway.

For the toy fold, take theta equal to twenty-two. The prover reports the values, and field arithmetic alone confirms both weighted sums, modulo forty-three.

The challenge theta has to come after the prover names the new commitments, because a wrong commitment shows up as a nonzero difference polynomial. Suppose the prover names a commitment to the folded left vector with its entry four raised by one. The difference between the two sides is then theta to the fourth, so the check fails at every theta except zero.

A cheater who chooses the change more carefully does better, but only up to a limit. The vectors have eight entries, so the difference has degree at most seven, and at most seven roots. A cheater can reach that limit, and the check then passes at seven of the forty-three values. At the other thirty-six, a prover who wants to pass has to report a false value, which becomes a false evaluation claim for the batching to catch.

Why the spot check costs less.

The saving depends on what the list already contains. The split accumulation list holds the evaluation claims of each fresh proof. Two of them concern the same commitments as that proof's revdot claim, because the proof opens those commitments at two of its points. So the spot check adds a second point to polynomials that the list already queries.

The batching from the chapter on batched openings combines all the claims into one commitment, with one weighted term for each distinct polynomial and one for the quotient commitment. So it pays one scalar multiplication per polynomial, however many points that polynomial is queried at. The claimed values combine into one number, which costs only field arithmetic.

For our three proofs, the combined commitment held the quotient commitment and six input commitments before the spot check. After it, the combined commitment holds the quotient commitment and eight commitments, since only the two folded commitments are new. So the spot check adds two scalar multiplications to the deferred work, where the direct sums would add six. The rest of its new work is field arithmetic.

The book states the condition, that the saving appears when an input polynomial is already queried elsewhere. A claim whose polynomials no other check queries gains nothing. An old accumulator in our runs is such a claim, since its commitments enter as new polynomials and cost one scalar multiplication each, as the direct sum would.

What follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The code has no separate theta. It reuses the point x from the complete proof, and it fixes the folded pair before it draws x. So x plays the role of a theta drawn after the folded commitments are fixed. The chapter on recursion in Ragu shows the order of the messages.

Two layers.

The circuit still has to compute the new target, which has m squared terms. Factoring the weights lets the circuit use Horner's rule, which is repeated multiply and add. For each row it sums the entries weighted by powers of mu times nu, and then it sums the row totals weighted by powers of the inverse of mu.

Each inner sum takes m minus one multiplications, and the outer sum takes m minus one more, for m squared minus one in total. Precomputing mu times nu and the inverse of mu adds two, which gives the book's count of m squared plus one constraints. For the toy matrix that is eight multiplications plus the two precomputed values, and the result is again nineteen.

Quadratic growth is a problem because each circuit is limited to two to the eleven gates. The book's example of one hundred thirty-three claims would need far more than that. The book also notes that smaller circuits lead to smaller commitments and faster verifiers later on. Since the cost is the square of the list's length, shorter lists help, so we can fold in stages.

The two-layer reduction.

Divide the claims into N groups of M claims each. In the first layer, the prover sends the cross terms within each group. The verifier sends mu and nu, and folding each group gives one intermediate claim per group.

In the second layer, the prover computes the cross terms between the intermediate claims and sends them. The verifier sends fresh challenges, mu prime and nu prime, and folding the intermediate claims gives the final claim. The second layer's diagonal holds the intermediate targets, which the verifier computed in the first layer. Cross terms between claims in different groups never appear on their own, because they are bundled into the cross terms between intermediate claims. That is why the count drops.

We extend the chain to six proofs and use two groups of three. The first group holds the three claims from before. The second holds proofs that the function sends seven to eleven, eleven to fourteen, and fourteen to eleven.

In the first layer the prover sends twelve cross terms, six per group. The verifier sends mu equal to six and nu equal to thirteen, and the two groups give intermediate targets nineteen and one. In the second layer the prover sends two cross terms, the verifier sends fresh challenges, and the final target is nine. The final vectors have revdot product nine, so the decider accepts.

The prover sent fourteen cross terms, where a single layer over six claims would have needed thirty. The book's count for this split is twenty-three constraints, against thirty-seven for one layer.

Applying Horner's rule at each layer gives the book's general count. The total is N times M squared, plus N squared, minus N, plus three. The first term favors small groups and the second favors few groups, so the best split lies between the extremes. For its one hundred thirty-three claims, the book picks nineteen groups of seven. That costs one thousand two hundred seventy-six constraints, where one layer would cost seventeen thousand six hundred ninety.

Why cheating fails.

This argument is informal. On the toy curve every discrete log is known, which permits forgeries. So the counts below model a prover who holds real vectors and does not use those relations, except in the last example.

Start with a false claim and honest cross terms. Take the false list, whose false claim sits in slot one with diagonal weight nu. The stated target then differs from the true product by minus five times nu, which is zero only when nu is zero. The fold passes at forty-two of the one thousand eight hundred six pairs, all of them with nu equal to zero.

So the fold really does accept a false claim at nu equal to zero. But a uniform challenge takes that value with probability one over the field size. That is one chance in forty-three on the toy curve, and negligible on a field of two hundred fifty-five bits. A false claim in slot zero has weight one and no such exception.

A cheater can also move the cross terms away from their honest values. The difference between the two sides then becomes a polynomial in mu and nu, which is nonzero because of the diagonal error. Multiplying by a power of mu clears the negative powers without changing any root with nonzero mu. The total degree is then at most three times m minus one, which is six here.

So the Schwartz Zippel bound allows at most two hundred fifty-eight roots among all the pairs. The best attack we found raises two cross terms so that the difference vanishes for every nu at mu equal to three and at mu equal to seven. It passes at one hundred twenty-six of the one thousand eight hundred six pairs. The best of three hundred random choices passes at eighty-three. On a real field with one hundred thirty-three claims, the bound falls below two to the minus two hundred forty-five.

Sending cross terms after the challenges.

Every count so far assumes that the prover fixes its cross terms first. In a non-interactive proof, that means the transcript absorbs them before it produces mu and nu. At mu equal to six and nu equal to thirteen, the false list fails with honest cross terms. But a prover who already knows these challenges can raise one cross term by forty-two, which solves one linear equation, and the fold passes. The same shift committed in advance passes at only eighty-four of the one thousand eight hundred six pairs.

Two layers and fresh challenges.

A false claim survives the first layer as a false intermediate claim, which the second layer treats as an ordinary false claim on its diagonal. Put the false claim in the first group of the six-claim run, and keep the cross terms honest. The first layer passes at forty-two of the one thousand eight hundred six pairs. The error then sits on the second layer's diagonal with weight one, so the second layer passes at none of its pairs.

The book explains the fresh challenges by noting that the root-counting argument applies separately at each layer. That argument needs each layer's messages to come before that layer's challenges. The second layer's messages depend on the first layer's result, so reusing the challenges breaks the order.

Suppose the second layer reused mu and nu. The prover learns them before it sends the second layer's cross terms, so it can compute the error in the first group's target and cancel it through a cross term. Doing this at every pair with nonzero mu and nu passes all one thousand seven hundred sixty-four of them. With fresh challenges the same message is fixed first, and it passes at forty-two of the one thousand eight hundred six pairs.

A false claim reaches the decider.

As in the split accumulation chapter, the verifier checks arithmetic and has no way to know that a claim is true. Start the chain of three proofs from the false witness, and keep every later step honest. The false claim is now the starting accumulator, in slot zero of each fold.

Its stated target is thirteen while the true product is eighteen. Slot zero's diagonal weight is one, so the gap passes through each fold unchanged, and the verifier sees nothing wrong. After two folds, the stated target is forty-one and the true product is three, the same gap modulo forty-three. The decider rejects the last accumulator, because its product differs from its stated target.

So a chain that never runs the decider has established nothing about its revdot claims. Two more requirements come with this. The target of a new claim must come from the statement and not from the prover, which was the lesson of the chapter on the complete proof. And the old accumulator's instance must be tied to the previous proof, as the split accumulation chapter showed. A comment in the code records the first of these requirements.

Binding at the decider.

The decider's check means something only because the folded left commitment opens in just one way. Using the known discrete logs of the toy generators, a prover can state the false target twenty for the toy fold. It presents a different left vector that commits to the same folded left commitment and has revdot product twenty, so the decider accepts. On a real curve, such a vector would give a discrete log relation among the generators.

The assumptions.

The reasoning relies on the discrete log relation assumption. It treats the transcript hash as a random oracle, with each challenge drawn after the data it tests. It assumes that the circuit takes each target from the statement or from the previous proof, and that the decider runs honestly. Private retries under Fiat Shamir add to these chances. We do not analyse Ragu's scheme as a whole here.

The quotient fold in Ragu's fuse.

The matrix has one remaining cost. The prover sends one field element per off-diagonal cell, and each of those values enters the circuit. But the verifier uses them only through the weighted sum that forms the new target. So we can ask whether a shorter message could determine that sum.

The code at the commit this site pins answers this by changing the weights. Its fold has a single challenge, which we call tau to keep it apart from the matrix's mu. The code and the later chapters call this challenge mu, and its second-layer counterpart mu prime, so read tau as mu when comparing with them.

The derivation.

The single challenge failed earlier because the prover's message entered the check with the same power of r as a claim's own diagonal entry. The matrix fixed this by giving every cell its own monomial, at the price of m squared minus m messages. A different fix keeps one challenge and instead makes the message harmless at the places where the claims get checked.

Suppose each claim i gets checked by what some polynomial in a new variable, T, says at T equal to i. Suppose also that the prover's message enters only through a product with the vanishing polynomial, which is the product of T minus i over every claim. That polynomial is zero at every node. So the message drops out at every node, and nothing the prover sends can change what a claim's own check sees.

To put claim i at node i, we interpolate. For each claim, take the Lagrange basis polynomial that equals one at that claim's node and zero at the other nodes, as in the chapter on polynomial encoding. Weighting each claim's left vector by its basis polynomial and adding gives a left vector polynomial. Doing the same for the right vectors and for the targets gives a right vector polynomial and a target polynomial. At node i, these reduce to claim i's own vectors and target.

Now take the difference between the revdot product of the two vector polynomials and the target polynomial. At node i, this difference equals e i i minus claim i's target. So it vanishes at every node exactly when all the claims are true, and a polynomial that vanishes at all m nodes is a multiple of the vanishing polynomial.

This is the divisibility test of the polynomial encoding chapter, applied to a list of claims in place of a list of gates. The nodes play the role of the domain. As there, the prover fixes the quotient before a random point tests the identity.

Count the degrees. The product of the two vector polynomials has degree at most two m minus two, and the vanishing polynomial has degree m. So the quotient has degree at most m minus two, and therefore only m minus one coefficients. This is the shorter message. The prover sends the coefficients of the quotient, and the verifier sends one challenge, tau.

Evaluating at tau gives the new claim. Both sides get the same weights, which are the basis polynomials' values at tau. The new target is the target polynomial at tau, plus the vanishing polynomial at tau times the quotient at tau. That is right, because the folded product equals the target polynomial plus the difference, and the difference equals the vanishing polynomial times the quotient.

The prover's work has not shrunk, though. Expanding the product of the vector polynomials still needs every off-diagonal entry, which are the same products that the matrix fold needs. The diagonal adds nothing, because an honest prover can use each claim's target in place of its diagonal product.

The code calls the coefficients of the quotient error terms. The book uses cross term and error term interchangeably for an off-diagonal product, which is a different number. So we keep cross term for a product and say quotient coefficient for the code's values. In the code, the prover commits to a stage holding the coefficients, and the transcript absorbs that commitment before squeezing tau.

A run with three claims.

We reuse the toy matrix, so the two folds can be compared directly. The prover divides by the vanishing polynomial, which leaves no remainder, and gets the quotient thirty-four plus thirty-one T. It sends these two coefficients before it sees the challenge.

The verifier sends tau equal to twenty. It finds that the target polynomial is zero there, the vanishing polynomial is three, and the quotient is nine. So the new target is three times nine, which is twenty-seven. The prover's folded vectors have revdot product twenty-seven as well, and all forty-three values of tau pass for these honest claims. The prover sent two field elements, where the error matrix needed six.

Why one challenge is enough.

The opening of the derivation gives the argument. Suppose claim i is false, and consider any quotient the prover might send. At node i the vanishing polynomial is zero, so the quotient's contribution disappears there, and the difference equals the claim's error, which is not zero. So no quotient makes the difference polynomial zero. It has degree at most two m minus two, so it has at most that many roots in tau.

Take the false list again, whose false claim sits in slot one and is false by five. We tried all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine choices of the two quotient coefficients against all forty-three values of tau. The best choice passes at four values of tau, which is exactly the bound.

Every choice passes at tau equal to zero and tau equal to two. At a node the weights select one claim and the vanishing polynomial is zero, so the fold checks only the claim in that slot. No choice passes at tau equal to one, the node of the false claim. Those passes at nodes show the fold's weak spot. The code's comments note it too, and they rely on a challenge hitting a node only with probability m over the size of the challenge set.

The message order matters as before. At tau equal to twenty, a cheater who already knew the challenge would need the quotient to equal thirty there, and one linear equation provides it. This is why the code fixes the quotient before the challenge.

The code's comments give each layer a failure bound of two m minus two over the size of the challenge set. They put the nested challenges in a set of two to the one hundred forty-three values, the endoscalar challenges of the curve cycles chapter. The largest reduction on the nested side has ten claims, which gives a bound below two to the minus one hundred thirty-eight.

Two layers in the code.

The code still folds in two layers, and the second layer treats the groups' values as its targets with a fresh challenge. For the six claims from before, the prover sends two quotients of two coefficients each, and then a second-layer quotient with one coefficient. The final target is forty-two, which equals the revdot product of the final vectors. The prover sent five field elements, where the matrix version sent fourteen.

A second layer still helps, even though the quotient fold already sends only m minus one numbers per layer. The prover still needs every off-diagonal product among the claims it folds together. Grouping limits those products to pairs inside one group, plus pairs of the groups' results. The code's comments give this reason. They also note that the prover sends one coefficient fewer than the number of slots, however the slots are grouped, so grouping costs the message nothing.

Comparing the three options.

On the native side there are ninety-eight slots, in fourteen groups of seven. The book's two-layer error matrix has the prover send seven hundred seventy field elements before the challenges. It computes seven hundred seventy off-diagonal products, and it spends eight hundred seventy-one multiplication gates folding the scalars.

The code's quotient fold computes the same seven hundred seventy products, but it sends only ninety-seven elements and spends two hundred sixty-two gates. A quotient fold with one layer would also send ninety-seven, but it would compute nine thousand five hundred six products and spend four hundred eighty-four gates. The Lagrange weights for ninety-eight nodes cost more than the weights for seven nodes and fourteen nodes together.

So the quotient fold does not trade prover work for a shorter message. It sends and checks far less for the same work. The matrix's gate count uses the book's formula, and the other two are our own count of the code's multiplications. They cover only the folding of scalars, since the circuit also computes each claim's target.

The code forms the diagonal products as well. Its division routine computes every product of a group and uses each diagonal product as that claim's target, for eight hundred eighty-two products in all. This is a choice of the implementation and not a requirement of the fold, since substituting the targets gives the same quotient.

The fold's output has the form of its inputs, so the code carries it as the revdot accumulator. At the next fuse, the folded pair enters the list as a claim whose target is the previous fold's target. The staging chapter and the chapter on recursion in Ragu cover the order of the messages, the stages that hold the quotients, and the circuit that checks the fold.

What the code shows.

Several points here are reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed them. The code's comments credit the quotient identity to a paper called ProtoGalaxy, which we could not read, so the derivation above is our own. The toy runs and the attacks are also our own, checked by the script behind the page, and the gate counts for the code's fold are our reading of its functions.

The native side has fourteen groups of seven, which gives ninety-eight slots for its eighty-six claims. The nested side has ten groups of eight for its seventy-two claims, and unused slots stay zero. The book's accumulator has blinding factors, but the code stores each polynomial with its commitment and no such factors, and our runs include them to match the chapter on the complete proof. One set of challenges drives both sides' folds, as the curve cycles chapter described for the other challenges.

All of this describes Ragu at the commit this site pins. Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and its design is still changing. It pins a different Ragu revision and runs its steps against a mock of Ragu, so nothing at its pinned commit executes this fold.

Compression and the forms of the fold.

Compression folds again when a proof leaves the tree, and here the matrix returns. The compression code also names its two challenges mu and nu, but they enter differently from the book's. Compression weights the left vectors by plain powers of its first challenge and the right vectors by plain powers of its second.

These are the plain weights we started from before renaming, so the book's renaming carries its form over to compression's. For the toy fold, the book's six and thirteen correspond to compression's thirty-six and thirty-five, which give the same folded vectors and the same target, nineteen.

The compression verifier does not read the cross terms either. The prover packs them into a committed vector, and a public vector holds their weights in mirrored positions. So the weighted sum of the cross terms becomes one more revdot product. Each of compression's two layers packs its cross terms this way, so the fold leaves three revdot claims. The split from the polynomial encoding chapter then proves a random combination of the three at once. This is the one place that pays for the product polynomial and its transforms.

The Ragu Book and the code differ here as well, and the sources contain four forms of this fold. The book's revdot chapter uses the weights we taught, with nineteen groups of seven. The book's chapter on structured vectors swaps which side gets which weight, and it has the prover send the whole matrix. The code's fuse, along with the book's recursion chapter, uses the quotient fold. The code's compression, along with the book's compression chapter, uses plain powers and commits to its cross terms.

The first, second and fourth forms are one family of weights written in different ways. The book's revdot chapter also says that its one hundred thirty-three claims belong to Ragu's fuse operation, whereas the code's fuse has eighty-six native claims and uses the quotient fold. This site follows the code. We found no statement in the sources of why the fuse uses one form and compression another, so we offer none.

Where this leaves us.

Let's collect the steps. A revdot claim has an instance of two commitments and a target, and a witness of two vectors and their blinding factors. Neither the direct check nor the split suits every step of a long computation. Folding two claims with one challenge needs one cross term, but with three claims a cross term can cancel a false target at every r.

Independent weights give each cell of the error matrix its own monomial, and the prover sends the off-diagonal cells before the challenges. The result serves as an accumulator, so most steps avoid the split, and a decider finishes with two multi-scalar multiplications and one revdot product.

A spot check at a random point turns the commitment sums into field arithmetic and evaluation claims, which saves work for claims whose polynomials another check already queries. Two layers with fresh challenges cut the quadratic field arithmetic. A false claim passes only at rare challenges, as long as each layer's messages come before its challenges and nobody knows a relation among the generators. If it does pass, it stays false until the decider.

Ragu's fuse replaces the matrix with a quotient of m minus one coefficients and one challenge per layer. The prover computes the same products as for the matrix, but it sends far fewer values, and the circuit checks far fewer. Compression keeps a committed form of the matrix and then runs the split.

The fold accepts any right commitment whose vector has the right product. We have not yet asked whether that vector contains the right circuit's wiring. The verifier's other linear cost, computing the wiring value, concerns the same question. The next chapter, Wiring and the Registry, answers it.
