Chapter seventeen. Staging.

Up to now a circuit's trace has been one polynomial, committed all at once. The curve cycles chapter already had to break that habit, because a nested commitment only works if it covers part of a trace that some other circuit runs on. That chapter showed the idea on a short list and promised the full mechanism here.

In this chapter we split a trace into pieces that are committed separately, find out what can go wrong when the pieces are added back together, and build the check that prevents it. Then we use the pieces for the two jobs Ragu needs them for, which are sharing data between circuits and committing to data before a challenge. The chapter closes with a difference that the curve cycles chapter left open, because the book adds the commitments of the pieces and we found no such addition in the code.

Much of what follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. That covers the trace layout, the masks and their closed form, the enforced and unenforced loading of stages, the bonding polynomials, and the code's version of the final check. Where the book and the code differ, we give both. The small circuits are our own, because the toy curves have too few points to serve as generators for one of Ragu's real circuits. The script behind this chapter runs each of them with the real gate and wiring equations.

A word on names before we start. The book calls the pieces stage polynomials, and the code's module documentation calls them staging polynomials. This site says stage polynomial, and it follows the book in calling the remainder the final trace.

Two jobs for a partial commitment.

Two situations in a recursive step call for committing to only part of a trace. The first is a challenge in the middle of a circuit. Suppose a circuit holds many wires and would like to check something about them with a random linear combination. The challenge must depend on those wires, or the prover could pick the wires to suit the challenge. So the circuit has to hash them, and hashing inside a circuit is expensive.

A commitment offers a cheaper route, since it binds all the wires under the same assumption as any Pedersen commitment and takes only two coordinates to hash. Those coordinates lie in the other field of the cycle, so the saving comes at the price of a nested commitment and a second circuit, as the curve cycles chapter found.

The toy sponge shows the size of the saving. It takes in two values per permutation, and one permutation applies thirty-two S-boxes of three multiplications each, which is ninety-six gates before counting anything else. Hashing eight wires therefore takes four permutations and three hundred eighty-four gates. Hashing the two coordinates of a commitment to those same eight wires takes one permutation and ninety-six gates, and that cost stays the same however many wires the commitment covers.

The second situation is data that several circuits have to read. The recursive proofs chapter noted that Ragu divides its verifier into many small circuits, and these circuits work on the same child proofs. Passing the shared data through public inputs would make every shared wire a term of the public input polynomial, which a verifier has to evaluate. It would also publish any wire that is meant to stay secret. It would be better for each circuit to read the data as if it were part of its own witness.

Both jobs need the same thing, which is a commitment to some of the wires of a trace, made before the rest of the trace exists.

Splitting a trace.

Commitments add, so we can divide a trace into parts that each get their own commitment. We write the trace as a stage polynomial plus a final trace, and more stages can be added the same way. The prover commits to the stage and to the final trace, and it does not need to commit to their sum, because the commitment to a sum is the sum of the commitments.

We need a small circuit to work with. Keep the secret value three from the arithmetization chapter, and start with the statement that its square plus one equals ten, which takes one multiplication. The trace uses the layout from the arithmetization chapter, including the system gate at index zero that the chapter on the NARK introduced. We take four gates, each with four wires, and we'll call the wires of a gate the first, second, third, and fourth wire.

We reserve gate one for the stage. The stage holds the secret input, which we'll call x, and also its square, which we'll call v, because the next circuits will use it. Sixteen entries need sixteen generators, so we add four points to the twelve from the chapter on the NARK.

Now read the two parts gate by gate to see which part supplies each wire. In gate zero, the stage puts the value ten on the first wire, and the final trace puts twenty-five there, along with the constant one on the fourth wire. In gate one, the stage holds three on the first wire and nine on the fourth wire, and the final trace holds nothing. Gate two belongs to the final trace, with three, three, and nine on its first three wires. Gate three is unused.

The stage commits to the point twenty-two, twenty-four, which we'll call the stage commitment, and it adds to the final trace's commitment to give the whole trace's commitment.

Gate two is the multiplication of x by itself to give v. Its two copies of x come from the wiring, through rows saying that each input wire of gate two equals the first wire of gate one. So the circuit's constraints reach across into the wires of the stage. This is what the split is for, because the final trace does not need its own copy of x.

Gates that hold free values.

The consolidated equation from the arithmetization chapter applies to the sum of the parts, and it includes a gate check for every gate. A stage's gate has to pass that check for whatever values the stage holds, because the prover commits to the stage before anything else about the circuit is known.

The code's wire allocator achieves this with a particular shape. It writes each value to the first wire of a new gate, with the second and third wires set to zero, and it writes the next value to that gate's spare fourth wire. This is why a stage carries two values per gate. The two gate equations then read first wire times zero equals zero, and zero times fourth wire equals zero, which hold whatever the values are. So a stage's gates do no checking of their own. They are storage, and any meaning the values have comes from constraints in the circuits that read them.

The system gate needs separate care, and the reason has to do with how commitments travel. A commitment is passed on as its two coordinates. Later in this chapter those coordinates are written into wires of the other circuit, and the transcript absorbs them. The identity point of the curve has no such coordinates, so a commitment that comes out as the identity cannot be written into wires or absorbed, and the proof cannot continue past it.

A Pedersen commitment is the identity when every entry of its list is zero. A nonzero list can commit to the identity only if its entries form a relation among the generators, and the binding assumption says that nobody can find one. A complete trace is never all zero, because it holds the constant one at the system gate. A stage has no such wire, so an honest stage whose values all happen to be zero would commit to the identity.

One nonzero entry of the stage's own, on the first wire of the system gate, prevents this. If our stage held zero and zero with nothing at the system gate, every entry would be zero and the commitment would be the identity. Put the value ten on the first wire of gate zero, and the same stage commits to ten times one generator, which is the point five, two.

The prover does this for every stage, and that is the ten in gate zero of our stage. We'll call this entry alpha. Any nonzero constant would rule out the all-zero case, but the prover draws alpha at random, for a reason that shows up once stages are combined. The toy curve is too small for the binding assumption, but on a real curve the one entry alpha is enough.

Alpha is random, but it is not there for hiding, and the book's wiring chapter says so directly. As the chapter on the NARK showed, the proof later reveals evaluations of these polynomials, so one random entry does not keep the other entries secret. The same chapter recorded that the code commits without Pedersen blinding, which is why this chapter leaves out the blinding generator.

With those two points settled, the consolidated equation applies to the sum as it does to any trace. At one pair of challenges, both sides come to twenty-eight, and the two sides agree at all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of challenges. The final trace alone fails at one thousand seven hundred sixty-three of those pairs, which shows that the circuit reads its input from the stage polynomial and not from the final trace.

Overlapping the stage.

The split has a weakness. Adding polynomials adds their coefficients in every position, so nothing so far stops the final trace from putting a value in a slot reserved for the stage. To see what that buys a cheater, consider two equations over the field of forty-three elements. The square of a number plus one equals ten has the solutions three and forty. The square of a number plus one equals seventeen has the solutions four and thirty-nine, so three does not solve it.

A stage is meant to fix the value that a circuit reads, so the verifier takes the squaring circuit's statement to be about the x inside the stage commitment. Suppose the stage holds three, and a cheater wants the squaring circuit to accept the claim with seventeen about that same x. Three does not solve that equation, but a stray entry in the circuit's final trace can change what the circuit reads.

The prover leaves the stage commitment alone, so the stage still says three. Then it puts the value one on the first wire of gate one in the final trace, and its gate two now multiplies four by four to give sixteen. The sum holds four in that slot, and the circuit for the false claim accepts it at every challenge pair. The commitments still add up, so the stage commitment still holds three, while the circuit ran on four.

The curve cycles chapter saw the same effect on a shorter list. A commitment check cannot find the problem, because a commitment to a sum equals the sum of the commitments however the entries overlap. We need a check that looks at where a polynomial's nonzero entries sit.

The stage mask.

The requirement is linear in the polynomial's entries. A stage must be zero at every wire of every gate outside its reserved range, apart from the system gate. Call those the forbidden positions. Requiring many values to be zero is the task that a random combination handles. So we weight each forbidden entry by a distinct power of a random challenge, y, and ask for the total to be zero. If all the forbidden entries are zero, this holds for every y. If any of them is nonzero, the total is a nonzero polynomial in y of degree at most fifteen, which has few roots.

This combination can be written as a revdot product, and that matters because revdot products are the claims that Ragu's accumulators fold. The revdot product pairs each position with its mirror position, counted from the other end of the list, so we choose each entry's power of y to match its mirror. We define a public list that holds a power of y wherever the mirror position is forbidden, and zero everywhere else. Its revdot product with the stage gives exactly the combination we wanted. Ragu calls this public list the stage mask.

The mask is an ordinary wiring polynomial, for a circuit with an unusual shape. Take one constraint per forbidden wire, saying that the wire is zero. The arithmetization chapter places each coefficient at the wire's partner position and weights each row by a power of y. We are free to choose the order of the rows, and one order makes the mask cheap to evaluate.

Take the constraint that the first wire of gate two is zero. That wire sits at position five of the trace, so its coefficient goes to the partner position ten. The code's test version of this circuit numbers this constraint as row ten, so the row contributes a single term with the same exponent, ten, on both variables. Every other forbidden wire gives a term of the same kind, with one shared exponent.

Notice what is missing compared with the consolidated equation. A stage polynomial does not satisfy gates or the circuit's constraints on its own, so the claim has no dilation and none of the circuit's extra terms. It also has no public inputs, so its target is zero. The full claim is that the revdot product of the stage with its mask equals zero.

For our stage, which reserves gate one, the forbidden wires are the eight wires of gates two and three. The stage has nonzero entries at only three positions, and their partners are positions where the mask has zeros. So the product is zero, and it stays zero at all forty-three values of y.

The final trace has its own mask, which forbids gate one, and we can already apply it to the overlap attack. The stray entry sits at the partner of the ninth power, so its product with the final mask is y to the ninth power. At y equal to seven that is forty-two. It is zero only when y is zero, so forty-two of the forty-three values of y catch it.

Every mask product passes when y is zero. The zeroth power in the mask is the partner of the constant wire at the system gate, which no mask forbids. So the product never has a constant term, and it always vanishes at zero.

The cheater has more freedom, because it can also fill the other three forbidden wires of gate one in the hope of building roots. We tried all seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven choices, and none gives more than five nonzero roots out of the forty-two nonzero values of y. Only eighty-five of the choices also leave gate one satisfied, as the circuit needs, and none of those gives more than four.

The general bound says more. With four gates, the mask has degree at most fifteen in y, so a nonzero product vanishes at no more than fifteen of the forty-three values. The cheater that keeps gate one satisfied stays well below that bound, passing at y equal to zero and at its four nonzero roots, which is five in forty-three. On a field of about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, with a list of two to the thirteen entries, the same bound falls below two to the minus two hundred forty.

The book's description of the mask differs from the code here. The book first says that wires outside the reserved range should be zero, and then says the mask enforces that everything must be nonzero inside the range. The code, at the commit this site pins, forces wires to zero outside the range and says nothing about wires inside it. This site follows the code, and it reads the word nonzero in the book as a slip for zero outside.

A closed form for the mask.

So far we tested a mask only at y, as a list. The verifier from the chapter on the NARK does more than that. It evaluates the wiring polynomial at the random opening point as well as at y, and for an arbitrary circuit that evaluation was one of its two linear costs, because such a circuit has no structure to exploit. A mask does have structure, because it depends only on the product of its two variables, which we'll call t. That makes it far cheaper, and we can derive the form the code uses.

Start from all the powers of t up to the length of the list, and remove four powers for the system gate and four for each reserved gate. Within a gate, the partner powers pair off at a distance of half the length of the list, so their sums factor. The system gate's four powers become a product of two short terms. For the reserved gates, the first and fourth wires form one group and the second and third wires another, and each group becomes a short factor times a geometric sum over the reserved range.

The full sum and the system gate's term do not depend on the stage, and the code calls them the global mask. The remaining term is the notch for this stage. Each geometric sum has a closed form that takes a logarithmic number of multiplications, as in the chapter on the NARK.

A small run confirms the formula. With the opening point nine and y equal to seven, t is twenty. For our stage, the global term is eighteen and the notch is twenty-five, so the formula gives thirty-six modulo forty-three. Adding up the eight forbidden powers directly gives the same value. We checked the formula against the direct sum for every mask in this chapter, at all forty-two nonzero values of t.

The saving grows with the circuit. A circuit of two to the eleven gates and a stage of one hundred gates has a mask with seven thousand seven hundred eighty-eight nonzero coefficients. The closed form needs only a few powers of t and two geometric sums. The registry computes the shared global term once, and each mask contributes only its notch. The chapter on wiring and the registry covers the registry itself.

A mask for every polynomial.

Masks have to cover the final trace as well, because the overlap attack put its stray entry there. The final trace has the same kind of mask, with the reserved range running from the end of the last stage to the top of the trace. In the code, this final mask uses the same type as a stage's mask, with a larger count of gates.

Stages also stack. A stage names its parent and starts where the parent ends, and it takes half as many gates as it has values, rounded up. Later we add a second stage at gate two, so our circuits need four masks. The two stages may use only gates one and two respectively, the final trace after the first stage may use gates two and three, and the final trace after the second may use only gate three.

The system gate sits outside every mask, which is why each polynomial can have its own alpha there. A polynomial could also put values on the other three wires of the system gate, and that does no harm. The circuit's row for the constant wire pins the sum's fourth wire there to one. The gate equation that multiplies the third wire by the fourth then forces the third wire to zero, and that forces the product of the first two wires to be zero.

The stage values fill only reserved gates, and the builder writes zeros in those slots of the final trace, so that the sum places each value in one spot. A mask leaves all four wires of its reserved gates free, so a stage could in principle put values on the second and third wires. That gains nothing, because the gate equations on the sum still apply to that gate, and circuits read stage data only from the first and fourth wires.

Sharing one mask claim.

A recursive step has two children. A child does not check its own mask claims in the step that makes them. Each claim joins that child's accumulator, as the accumulation chapter described for claims in general, so a false stage leaves a false claim that the next step has to check. The parent therefore inherits two polynomials for the same stage, one from each child, and each still needs its mask claim checked.

These claims can be combined with a random weight, because the mask claim is linear in the polynomial. Multiply the left child's stage by the weight, add the right child's stage, and take the revdot product with the one mask. The book and the code call this weight z. We call it rho, to keep z for the dilation challenge of the consolidated equation.

The target stays zero, because mask claims have no public inputs that would need combining too. We tried this with our stage as the left child's, and a right child's stage holding five and twenty-five, with alpha equal to eight. The fold's product with the mask is zero at all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of rho and y, so the parent checks one mask claim in place of two.

Folding also explains why alpha is random. The prover commits to weighted combinations of stored commitments, as we'll see near the end of the chapter. A combination whose entries all vanish leaves the prover with the identity, a point it cannot pass on. Entries that anyone can predict, such as a stage that holds public data, make this possible.

Suppose both children carry the same public data in this stage, three and nine, and neither uses an alpha. The two stages are then equal. At rho equal to forty-two, which is minus one modulo forty-three, the fold has every entry zero, so its commitment is the identity.

Now give the left stage an alpha of ten and the right stage an alpha of eight. At rho equal to forty-two, the fold keeps forty-one on the first wire of gate zero, and no value of rho makes every entry vanish. Of the forty-three possible values of the right alpha, only one, equal to the left alpha of ten, would let the fold cancel at some rho.

A fixed constant in place of alpha would not help, because two equal stages would still cancel at the same rho. A random alpha works because a collision then also needs the two secret values of alpha to line up.

The code's documentation says that any wire slot whose value can be predicted can cancel in a linear combination of stages. It does not name a party who would build the cancelling stage, and we did not find one. In the example, two honest children reach the identity by accident at one value of rho, which is unlikely on a real field but not excluded. A cheater gains nothing by causing it, since the result is a proof that cannot continue. So we read the random alpha as protection for honest proofs rather than as a defence against an attack.

The folded claim still catches a bad stage. Put a stray eleven in gate three of the right stage. Among all the pairs of rho and y, the folded claim passes only at the forty-three pairs where y is zero. Two stray entries at one position, one per child, can cancel each other at a single value of rho, since their combination has degree one in rho.

The same step does not work for a circuit's own claim, because the trace appears twice in that equation. Substitute a weighted combination of two traces into the quadratic part, and three kinds of term come out. One carries the square of rho, one carries rho itself, and one carries neither. The middle term mixes the two traces, and it has nothing to match it on the target side.

Here is that failure on our circuit. Fold two honest traces of the squaring circuit, one with x equal to three and one with x equal to five, at rho equal to two. The two claims are both true, but the folded product is thirty while the folded target is twenty-four. The two sides agree at only two of the forty-three values of rho.

So circuit claims go through the error terms of the revdot folding chapter, and each one stays a separate entry going into that fold. Mask claims skip that cost, which the code's documentation lists as a property of gate-free claims. The code also folds across circuits. Any polynomials with the same reserved range can share a mask, so the final traces of all the circuits that end on the same last stage fold into one claim.

The book and the code order the weights differently. The book's combined claim puts the weight on the second polynomial. The code's fold gives the first polynomial the highest power of the weight, as its module documentation also shows. This site follows the code, and the two orders are equally sound.

Two circuits on one stage.

We can now do the second job, sharing data. Two circuits read the same stage by having the same stage polynomial inside their traces. The second circuit here is the cubing statement from the arithmetization chapter, that x cubed plus x plus five equals thirty-five, with x and v read from the stage. It has one gate checking that x times x equals v, and another gate computing v times x. Each circuit has its own final trace and its own wiring, and each consolidated equation holds at every pair of challenges.

The verifier holds three commitments and not four, because the stage commitment appears once and serves both traces. Neither statement mentions x in its public inputs, and yet the shared commitment ties the two statements to one value.

The difference shows when a prover tries the false claim from the overlap attack, where the squaring circuit asserts seventeen. The cubing equation has the single solution three, while the false squaring equation has the solutions four and thirty-nine. No value appears in both lists.

With separate traces, the prover can use three for the cubing circuit and four for the false squaring circuit, and each trace passes its own equation at every challenge pair. The verifier has no way to see that the inputs differ. With one shared stage, the prover has to pick one x, and no single value satisfies both equations. The overlap attack tried to break exactly this tie, and the final mask catches it.

A circuit does not have to read every stage below its own. The builder can skip a stage, which keeps the layout the same but leaves those wires out of the circuit's constraints. The polynomial then does not join the circuit's sum at all.

What a mask does not check.

A mask looks only at where entries sit, and it ignores their values. A stage whose wires are supposed to form a curve point or a bit passes its mask for any value. For example, a stage over the field of thirty-one elements that should hold a point of the toy curve passes its mask for all nine hundred sixty-one pairs of coordinates. Only forty-two of those pairs lie on the curve.

Constraints written while a stage is being built do not help, because the framework discards them. So some circuit that reads the stage has to state those constraints as its own. In our example, the cubing circuit ties the product x times x in its gate two to the stage's v, and that tie is what makes v the square of x. The squaring circuit never reads v, so it relies on the cubing circuit for that meaning.

The code makes this kind of reliance explicit. Loading a stage as enforced emits the checks in that circuit, and loading it as unenforced relies on some other circuit to do so. Since the circuits read the same stage polynomial, one reader's check protects them all. The chapter on recursion in Ragu names which circuit enforces each of Ragu's stages.

Stages across the cycle.

Now we turn to the first job, a challenge that depends on the stage. We do it in two parts. The first commits to a stage in a way the transcript can absorb, and the second uses the resulting challenge.

The curve cycles chapter explained the difficulty. The stage commitment is a point on the toy curve, so its coordinates lie in the field of thirty-one elements, and the sponge that derives challenges has wires in the field of forty-three elements. The fix was a nested commitment, and we can now say exactly what that object is. It is a commitment to a stage of the circuit over the field of thirty-one elements, and the book calls it a nested staged commitment. Its stage holds foreign coordinates as ordinary wires, so it follows every rule we've covered, including the layout, the alpha, and the mask.

The run takes four steps. First, commit to our stage on the main curve, which gives the point twenty-two, twenty-four. Second, place those two coordinates on the first and fourth wires of gate one in a stage of the other circuit, with an alpha of eight. Third, commit to that stage on the other curve, which gives the nested commitment. Fourth, absorb the nested commitment's coordinates into the sponge and squeeze out a challenge, which we'll call w. In our run, w comes out as eleven.

In this way the challenge depends on x and v through two commitments, and the sponge absorbed only two wires. The circuit over the field of thirty-one elements reads the coordinates as its own witness, so it can run the deferred group operations on them.

In our small version, that circuit uses the twelve generators of the other curve, which caps it at three gates. Its gate two is the first gate of adding the stage commitment to a fixed point. Holding the slope nineteen, it checks that nineteen times twenty is eight modulo thirty-one. Its consolidated equation holds at every pair of challenges, and both of its masks return zero at every y. The full addition needs two more gates, which this circuit has no room for, so we stop after the slope.

The mask gives input consistency across the cycle in the same way as before, so a prover cannot run this circuit on other coordinates than the ones the transcript absorbed. Suppose the prover adds seven to the reserved slot in the final trace of this circuit, so that its gates run on the coordinate twenty-nine while the nested commitment still holds twenty-two. Only y equal to zero hides the entry, and the same holds for all thirty nonzero amounts.

A challenge that depends on a stage.

Back on the side with forty-three elements, the challenge lets the prover commit to the next stage and finish the trace. Here we use w to compress two wires of the stage into one value, x plus w times v, which we'll call e. Two wires make a small example, and the same reduction applies however many wires the stage holds.

With w equal to eleven, three plus eleven times nine gives sixteen, modulo forty-three. The prover commits to a second stage, which holds e equal to sixteen on the first wire of gate two. On the fourth wire of that gate it holds a copy of v, which the combining circuit reads in place of the v in the first stage. The copy stands in for a situation that Ragu meets for real, and we'll come back to how the copy is tied to the original.

The final trace holds one gate, which multiplies w, eleven, by the copy of v, nine, to give thirteen. Besides the row for the constant wire, the wiring has four rows. Two of them make w and e public inputs. A third ties the gate's copy of v to the one in the second stage. The fourth states that e equals x plus w times v, reading x from the first stage. The full trace satisfies the consolidated equation at every pair of challenges.

The two public inputs have different reasons for being public. The verifier rederives w from the transcript, so making it public lets the verifier check that the circuit used the right challenge. The value e is public because it is what a later check would use.

The order of the commitments is what gives e its value as a check. The combination is a random linear combination, and such a combination catches a wrong wire only because the prover cannot predict the challenge when it fixes the wires. If the prover could choose the stage after seeing w, it could steer e to any value it liked. A check written on e would then accept wires that separate checks on x and v would reject.

To measure how much the commitment order limits the prover, we give it a target value for e, which stands for whatever value a later check would accept. The prover may change the stage, but v has to stay the square of x, because a reader such as the cubing circuit checks that relation. So a new stage means a new x with v equal to its square, and we let x range freely, as if no other statement fixed it.

Suppose the target is eight. With the stage fixed at three and nine, only one of the forty-three values of w gives that result, namely thirty-four. If the prover could see w equal to eleven before fixing the stage, it would solve x plus eleven times x squared equals eight. That equation has the solutions four and thirty-five, so it would reach the target for certain.

The commitment order prevents this, because the prover cannot see w first. Every new x changes the stage commitment, then the nested commitment, then w, so each try is a fresh draw. We ran that chain for every x. For two of the forty-three values, one of the two commitments is the identity, which cannot be absorbed, so we skip them.

Of the remaining forty-one values, one, x equal to fifteen, reaches the target. A search of about forty-three tries succeeds on a field of forty-three elements, which is the repeated-trials problem from the chapter on statements and challenges. The same search has no realistic chance on a field of two hundred fifty-five bits.

The book and the code differ on how each side encodes these inputs. The book gives two mirrored nested commitments, one for each circuit of the cycle, and treats them by symmetry. The code, at the commit this site pins, is not symmetric, because only the primary side has a transcript, and the secondary side squeezes nothing. The chapter on recursion in Ragu follows each value through the stages that carry it.

Linking stages with a bonding polynomial.

The second stage holds a copy of v on the fourth wire of gate two, and the combining circuit reads that copy. Nothing so far says the copy equals the v on the fourth wire of gate one, in the first stage. The mask of the second stage accepts any value there, because a mask checks only where entries sit. So a prover could compute e from one value of v and hand the combining circuit another.

The missing requirement is that those two wires are equal. A circuit with one gate could check it, but the requirement is only a linear equation between wires, so it needs no gates at all. The code provides for this case with a bonding polynomial. A bonding polynomial is the wiring polynomial of a circuit that has only constraints, with no gates and an empty instance, and its claim has the same shape as a mask claim.

A stage mask is the special case whose constraints each say that one wire is zero. The general case can compare stage wires with each other, and its claim applies to the sum of the stages involved. The revdot product of that sum with the bonding polynomial must be zero.

Recall from the chapter on the NARK that every circuit's row for the zeroth power of y is the constraint on the constant wire. So a circuit's wiring polynomial equals one when y is zero. A bonding polynomial is not allowed to reference the constant wire, so it has no such row, and it equals zero when y is zero. That is why its row numbering starts at one.

In our example, the two stages both hold nine on those wires. The only row says that the difference of the two wires is zero, and it sits at the first power of y. So the product is y times the difference, which is zero at all forty-three values of y. If the second stage holds twenty instead, the product at y equal to seven is nine, and only y equal to zero hides the difference.

Ragu meets the same need in the secondary circuit. That circuit receives the coordinates of primary commitments in several stages, the way our nested stage received the coordinates of the stage commitment. Each of those stages is committed at its own moment in the transcript, so that its commitment exists before the challenge that depends on it. A later computation wants all of those points gathered in one stage, in the order it uses them. So each point exists twice, and the two copies need the check that v needed above.

Apart from the masks, Ragu's one bonding polynomial does this. It comes from the nested loading circuit, which requires each point in the gathering stage to equal the same point in the stage that brought it in. The chapter on recursion in Ragu names these stages. Because the claim is linear, a grouped fold sums the stages within each child and combines the two children with rho.

Adding the stage commitments.

The curve cycles chapter recorded a difference that we can now settle. The book asks for two things about staged traces, that the parts do not overlap and that their commitments add up to the commitment to the overall trace. The masks handled the first. The second ties the trace that the circuit's claim is about to the stage commitments that the transcript absorbed. A prover who committed to one whole trace and to unrelated stages would fail it.

In the book's form, it works like this. In the circuit over thirty-one elements, the nested commitment to the stage and the commitment to the final trace are points on the other curve. So the next circuit, over forty-three elements, can add them with the three gates from the curve cycles chapter. In our run the sum matches the commitment to the whole trace.

The code reaches the same goal without a separate commitment to the whole trace. A proof stores a commitment per stage and none for the whole trace, so there is no second object to compare. Instead, the claim of each internal circuit is defined on the sum of its final trace and the stages it reads. The chapter on recursion in Ragu has a table of which circuit reads and enforces each stage.

These claims then fold into one polynomial, a weighted sum of the traces, by the method of the revdot folding chapter. Two parties then have to connect the folded polynomial to the stored commitments, and they do it in different ways.

The prover carries the folded claim forward, so it needs the commitment to the folded polynomial. Each trace is a sum of stored parts, so the folded polynomial is a weighted sum of stored parts. Since commitments add, its commitment is the same weighted sum of the stored commitments, and the prover computes it with one small multi-scalar multiplication.

The verifying circuit cannot follow that route, because the commitments' coordinates lie in the other field and adding them would need nested arithmetic. It works with values instead, which are elements of its own field. The point where it takes those values has to account for the dilation in the consolidated equation. Dilating a trace by z gives the same polynomial with its variable scaled by z. So the dilated trace's value at the opening point equals the trace's own value at the product of the opening point and z, as in the chapter on the NARK. The prover opens each stored polynomial and the folded polynomial at that one product point, and the circuit recomputes the folded value from the values of its parts.

Here is that run for our two circuits, with weights five and eight. The opening point nine times z equal to five gives the product point two. The stage belongs to both traces, so it takes the weight thirteen, while the two final traces take five and eight. The prover gets the folded commitment from three scalar multiplications, and a commitment to the full polynomial gives the same point.

The stage has value nineteen at the point two, and the two final traces have forty-two and twenty-one. So the verifying circuit computes five times the sum of nineteen and forty-two, plus eight times the sum of nineteen and twenty-one. That is twenty-three modulo forty-three, and it matches the opening of the folded polynomial at that point.

A polynomial identity checked at a random point is enough, as in the chapter on polynomial encoding, and the addition is linear, so it costs no gates. Evaluating at the shared point also means that a stage read by several circuits needs only one opening.

A cheater who breaks the tie between stage and claim gets caught here. Suppose the prover folds a squaring trace with stage input four, while the stage commitment in the proof still holds three. The polynomial it commits to as the folded polynomial then differs from the expected combination by a nonzero polynomial of degree fourteen. Of the forty-three opening points, two hide the difference.

The book checks the sum of the nested commitments in circuit, in the next circuit on the other field, while its chapter on the polynomial commitment scheme puts both kinds of check in the next secondary circuit. In the code we found no such addition of points.

In the code, the sum holds by definition of each claim, and the openings at the product point tie the stored commitments to the folded claim. The stage checks are revdot claims on both sides of the cycle, and the parent folds each with the rest of that side's claims. This reading is ours, and the site follows the code. The book's version remains a good way to understand the requirement, which is why this chapter and the curve cycles chapter show it.

Why cheating fails.

Each part of this chapter counted one attack, so here we list what the protection rests on and add one more attack. A prover can try to overlap the parts, and the stage and final masks catch that. It can try to give circuits different inputs, but they read the same stage polynomial, and the opening check ties the folded claim to the stored commitments. It can try to change a stage after seeing a challenge, but the commitment fixes the stage first, and through the nested commitment the stage fixes the challenge.

It can also try to supply a mask that suits its polynomial, but the registry fixes the mask for each stage, so it cannot select a different range. Masks for different ranges are not interchangeable. For example, our first stage fails the mask of the second stage, which forbids gate one. The product is seventeen at y equal to seven, and it vanishes at only three of the forty-three values of y.

The bonding polynomials also differ from circuit wiring in a way that blocks a substitution. A bonding polynomial equals zero when y is zero, and a circuit's wiring equals one there. So a verifier that fixes the constant term to one for a circuit claim cannot be satisfied by a bonding polynomial in its place.

As we saw when folding mask claims, none of these claims is checked in the step that makes it. A false stage leaves a false claim that the next step folds, and the decider rejects it at the end. The last step has no parent to do the checking, so the uncompressed verifier checks the root's own revdot claims, which include its mask claims with target zero.

The argument rests on a few assumptions, and it is informal. Nobody knows a relation among the generators of either curve, so the commitments bind. The transcript hash behaves as a random oracle, each challenge comes after the commitments it depends on, and the challenges y and z come after the stage polynomials they test. A mask's check is only as strong as the degree over the field size, which is negligible for real parameters.

Staging in Ragu.

Our small circuits match the structure of the real ones. The application's own circuit has a single trace with no stages, and the internal circuits are where staging happens. What follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it.

The code calls the primary circuit's side native and the secondary circuit's side nested, as the curve cycles chapter explained. The native side's stages form a tree, and the nested side's stages form one chain, and the chapter on recursion in Ragu names them. Each internal circuit's claim sums its own trace with the stages it reads, and each final mask covers all the circuits that end on one stage.

The prover randomizes the alpha of each ordinary stage, as the system gate required, and the last stage of the nested chain is the one exception. This stage holds the challenges that go to the secondary circuit, and the primary circuit already knows them. So the primary circuit checks the stage's commitment by recomputing it as a fixed combination of generators. A secret alpha would add a term that it could not recompute, so the code commits this stage with alpha equal to zero.

The entries of this challenge stage are hash outputs, so the all-zero case arises only with negligible probability. They are also public, which makes them predictable in the sense that mattered for cancellation, and we did not find where the code addresses cancellation for this stage.

Every stage mask and final mask is one claim across both children, with no dilation and target zero, and the saving is visible in the claim count. In the standard profile, the native side has thirty-three internal circuits, of which twenty-three are endoscaling steps, along with eleven stage masks and five final masks.

The count of revdot claims per fuse adds one raw claim per child, one application claim per child, one claim per child for each internal circuit, and one claim for each mask across both children. That gives eighty-six claims. Checking each mask once per child would cost sixteen more.

All of this applies to the Ragu revision this site pins. Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and its design is still changing. It pins a different Ragu revision and runs its steps against a mock of Ragu, so nothing at its pinned commit executes these stages.

Where this chapter leaves us.

We split a trace into stage polynomials and a final trace, each with its own commitment, so that some wires are fixed before the others exist. Stage values sit in gates that accept any values, and a random alpha keeps each stage's commitment and its combinations away from the identity point.

Adding parts can hide an overlap that no commitment check sees, so each part has a mask, a gate-free revdot claim that forbidden wires are zero. A mask has a closed form, and it folds across polynomials without error terms because its claim is linear with target zero.

Circuits share data by including the same stage in their sums, and one reading circuit has to enforce what the values mean. A nested staged commitment lets the transcript absorb a commitment's coordinates, and bonding polynomials compare copies of a value across stages. The book adds stage commitments to reach a trace commitment, while the code ties each claim's sum to the stored commitments by openings at a random point.

We now hold every part that the internal circuits use, namely folding, the registry, endoscalars, and stages. The next chapter assembles them into the recursive step that Ragu runs.
