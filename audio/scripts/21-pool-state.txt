Chapter twenty-one. Pool State and Anchors.

Chapter one ended its account of validator state with a promise. Validators forget old tachygrams, yet a spender still has to prove facts about them. So a proof needs some way to refer to history that validators no longer hold. This chapter builds that reference. Each stamp's tachygrams become a polynomial, the polynomial becomes one curve point, and the curve point goes into a hash chain. We call the current state of that chain the anchor.

We also cover the rule that consensus applies around this chain, which is the two-epoch window for duplicate tachygrams. Chapter twenty explains what a tachygram is and why a spend publishes two nullifiers, so here we treat tachygrams as opaque field elements. Chapter twenty-two then uses this chapter's objects to prove that a nullifier is absent from a whole epoch.

The status of these rules.

Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and this part of its design is still changing. Everything here describes the Tachyon commit this site pins, and that commit uses a different revision of Ragu from the one in the Ragu chapters. The accumulator ZIP is an empty outline, so no normative specification exists yet. Our sources are the book's anchor and tachygrams pages, its deep dive, and the Tachyon crate.

The crate implements the fold functions and the proof steps, but we did not find a validator in it. The window and the anchor lookup appear only in the book and in doc comments, and the crate's own comment calls its proof system the mock Ragu application. Later sections mark the places where the sources disagree.

Every description in this chapter of what a proof step or a code check does is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. That covers the refused commitments, the unmerge step, the seal step, and the two anchor span steps together with the lift.

What the pool state has to do.

Start from the Orchard design in chapter one. There an anchor is a Merkle root over note commitments, and a spender proves that a commitment sits at some leaf under a recent root. Tachyon cannot reuse this directly, for two reasons. Its published values include nullifiers as well as commitments, and spends have to prove absence as well as presence. A Merkle path over unsorted leaves does not provide absence. Tachyon also assigns notes no canonical position, since a stamp publishes an unordered set.

The replacement has to meet four requirements. First, a proof must be able to test whether a value belongs to a published batch, in either direction, without validators holding the batch. Second, a proof must be able to name a point in history with one short value, and validators must be able to check that the point is real. Third, that value must also fix which epoch the point belongs to, because each epoch has its own nullifiers. Fourth, a validator's work per stamp must stay small, or the savings from aggregation would vanish here.

The first requirement leads to a polynomial whose roots are the batch. The second and third lead to a hash chain that absorbs a short commitment to each polynomial along with an epoch number. The fourth explains why the chain absorbs a commitment the stamp already carries, instead of asking validators to rebuild anything larger. We take the polynomial first and then the chain.

The deep dive calls the first object the tachygram accumulator. We avoid that name, because the accumulator of chapter eleven is a different object. We say set commitment instead, as the code does.

Encoding a set as a polynomial.

A stamp publishes a batch of tachygrams. A stamp covering some number of actions in some number of bundles holds twice the actions plus the bundles. So a single-action autonome holds three tachygrams.

Chapter twenty already introduced the polynomial whose roots are a stamp's tachygrams, and showed that one evaluation tests presence or absence. The book calls it the set polynomial. It is the product, over all the stamp's tachygrams, of X minus that tachygram. Tachygrams are elements of the base field of Pallas, and this polynomial has coefficients in the same field.

This chapter works in the field of forty-three elements, because the next sections commit to the polynomial on the toy curve of chapter six, whose scalar field has forty-three elements. Small values stand in for real tachygrams, which are hash outputs. What is new here is how the polynomial behaves under reordering and repetition, since both matter once stamps are merged.

Take a stamp we'll call A, with the three tachygrams twenty-two, twenty-four, and forty. Multiplying the first two factors and reducing modulo forty-three gives X squared, plus forty X, plus twelve. Multiplying that by X minus forty gives X cubed, plus three X, plus thirty-six.

As in chapter twenty, a zero means the value is in the set. This polynomial gives twelve at twenty and also at six, neither of which is in the set, and across all forty-three field elements it is zero at exactly the three tachygrams. All six orderings of the factors expand to the same coefficients, so the polynomial records the set and no order.

Two properties of this encoding matter later. It ignores order, which fits a stamp whose tachygrams carry no positions. It also holds a multiset rather than a set, because a repeated value becomes a repeated factor. A membership test, though, sees only whether the value is a root. For example, the multiset twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty-four has a double root at twenty-two, and evaluating there still gives zero, the same answer as a single occurrence.

Committing to the set.

The polynomial has one more coefficient than the stamp has tachygrams, so it does not fit in a hash chain that should take a short input. The Pedersen commitment of chapter six compresses it to one curve point. It has the added advantage that the proof system can open such a commitment at a point. The Tachyon code commits to the coefficients directly with Ragu's fixed generators on Vesta. It multiplies each coefficient by the generator for its position and adds the results.

Vesta's scalar field is the same field the tachygrams live in, so the coefficients act directly as scalars. On the toy curve, the four coefficients of stamp A's polynomial are thirty-six, three, zero, and one, from the constant term up. The zero coefficient contributes the identity, and the running sum ends at the point twenty-three, twenty-four. That point is the commitment to stamp A, with coordinates modulo thirty-one.

The code labels this commitment deterministic and untrapdoored, which means it has no blinding term. A blinding term would stop anyone else from reproducing the point, and validators have to reproduce it. The tachygrams are published in the stamp in any case, so a blind would hide nothing.

The code also refuses two commitments that are valid curve points. The function that advances the hash chain refuses to absorb the identity point, and it also refuses the commitment to an empty set. On the toy curve, the empty set has the constant polynomial one, so its commitment is the first generator, the point one, two. That point differs from the identity, so the code needs a separate check for it.

The code's comments state these rejections without giving a reason, but we can see what each one excludes. Every real stamp covers at least one bundle, so by the count from earlier it holds at least one tachygram. An empty commitment can only come from a malformed stamp, and refusing it stops that stamp from adding a step that publishes nothing.

The identity point is the commitment to the list of all zero coefficients, since zero times any generator is the identity. No set polynomial has that list, because every set polynomial is monic, meaning its leading coefficient is one. A nonzero list that committed to the identity would be a discrete log relation among the generators, which chapter six assumes nobody can find. So, like the empty-set check, the identity check excludes only inputs that no honest stamp produces.

Combining and dividing sets.

Aggregators merge stamps, and chapter two showed the Stamp Merge step doing this at the level of headers. We can now see what the merge does to the polynomials. The union of two multisets is the product of their polynomials, because the roots of a product are the roots of its factors with multiplicities added.

Point addition cannot compute this. A Pedersen commitment is additive, so adding two commitments gives a commitment to the sum of the polynomials and not to their product. So the merge step witnesses all three polynomials, the two inputs and the claimed union. It tests that the union equals the product of the inputs at one challenge, derived after all three commitments are fixed. The three values come from opening the commitments with the argument of chapter seven, so each is bound to the polynomial behind its commitment.

Here is an example. Stamp B has the tachygrams twenty, twenty-three, and forty-one. Its union with stamp A has degree six and seven coefficients, so the example adds three generators to our original four. At the challenge eleven, the union evaluates to twenty-seven. Stamp A's polynomial gives twenty-four there and stamp B's gives twenty-eight, and their product modulo forty-three is also twenty-seven, so the two sides agree.

The order of events is what gives the test its force. If the claimed union is wrong, its difference from the true product is a nonzero polynomial of limited degree. Then Schwartz-Zippel from chapter three bounds the number of challenges that pass. Here is that bound in a cheating attempt.

Suppose a prover claims a union with the root forty replaced by thirty-one. Then forty would look absent from the merged set, and thirty-one would look present. Trying every challenge, the false product passes at exactly five of the forty-three values, namely twenty, twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four, and forty-one.

The bound explains this count. Both the false union and the true product are monic of degree six, so their leading terms cancel, and the difference has degree at most five. The two products share every factor except one. So the difference is nine times the product of the five shared factors, and its roots are exactly the five passing challenges. This example therefore meets the bound of five.

The chance of five in forty-three is large here only because the field is tiny. On a field of about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, the same bound is negligible.

Division is the reverse. A quotient with zero remainder exists exactly when the divisor's multiset is contained in the dividend's. This is how the Stamp Unmerge step from chapter twenty-four removes a contributor. An aggregator needs that step when a block includes one of its inputs on its own, because publishing the aggregate would then repeat that input's tachygrams.

The step never divides inside the proof. It witnesses the quotient, and it proves that the dividend equals the quotient times the divisor with the same late-challenge product test as the union. In our example, dividing the union by stamp B's polynomial returns stamp A's polynomial with remainder zero, which removes stamp B. Dividing the union by X minus thirteen leaves the nonzero remainder three, because thirteen is not in the set.

The same product gives one polynomial for a whole epoch, which matters for exclusion proofs. Rather than query every stamp separately, the deep dive multiplies all the stamp polynomials of an epoch into one epoch polynomial. A nullifier is absent exactly when that polynomial is nonzero there. Soon we'll build a chain whose first epoch, epoch zero, holds stamps A and B and a third stamp C, with the tachygrams thirteen, nineteen, and twenty-one. Their product has degree nine. It gives zero at forty-one, a value published in stamp B, and thirty-seven at four, a value that does not appear in the epoch.

The degree of the epoch polynomial grows with the epoch's traffic, which is why chapter twenty-two splits it into buckets. An empty epoch has the polynomial one, which has no roots.

Checking a commitment against the list.

A stamp publishes both the list of tachygrams and the set commitment. Binding only tells us that the commitment opens to one polynomial, not that this polynomial has the published list as its roots. A prover who commits to a different polynomial could add a root or remove one, and the deep dive stresses this point. So someone has to compare the list and the commitment, and the sources describe two ways of doing it.

Suppose stamp A publishes the list twenty-two, twenty-four, forty, but carries a commitment to the polynomial with roots twenty-two, twenty-four, thirty-one. That commitment is the point sixteen, ten. In the first method, recomputation, the validator commits to the polynomial built from the list, gets twenty-three, twenty-four, and rejects the stamp because the points differ.

In the second method, the validator evaluates at a random point. It picks the point eleven after it sees the commitment, so the prover cannot choose the commitment to suit that point. Using the list, it computes the product of eleven minus each tachygram, which comes to twenty-four. The polynomial behind the carried commitment gives twenty-one at eleven, so an opening to twenty-four fails. The two polynomials agree at two of the forty-three challenges, which is the prover's chance of getting past this test.

The sources differ on how the commitment is checked. The deep dive uses the random point, and presents this as avoiding the group operations of a recomputation. The bundle page instead says full validation recomputes the commitment, and the code does the same. This site follows the code.

The proof itself is verified against the carried commitment and does not read the list. So in either design, a validator must compare the list and the commitment separately. Once this check has passed, later work can rely on the point alone, which is why the stamp carries it. Advancing the chain reads the carried point and never touches the tachygrams again.

Folding stamps into a hash chain.

We now need one short value that names a point in history. Consider what a validator holds after pruning, which is no tachygrams from old epochs. A proof made today has to convince it that some old set commitment was really published, and the validator cannot look that up.

A hash chain solves this with very little state. Each new state is a hash of the previous state and the new data. So one state fixes the whole sequence behind it, assuming the hash resists collisions. A validator needs to compare only the final value, while a prover can recompute any part of the sequence inside a proof by supplying the inputs. Poseidon suits this, because chapter three chose it for being cheap inside circuits.

Before writing the hash down, we separate two ways in which a stamp touches the chain. Chapter two listed an anchor among the three fields of a stamp's header. That carried anchor names a state that already existed, and the stamp's spends make their claims about the pool as it stood there. A wallet starts from the state it saw when it built its proofs, and a later section shows how it can move that anchor forward.

When a validator accepts the stamp, it does something different. It absorbs the stamp's set commitment into the chain and so produces a new state. A stamp therefore points back at an old state and also creates a new one, and the two values are usually different.

The anchor page calls each step a fold. This word has nothing to do with the folding of proofs in earlier chapters, since here it means one hash. A stamp in a block gives what we call a stamp absorption. It hashes four inputs under Poseidon with a domain tag, namely the current anchor, an epoch, and two limbs that encode the set commitment. The result is the new anchor. Each input is there for a reason, so we take them in turn.

The epoch is the epoch of the block containing the stamp, which a validator reads from the block height. Absorbing it at every step lets a proof learn the epoch of a chain segment from the segment itself, with no outside help. This is the epoch of the absorption. The stamp's carried anchor names an earlier state, which can lie in the preceding epoch.

The two limbs encode the set commitment. Poseidon works over the base field of Pallas, while the coordinates of a Vesta point lie in the other Pasta field, as chapter thirteen explained. A coordinate may not fit in Poseidon's field as an element, and the point also needs a bit for the sign of its second coordinate. So the code takes the thirty-two byte compressed encoding of the point and reads each half as a one hundred twenty-eight bit integer. Both integers lie far below the modulus, and the pair determines the point.

On the toy curve, coordinates are smaller than the hash field, so the toy does not reproduce the problem the limbs solve. We split a six-bit encoding into two three-bit limbs anyway, so that the toy fold takes the same inputs as the real one. Stamp A's commitment has first coordinate twenty-three and an even second coordinate, so it encodes as twenty-three. That splits into a low limb of seven and a high limb of two, and all forty-two affine toy points get different encodings.

The domain is a tag placed in the sponge's capacity, as with every Tachyon Poseidon hash. Here the string is Tachyon Anchor S T. We'll need it shortly to tell two kinds of fold apart. The deep dive writes this fold more loosely, and we use the code's form.

Real Poseidon values are numbers of about two hundred fifty-four bits, which are hard to follow. So the rest of this chapter uses a stand-in, which is S H A two fifty-six with the domain string as a prefix, reduced modulo forty-three. The stand-in copies only the structure of the chain. With only forty-three outputs it collides often, and we'll use that later to show what a collision would buy.

The chain starts from the genesis state twenty-nine, which is the entry anchor of epoch zero that the next section derives. Stamp A lands in epoch zero, so absorbing it gives the new anchor twenty-two. Absorbing B after A gives three, while absorbing A after B gives eighteen. So the anchor records the order of stamps, even though each stamp's own polynomial does not. A test in the code asserts the same dependence on order.

Claiming epoch one for stamp A would give twenty-seven instead of twenty-two, and that value appears nowhere in the chain we're about to build.

The deep dive also explains why the chain ticks per stamp rather than per block. A stamp already carries its commitment, so one hash is enough. A per-block fold would need a block-wide commitment. Validators would then have to rebuild it from every tachygram in the block with an expensive multi-scalar multiplication.

Crossing epoch boundaries.

Stamp folds alone do not mark where an epoch ends, and an epoch with no stamps would leave no trace at all. Later we'll need a point in the chain that a proof can recognize as a boundary, so the chain gets a second kind of fold. When the chain enters a new epoch, it applies a boundary digest. This is a Poseidon hash of the current anchor and the new epoch's number, under the domain Tachyon Anchor E P.

A boundary digest absorbs no tachygrams, and it absorbs the epoch being entered rather than the epoch being left. Since the two folds hash under different tags, an output of one matches an output of the other only through a Poseidon collision. So a proof step can confirm that an anchor is a boundary digest when the prover supplies its preimage.

Two positions around each boundary have names. The entry anchor of an epoch is the first anchor of that epoch. It is the boundary digest of the previous epoch's final anchor, except at epoch zero, where it is the boundary digest of zero and zero. The code sets that value as its default anchor. The final anchor of an epoch is the last anchor inside it, and it equals the entry anchor when the epoch published no stamp.

The single fold from one epoch's final anchor to the next epoch's entry anchor is called the crossing. The deep dive calls the entry anchor the epoch sentinel, and we use the anchor page's names, which the code follows.

Here is a complete run. The code's epochs have four thousand ninety-six blocks, and we use four so that several epochs fit in the example. The chain starts at twenty-nine, the entry anchor of epoch zero. Stamp A in block zero moves it to twenty-two. Block two holds two stamps, B and C, which move it to three and then to twenty-six, the final anchor of epoch zero.

Block four starts epoch one, so the crossing takes twenty-six to forty. Epoch one has no stamps, so forty is both its entry anchor and its final anchor. Block eight starts epoch two, and the crossing takes forty to eighteen. Stamps D and E, in blocks eight and eleven, then move the chain to seventeen and to seven. Block twelve starts epoch three, the crossing gives thirty-eight, and a stamp F in that block gives forty-one.

The empty epoch shows what the boundary digest provides. Epoch one has no stamps, yet it has its own value in the chain, and epoch two's entry anchor differs from it. As the deep dive puts it, even an empty epoch has two distinct boundaries. So each state in the chain belongs to one epoch, determined by the most recent boundary digest.

The run also puts numbers on the two roles of a stamp. Suppose stamp B's wallet built its proofs while twenty-two, the state after block zero, was the latest, so B carries the anchor twenty-two. When the validator absorbs B in block two, the chain moves from twenty-two to three, a new state. Stamp C in the same block could carry twenty-two as well, even though its absorption starts from three.

End-of-block anchors.

Every proof about the pool ends on an anchor that consensus has to look up, so the states that consensus accepts decide where a proof may end. A block may contain several stamps, so the chain passes through several states within one block. The anchor page says that consensus acknowledges only the state at the end of a block, and this site follows that rule, although the sources disagree.

The same page uses the word anchor in both a narrow and a broad sense, so we fix our terms here. We say anchor for every state of the chain, as the code does, and end-of-block anchor for a state that ends a block.

The anchor page gives no reason for the rule. Our own guess, which no source states, is that the inner states depend on how a miner orders the block's stamps, so a wallet cannot predict them. A block with no stamp leaves the state unchanged, unless it opens an epoch and so ends on the new entry anchor.

Proofs still handle inner states. A note's creation proof computes the state immediately after its creation stamp, which is probably not the end of a block. So that state has to be lifted to an end-of-block state before consensus sees it, and a later section explains lifting.

The sources disagree on which states consensus accepts, and the question is open. The anchor page says consensus may acknowledge only end-of-block states, with a comment in the source asking should or must. A code comment agrees that validators check anchors against a published per-block anchor sequence. The deep dive asks only that the target anchor occur in canonical chain history, and it allows the target to be a sentinel or an ordinary anchor. The test simulator indexes every state, including inner ones. This site follows the anchor page and the code comment.

The sources also place the crossing differently. The deep dive has the last block of the old epoch commit to the new entry anchor. The anchor page and the simulator apply the crossing at the start of the first block of the new epoch, before that block's stamps, and this site follows them.

The placement matters for our run. With the crossing at the start of the new epoch, block eight opens epoch two by crossing to the entry anchor eighteen. It then absorbs stamp D, so it ends on seventeen. The entry anchor eighteen is therefore never an end-of-block value, and we'll come back to what that means for proofs.

Covering a whole epoch.

An exclusion proof has to show that a nullifier is absent from all of an epoch's stamps. We already have the test, which evaluates the epoch polynomial at the nullifier. What we left open is how the proof knows that it multiplied the right stamps. The validator holds no list of an epoch's stamps, only the chain.

The chain supplies that link. In its simplest form, an epoch exclusion proof walks the chain itself. It starts at the epoch's entry anchor. For each stamp in turn, it recomputes the stamp fold from the stamp's set commitment, and it multiplies the polynomial behind that commitment into a running product.

Each multiplication is checked with the late-challenge product test, and the factor's value comes from opening the same commitment that the fold just absorbed. The anchor where the walk ends then certifies which commitments went into the product. By collision resistance, no other sequence of commitments leads to that anchor, and the product tests tie the running product to those commitments.

Chapter twenty-two splits the product into buckets to keep each opening small, but every bucket walks the chain in this way.

Here is the walk for epoch zero of our run, testing the value forty-one from stamp B. It starts at twenty-nine with a running product of one. Stamp A moves it to twenty-two, and the product at forty-one becomes twenty-two. Stamp B moves it to three, and the product drops to zero, because forty-one is one of B's tachygrams. Stamp C reaches twenty-six, and the product stays zero.

This walk has a weakness, and the boundary digest exists to fix it. Suppose a prover stops the walk early. The stamps it left out may contain the nullifier it claims is absent. Every state along the way is a genuine chain state. So checking the end of the walk against consensus would reveal nothing wrong, as long as that state is an end-of-block value.

Consensus can only confirm that an anchor exists, and it cannot confirm that a walk ending at that anchor was complete. So the walk has to end on something that only a complete walk can reach. The seal step, called Q R Bucket Seal, does this, and chapter twenty-two discusses it in full. It works by performing the crossing itself. It takes the last state of the walk, computes the boundary digest into the next epoch, and uses the result as the end of its range. For epoch zero, that crossing takes twenty-six to forty.

Suppose a prover runs this walk for epoch zero and stops after stamp A, at anchor twenty-two, leaving out B and C. Its product is then only stamp A's polynomial, which gives twenty-two at forty-one. That is nonzero, so the value forty-one from stamp B would look absent from epoch zero. The anchor twenty-two is a real end-of-block value from block zero, so a consensus check on it would pass.

Now perform the crossing from it. The result is fourteen, while the true entry anchor of epoch one is forty. The value fourteen appears nowhere in the chain, so nothing built on it can reach a published anchor. A test in the code seals a short run in exactly this way and asserts that the result is absent from the published chain.

The argument depends on the fact that the chain has only one boundary digest into each epoch. A different input to the crossing gives a different output unless the hash collides. Because our stand-in has only forty-three outputs, we can count the inputs that would rescue the short run. Three of the forty-three possible states cross to forty, namely the true final anchor twenty-six and the colliding values eight and thirty-seven. A collision like this is what a cheater would need, and on the real field finding one means breaking Poseidon.

Stopping early is one way to leave stamps out of the walk. Starting late is the mirror image, and the crossing at the end does not catch it. Suppose instead that a prover starts the walk for epoch zero at twenty-two, just after stamp A, and folds only B and C. Its product leaves out stamp A, and it gives three at twenty-four, so the value twenty-four from stamp A would look absent.

That walk passes through three and ends at twenty-six, which is the true final anchor. The crossing then gives forty, the true entry anchor of epoch one. The check at the end of the walk passes, so the defense has to come from the start.

So the seal checks the start of the walk as well as its end. The prover supplies a state that it claims is the final anchor of the previous epoch. The step then checks that the boundary digest of that state into the current epoch gives the walk's first anchor. This means the walk must begin at the output of a boundary digest into its own epoch.

The true entry anchor passes, because it is exactly such an output. A state inside the epoch is the output of a stamp fold, under the other domain tag. In the late start, the seal asks for a state whose boundary digest into epoch zero is twenty-two, and twenty-two came from a stamp fold, so any such state is a collision between the two domains. Our stand-in has only forty-three outputs, and two such inputs exist, namely fourteen and forty-two. For the genuine start twenty-nine, the only input is zero. On the real field, finding any such state for a stamp-fold output means breaking Poseidon across domains.

Chapter twenty-two builds the walk for an epoch from several pieces. The step that joins two pieces requires the first piece's end to equal the second piece's start. So only the first piece has a start of its own, and the seal checks that one.

The checks at both ends are why both ends of an exclusion proof's range are entry anchors, and the range excludes its upper end. An entry anchor belongs to the epoch it opens. So the range from one epoch's entry anchor up to the next epoch's entry anchor covers exactly that one epoch. The ranges of consecutive epochs meet on entry anchors, because each range ends where the next one begins.

The anchor page also warns about a related gap. A boundary digest into an epoch can be computed from any input at all. So the seal's check shows that the walk starts at some boundary digest into its epoch, but not that it starts at that epoch's entry anchor. A prover could invent an input and begin the walk from its boundary digest. The next section explains why this is acceptable.

Moving a proof along the chain.

Several proof steps take their starting anchor as a free witness. At first this looks like a hole, since a prover can put anything there. It works because the proof's obligations pass along the chain until they reach consensus.

Suppose a proof shows that a run of folds leads from one state to another, and consensus later confirms that the later state is in the chain. By collision resistance, the preimage of that state can only be its true predecessor, and the same argument applies to that predecessor in turn. So one consensus check at the far end pins every state, epoch, and set commitment along the way. A wrong witness does not break the proof, and it only leads to an anchor that consensus does not recognize.

For instance, a wallet could witness the predecessor twenty-three in place of twenty-two for stamp B. The fold then gives six, which is not in the chain. The proof runs without complaint, and the error surfaces only when consensus looks up the anchor.

The same logic covers the epoch witnessed in the Anchor Span Seed step. The epoch is an input to every stamp fold, as stamp A showed, so a span built with the wrong epoch holds anchors that are not on the published chain.

It also covers the invented boundary digest that the seal left open at its lower end. A walk that starts from an invented boundary digest begins at a state that is not on the chain. The stamps it folds may be genuine, but each fold hashes the previous state together with the stamp. So a wrong first state changes the next output, and that wrong output changes the one after it. Every state the walk produces is therefore off the chain unless some fold collides, and its crossing misses the published entry anchor of the next epoch.

Take epoch zero of our run, and suppose a prover invents the input four in place of the true zero. The seal accepts a walk that starts at forty-two. Folding the genuine stamps A, B, and C from there gives twenty, six, and thirty-five, and the crossing gives thirty-one. Folding only A and C, so that the value forty-one would look absent, gives twenty and zero, and the crossing gives five.

None of these values appears in the chain from our run. Both crossings miss the published entry anchor forty. We tried all forty-two invented inputs, and none of them makes the walk that skips B end at forty.

The general rule is that a wallet's proofs about a note form a sequence of proof steps ending at the anchor its stamp carries, which consensus looks up. An invented starting state anywhere in that sequence leads to an anchor the lookup does not find. A late start inside the epoch is different, because every state it passes through is genuine, and that is why the seal needed its domain check.

This structure lets a stamp's anchor move forward. A wallet may move its stamp closer to the chain's latest anchor before publishing, and aggregators need all their inputs at the same anchor. Moving is safe within an epoch because of what a stamp's claims depend on.

Every spend under a stamp proved its nullifiers absent from the epochs before the anchor's epoch, and it published the nullifier pair derived at the anchor's epoch. Neither claim refers to the anchor's position inside its epoch. So any later anchor in the same epoch supports the same claims.

Moving a stamp therefore needs only a proof that the old anchor lies on the path to the new one, inside one epoch, and an anchor span provides it. A span's header holds a start, an end, and a commitment to a members polynomial. The roots of the members polynomial are the anchors produced inside the span.

The steps reuse the tools from earlier in this chapter. Anchor Span Seed folds one stamp and commits to X minus the new anchor. Anchor Span Fuse requires the first span's end to equal the second span's start, and it proves that the combined members polynomial is the product of the two.

The lift then opens the members polynomial at the stamp's anchor. It multiplies that value by the stamp's anchor minus the span's start, and it requires the result to be zero. The first factor vanishes for a member and the second for the start, so the product vanishes exactly for anchors in the span. The polynomial does not record the order of its members, so the lift always outputs the span's end rather than a chosen member.

Take epoch two from our run. A span starts at the entry anchor eighteen and folds stamps D and E, producing the members seventeen and seven. A stamp anchored at eighteen passes because it is the span's start, and it lifts to seven. A stamp anchored at seventeen passes as a member and also lifts to seven. A stamp at seven passes too, and stays where it is.

A stamp anchored at twenty-six, the final anchor of epoch zero, gives a nonzero product of thirty-five and is rejected. Exactly three field elements pass.

A span cannot cross an epoch boundary, and this is deliberate. The seed step folds only with the stamp absorption, so no span contains a crossing. In our run, a span over epoch two ends at seven, and a span over the stamp in epoch three starts at thirty-eight. The fuse requires those two values to match, and they do not, because the crossing sits between them. The code tests this failure, with an error saying the spans do not share a vertex.

A stamp needs this restriction because each spend beneath it derived its nullifier pair at the anchor's epoch. If a lift could cross a boundary, the published pair would no longer match the epoch the anchor implies. To move a note into a later epoch, a wallet instead keeps a separate proof about the note itself, which chapter twenty-three calls a spendable. The steps of chapter twenty-three advance that proof across the boundary by proving the note's nullifier absent from the epoch it crosses. The wallet then builds a new stamp at the end.

An extra lift for entry anchors.

The next point is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. Every exclusion range ends on an entry anchor. With the code's placement of the crossing, an entry anchor is an end-of-block value only if the first block of its epoch carries no stamp. When that block does carry a stamp, a proof whose final anchor is an entry anchor needs one more lift to a later state in the same epoch before consensus accepts it.

The span example shows such a lift, from the entry anchor of epoch two to the span's end. In our run that entry anchor is not an end-of-block value, because block eight carries stamp D. We did not find this stated in the book. It follows from combining the simulator's placement with the end-of-block rule, and it is one reason to treat this rule as unsettled.

The deep dive's placement avoids this case, because there the last block of each epoch ends on the next epoch's entry anchor. The two placements disagree only at those last blocks. Block three, for example, ends on twenty-six under the code's placement and on forty under the deep dive's.

So under the code's placement, the entry anchor eighteen of epoch two is never an end-of-block value, and under the deep dive's, the final anchor seven never is. Under both, the state three just after stamp B never is, because stamp C follows it in the same block.

The consensus window.

We can now state what a validator does with a stamp and why it needs a window. The deep dive lists most of the steps. We rephrase them and add the duplicate check and the absorption. No source fixes the order across all six, and no validator code exists to check it against.

First, the validator looks up the stamp's anchor in the chain and obtains its epoch. Second, it requires that epoch to be the epoch of the current block or the one before it. Third, it checks the set commitment against the published tachygrams, and it checks the action digests in the same way. Fourth, it verifies the proof against the action commitment, the set commitment, and the anchor. Fifth, it checks each tachygram against the retained window, inserting each as it passes. Sixth, it absorbs the set commitment into the chain under the current block's epoch.

The fourth step establishes the note's history, but only up to a limit that needs care. For a note created before the anchor's epoch, the proof shows that the note's nullifiers were absent in every complete epoch before that epoch. It makes no claim about the anchor's epoch itself, even when the anchor sits late in that epoch.

A note created in the anchor's epoch needs no exclusion range at all, because no complete epoch lies between its creation and the anchor. In both cases nothing in the proof rules out a repeat inside the anchor's epoch. So the window of the fifth step must catch any such repeat, which means it must hold all of the anchor's epoch.

The tachygrams page explains why the anchor's position inside its epoch cannot narrow that scan, which is the point chapter twenty left to this chapter. One might hope to scan only the blocks after the anchor. But a stamp built at an early state of an epoch can be lifted to a later one. So the carried anchor tells a validator where the stamp ended up, and not where the wallet built it.

In the span example, a stamp that carries the span's end could have been built at the span's start, before stamps D and E were absorbed. Its spends then say nothing about D and E. So consensus compares the stamp's tachygrams against the whole of the anchor's epoch.

The second step lets a stamp anchored in one epoch land during the next. So all of the anchor's epoch must still be retained while the next epoch is current, and the window therefore spans two epochs. The same page gives a second reason for two epochs, the grace period behind the pair of nullifiers, which chapter twenty covered.

The window moves with the block being validated, so old values leave it at each boundary. In our run, the window during epochs zero and one holds stamps A, B, and C, which is nine tachygrams. In epoch two the window covers epochs one and two, so it holds only D and E, which is six. In epoch three it holds D, E, and F, nine again.

So the value nineteen from stamp C is rejected if a stamp republishes it in epoch one. It passes this check in epoch two, because epoch zero has left the window. Forgetting epoch zero at that point is safe for spends. A spend accepted in epoch two targets epoch one or two, so its exclusion proof already covers epoch zero.

Chapter twenty already enumerated the double-spend cases. It showed that every second spend either repeats a nullifier the window still holds or fails its exclusion proof. The deep dive's case analysis reaches the same conclusion, so we do not repeat it here.

Validators process candidates in a fixed order, checking and inserting each value before the next. So a candidate can conflict with the retained window, an earlier bundle in its block, or an earlier value in its own bundle. This also gives a useful property to the set polynomials. A set polynomial is a multiset encoding in general, but no accepted epoch can repeat a tachygram. So the code treats each epoch's product as square-free.

The second validator step also gives aggregators a deadline. A stamp anchored in one epoch cannot be published after the end of the next. Since lifts cannot cross a boundary, an aggregate in progress at that point has to be rebuilt.

What validators keep.

A validator keeps two things, and they grow differently. The first is the window, whose size chapter one already estimated, and which stops growing because it holds only two epochs. The second is the anchor chain, which the deep dive says validators keep unpruned, so that they can look up any anchor a stamp names. Each state is thirty-two bytes.

Every stamp costs the validator one fold, so aggregation always reduces the folds per block. Whether it also reduces storage depends on which states a validator keeps. Keeping every inner state, storage grows with the number of stamps, but with only end-of-block states it could be one state per block. So the following figures give an upper limit for storage. At the seventy-five second block target there are four hundred twenty thousand four hundred eighty blocks per year. Take the hypothetical one hundred transactions per second from chapter one, which is seven thousand five hundred transactions per block. These inputs are illustrative.

If every transaction is an autonome, the chain folds seven thousand five hundred times per block. Keeping every inner state, that is three billion one hundred fifty-three million six hundred thousand anchors per year, or one hundred point nine gigabytes. With one aggregate per block, the chain folds once per block, which comes to thirteen point five megabytes per year.

The sources we read do not say whether validators keep inner states. The end-of-block rule is itself unsettled, because under it some exclusion proofs need an extra lift, and if consensus accepts inner states after all, validators must keep them. We therefore treat the storage difference as open.

The compute difference is not open, since one fold costs one Poseidon hash. In the first case a validator computes one hash per transaction, against a single hash per block in the second. Either way the cost grows with the number of stamps rather than tachygrams.

The pruned tachygrams still have to exist somewhere, or nobody could build exclusion proofs. That is the data availability question that chapter one left open.

Why cheating fails.

Let's collect the attacks this chapter has considered. Each one assumes that the stamp's proof cannot be forged, which rests on the soundness of the proof system from earlier chapters.

Publishing a list that differs from the committed polynomial fails at recomputation, which rests on commitment binding and so on discrete log. A false merged product fails at the late challenge, which rests on Fiat-Shamir, Schwartz-Zippel, and commitment binding.

Claiming a stamp with an invented history fails at the lookup of the final anchor. Claiming the wrong epoch for a fold drops the state off the chain, because the epoch is hashed in. Sealing an exclusion proof before the end of an epoch fails because the crossing misses the published entry anchor. All three rest on Poseidon collision resistance.

Passing a stamp fold off as a crossing fails because the two folds use different domain tags. Starting an exclusion walk partway through an epoch fails at the seal's start check, together with the rule that pieces join end to start. Lifting a stamp across an epoch boundary fails because spans fold only under the stamp tag, and the fuse needs one span's end to equal the next span's start. All three rest on Poseidon collision resistance across domains.

Lifting a stamp from outside a span fails at the members check, which rests on commitment binding and Poseidon collision resistance. Publishing a stale stamp fails at the epoch rule, which assumes correct validators. Spending a note twice fails at the window and the exclusion proof together, which assumes that the note and epoch determine the nullifiers, as chapter twenty explained.

Most of these rest on the same mechanism. A proof may witness history freely, but a false history leads to an anchor that differs from the one consensus holds. Reaching the real anchor from a false history would take a Poseidon collision.

Where this chapter leaves us.

A stamp's tachygrams become the roots of a polynomial, and a deterministic Pedersen commitment shrinks it to one point that validators check against the published list. Union is multiplication and removal is division, checked at a late challenge, and an epoch's stamps multiply into one polynomial.

A Poseidon chain absorbs each stamp's commitment with its block's epoch, and boundary digests under a second tag join the epochs. Consensus acknowledges end-of-block states, though the sources disagree on the exact rule and on where the crossing sits.

An exclusion walk must start at a boundary digest and end on the crossing it computes, so it cannot skip stamps at either end. Proofs may witness anchors freely, because one consensus check at the far end pins everything earlier. Spans move stamps forward within an epoch, and the two-epoch window covers the whole of the anchor's epoch because the stamp may have been lifted.

This chapter showed the exclusion proof only in its simplest form, and the epoch polynomial is too large to open directly. The next chapter, Oblivious Sync, shows how an untrusted service proves absence over a whole epoch by splitting that polynomial into small buckets, and what the service learns while doing so.
