Chapter twenty. Pool State and Anchors.

Chapter one ended its account of validator state with a promise. Validators forget old tachygrams, yet a spender still has to prove facts about them. So a proof needs some way to refer to history that validators no longer hold. This chapter builds that reference. Each stamp's tachygrams become a polynomial, the polynomial becomes one curve point, and the curve point goes into a hash chain. We call the current state of that chain the anchor.

We also cover the rule that consensus applies around this chain, which is the two-epoch window for duplicate tachygrams. Chapter nineteen explains what a tachygram is and why a spend publishes two nullifiers, so here we treat tachygrams as opaque field elements. Chapter twenty-one then uses this chapter's objects to prove that a nullifier is absent from a whole epoch.

The status of these rules.

Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and this part of its design is still changing. Everything here describes the Tachyon commit this site pins, and that commit uses a different revision of Ragu from the one in the Ragu chapters. The accumulator ZIP is an empty outline, so no normative specification exists yet. Our sources are the book's anchor and tachygrams pages, its deep dive, and the Tachyon crate.

The crate implements the fold functions and the proof steps, but we did not find a validator in it. The window and the anchor lookup appear only in the book and in doc comments, and the crate's own comment calls its proof system the mock Ragu application. We'll mark the places where the sources disagree as we reach them.

What the pool state has to do.

Start from the Orchard design in chapter one. There an anchor is a Merkle root over note commitments, and a spender proves that a commitment sits at some leaf under a recent root. Tachyon cannot reuse this directly, for two reasons. Its published values include nullifiers as well as commitments, and spends have to prove absence as well as presence. A Merkle path over unsorted leaves does not provide absence. Tachyon also assigns notes no canonical position, since a stamp publishes an unordered set.

The replacement has to meet four requirements. First, a proof must be able to test whether a value belongs to a published batch, in either direction, without validators holding the batch. Second, a proof must be able to name a point in history with one short value, and validators must be able to check that the point is real. Third, that value must also fix which epoch the point belongs to, because each epoch has its own nullifiers. Fourth, a validator's work per stamp must stay small, or the savings from aggregation would vanish here.

The first requirement leads to a polynomial whose roots are the batch. The second and third lead to a hash chain that absorbs a short commitment to each polynomial along with an epoch number. The fourth explains why the chain absorbs a commitment the stamp already carries, instead of asking validators to rebuild anything larger. We take the polynomial first and then the chain.

The deep dive calls the first object the tachygram accumulator. We avoid that name, because the accumulator of chapter eleven is a different object. We say set commitment instead, as the code does.

Encoding a set as a polynomial.

A stamp publishes a batch of tachygrams. A stamp covering some actions in some bundles holds twice the actions plus the bundles, so a single-action autonome holds three.

We want to test membership by computing something short, and a polynomial offers this through its roots. A value is a root of a product of linear factors exactly when one factor vanishes at that value. So a stamp's set polynomial is the product, over all its tachygrams, of X minus that tachygram. Tachygrams are elements of the base field of Pallas, and the polynomial has coefficients in the same field. In the toy setting we use the field of forty-three elements. Real tachygrams are hash outputs, and the small values here stand in for them.

Take a stamp we'll call A, with the three tachygrams twenty-two, twenty-four, and forty. Multiplying the three factors one at a time and reducing modulo forty-three gives X cubed, plus three X, plus thirty-six.

Membership now becomes one evaluation, and it answers both ways. A zero means the value is in the set, and a nonzero result means it is not. This polynomial gives zero at twenty-two, twenty-four, and forty. It gives twelve at twenty and also twelve at six, neither of which is in the set. Running through all forty-three field elements, it is zero at exactly those three. All six orderings of the factors expand to the same coefficients, so the polynomial records the set and no order.

Two properties of this encoding matter later. It ignores order, which fits a stamp whose tachygrams carry no positions. It also holds a multiset rather than a set, because a repeated value becomes a repeated factor. A membership test sees only whether the value is a root. For example, the multiset twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty-four has a double root at twenty-two, and evaluating there still gives zero, the same answer as a single occurrence.

Committing to the set.

The polynomial has one more coefficient than the stamp has tachygrams, so it does not fit in a hash chain that should take a short input. The Pedersen commitment of chapter six compresses it to one curve point, and it has the added advantage that the proof system can open such a commitment at a point. The Tachyon code commits to the coefficients directly with Ragu's fixed generators on Vesta. It multiplies each coefficient by the generator for its position and adds the results.

Vesta's scalar field is the same field the tachygrams live in, so the coefficients act directly as scalars. On the toy curve, the four coefficients of stamp A's polynomial are thirty-six, three, zero, and one, and running the sum gives the point twenty-three, twenty-four. Those coordinates live modulo thirty-one, while the coefficients live modulo forty-three. We'll call this point the commitment to stamp A.

The code labels this commitment deterministic and untrapdoored, which means it has no blinding term. A blinding term would stop anyone else from reproducing the point, and validators have to reproduce it. The tachygrams are published in the stamp in any case, so a blind would hide nothing.

The commitment gets two rejections that the polynomial itself does not suggest. The function that advances the chain refuses to absorb the identity point, and it also refuses the commitment to an empty set. These are separate checks because the empty set has the constant polynomial one, so its commitment is just the first generator. That is a valid curve point, and it differs from the identity. A stamp with no tachygrams would advance the chain without publishing anything, and these errors exclude that case explicitly.

Combining and dividing sets.

Aggregators merge stamps, and chapter two showed the Stamp Merge step doing this at the level of headers. The union of two multisets is the product of their polynomials, because the roots of a product are the roots of its factors with multiplicities added.

Point addition cannot compute this. A Pedersen commitment is additive, so adding two commitments gives a commitment to the sum of the polynomials and not to their product. The merge step therefore has to establish the product some other way. It witnesses all three polynomials and tests that the claimed union equals the product of the two inputs at one challenge. That challenge comes from a hash, derived after all three commitments are fixed.

Here is an example. Stamp B has the tachygrams twenty, twenty-three, and forty-one. The union with stamp A is a polynomial of degree six, which has seven coefficients and needs three generators beyond our original four. At the challenge eleven, the union evaluates to twenty-seven. Stamp A's polynomial gives twenty-four there and stamp B's gives twenty-eight, and their product modulo forty-three is also twenty-seven, so the two sides agree.

The order of events is what gives the test its force. If the claimed union is wrong, its difference from the true product is a nonzero polynomial of limited degree. Then Schwartz-Zippel from chapter three bounds the number of challenges that pass.

Suppose a prover claims a union with the root forty replaced by thirty-one. Then forty would look absent from the merged set, and thirty-one would look present. Trying every challenge, this false product passes at exactly five of the forty-three values. The degree bound allows at most six, because the difference has degree at most six and each passing challenge is one of its roots. The chance of five in forty-three is large here only because the field is tiny. On a field of about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, the same bound is negligible.

Division is the reverse. A quotient with zero remainder exists exactly when the divisor's multiset is contained in the dividend's, and this is how the Stamp Unmerge step from chapter two removes a contributor. Dividing the union by stamp B's polynomial returns stamp A's polynomial with remainder zero. Dividing the union by X minus thirteen leaves the remainder three, because thirteen is not in the set.

The same product gives one polynomial for a whole epoch, which matters for exclusion proofs. Testing a nullifier against every stamp of an epoch would take one query per stamp. So the deep dive multiplies all the stamp polynomials of the epoch into one epoch polynomial, and a nullifier is absent exactly when that polynomial is nonzero there. Soon we'll build a chain whose first epoch, epoch zero, holds stamps A and B and a stamp C with the tachygrams thirteen, nineteen, and twenty-one. Their product has degree nine. It gives zero at forty-one, a value published in stamp B, and thirty-seven at four, a value that does not appear in the epoch.

The degree of the epoch polynomial grows with the epoch's traffic, which is why chapter twenty-one splits it into buckets. An empty epoch has the polynomial one, which has no roots.

Checking a commitment against the list.

A stamp publishes both the list of tachygrams and the set commitment. Binding only tells us that the commitment opens to one polynomial. It does not say that this polynomial has the published list as its roots. A prover who commits to a different polynomial could add a root or remove one, and the deep dive stresses this point. So someone has to compare the list and the commitment, and the sources describe two ways of doing it.

Suppose stamp A publishes the list twenty-two, twenty-four, forty, but carries a commitment to the polynomial with roots twenty-two, twenty-four, thirty-one. In the first method, recomputation, the validator builds the polynomial from the list and commits to it. It gets a different point from the carried one, so it rejects the stamp.

In the second method, the validator evaluates at a random point. Using the list, it computes the product of eleven minus each tachygram, which comes to twenty-four. The polynomial behind the carried commitment gives twenty-one at eleven, so an opening to twenty-four fails. The two polynomials agree at two of the forty-three challenges, which is the prover's chance of getting past this test.

The sources differ on which method applies. The deep dive has the verifier evaluate at a random point and check a commitment opening, and it presents this as avoiding the group operations of a recomputation. The bundle page instead says full validation recomputes the commitment from the list. The code does the same, by building the polynomial, committing, and comparing points. This site follows the code. The proof itself is verified against the carried commitment and does not read the list, so the comparison must happen separately in either design.

Once this check has passed, later work can rely on the point alone, and this is why the stamp carries it. Advancing the chain reads the carried point and never touches the tachygrams again.

Folding stamps into a hash chain.

We now need one short value that names a point in history. After pruning, a validator holds no tachygrams from old epochs. A proof made today has to convince it that some old set commitment was really published, and the validator cannot look that up.

A hash chain solves this with very little state. Each new state is a hash of the previous state and the new data, so one state fixes the whole sequence behind it, assuming the hash resists collisions. A validator needs to compare only the final value, while a prover can recompute any part of the sequence inside a proof by supplying the inputs. Poseidon suits this, because chapter three chose it for being cheap inside circuits.

The anchor page calls each step a fold. This word has nothing to do with the folding of proofs in earlier chapters, since here it means one hash. A stamp in a block gives what we call a stamp absorption. It hashes four inputs under Poseidon with a domain tag, namely the current anchor, the epoch, and two limbs that encode the set commitment. The result is the new anchor. Each input is there for a reason, so we take them in turn.

The epoch is the epoch of the block containing the stamp, which a validator reads from the block height. Absorbing it at every step lets a proof learn the epoch of a chain segment from the segment itself, with no outside help. Keep this separate from the anchor that the stamp itself carries, which names an earlier state and can lie in the preceding epoch.

The two limbs encode the set commitment. Poseidon works over the base field of Pallas, while the coordinates of a Vesta point lie in the other Pasta field, as chapter thirteen explained. A coordinate may not fit in Poseidon's field as an element, and the point also needs a bit for the sign of its second coordinate. So the code takes the thirty-two byte compressed encoding and reads each half as a one hundred twenty-eight bit integer. Both integers lie far below the modulus, and the pair determines the point.

The toy curve has the same shape, so we split a six-bit encoding into two three-bit limbs. Stamp A's commitment encodes as twenty-three, which splits into a low limb of seven and a high limb of two. All forty-two affine points of the toy curve get different encodings.

The domain is a tag placed in the sponge's capacity, as with every Tachyon Poseidon hash. Here the string is Tachyon Anchor S T. We'll need it shortly to tell two kinds of fold apart. The deep dive writes this fold more loosely, and we use the code's form.

Real Poseidon values are numbers of about two hundred fifty-four bits, which are hard to follow. So the rest of this chapter uses a stand-in, which is S H A two fifty-six with the domain string as a prefix, reduced modulo forty-three. The stand-in copies only the structure of the chain. With only forty-three outputs it collides often, and we'll use that later to show what a collision would buy.

The genesis state is twenty-nine. Stamp A lands in epoch zero, so absorbing it gives the new anchor twenty-two. Absorbing B after A gives three, while absorbing A after B gives eighteen, so the anchor records the order of stamps even though each stamp's own polynomial does not. Claiming epoch one for stamp A would give twenty-seven instead of twenty-two, and that value appears nowhere in the chain we're about to build.

The deep dive also explains why the chain ticks per stamp rather than per block. A stamp already carries its commitment, so one hash is enough. A per-block fold would need a block-wide commitment, and validators would have to rebuild it from every tachygram in the block with an expensive multi-scalar multiplication.

Crossing epoch boundaries.

Stamp folds alone do not mark where an epoch ends, and an epoch with no stamps would leave no trace at all. Later we'll need a point in the chain that a proof can recognize as a boundary, so the chain gets a second kind of fold. When the chain enters a new epoch, it applies a boundary digest. This is a Poseidon hash of the current anchor and the new epoch's number, under the domain Tachyon Anchor E P.

A boundary digest absorbs no tachygrams, and it absorbs the epoch being entered rather than the epoch being left. Since the two folds hash under different tags, an output of one matches an output of the other only through a Poseidon collision. So a proof can check that an anchor is a boundary digest by exhibiting its preimage.

Two positions around each boundary have names. The entry anchor of an epoch is the first anchor of that epoch. It is the boundary digest of the previous epoch's final anchor, except at epoch zero, where it is the boundary digest of zero and zero. The code sets that value as its default anchor. The final anchor of an epoch is the last anchor inside it, and it equals the entry anchor when the epoch published no stamp. The single fold from one epoch's final anchor to the next epoch's entry anchor is called the crossing. The deep dive calls the entry anchor the epoch sentinel, and we use the anchor page's names, which the code follows.

Here is a complete run. The code's epochs have four thousand ninety-six blocks, and we use four so that a few epochs fit in the example. The chain starts at twenty-nine, the entry anchor of epoch zero. Stamp A in block zero moves it to twenty-two. Block two holds two stamps, B and C, which move it to three and then to twenty-six, the final anchor of epoch zero.

Block four starts epoch one, so the crossing takes twenty-six to forty. Epoch one has no stamps, so forty is both its entry anchor and its final anchor. Block eight starts epoch two, and the crossing takes forty to eighteen. Stamps D and E, in blocks eight and eleven, then move the chain to seventeen and to seven. Block twelve starts epoch three, the crossing gives thirty-eight, and a stamp F in that block gives forty-one.

The empty epoch shows what the boundary digest provides. Epoch one has no stamps, yet it has its own value in the chain, and epoch two's entry anchor differs from it. As the deep dive puts it, even an empty epoch has two distinct boundaries. So each state in the chain belongs to one epoch, determined by the most recent boundary digest.

End-of-block anchors.

A block may contain several stamps, so the chain passes through several states within one block. The anchor page says that consensus acknowledges only the state at the end of a block. It gives no reason, and a plausible one is that the inner states depend on how a miner orders the block's stamps, so a wallet cannot predict them. The same page uses the word anchor in both a narrow and a broad sense. We say anchor for every state of the chain, as the code does, and end-of-block anchor for the narrow sense. A block with no stamp leaves the state unchanged unless the block starts a new epoch.

Proofs still handle inner states. A note's creation proof computes the state immediately after its creation stamp, which is probably not the end of a block. So the proof has to be lifted to an end-of-block state before consensus sees it, and we'll explain lifting shortly.

The sources disagree on which states consensus accepts, and the question is open. The anchor page says consensus may acknowledge only end-of-block states, with a comment in the source asking should or must. A code comment agrees that validators check anchors against a published per-block anchor sequence. The deep dive asks only that the target anchor occur in canonical chain history, and it allows the target to be a sentinel or an ordinary anchor. The test simulator indexes every state, including inner ones. This site follows the anchor page and the code comment.

The sources also place the crossing differently. The deep dive has the last block of the old epoch commit to the new entry anchor. The anchor page and the simulator apply the crossing at the start of the first block of the new epoch, before that block's stamps, and this site follows them.

That difference changes which states appear at the end of a block, even though the sequence of states is the same. With the code's placement, the final anchor of epoch two, seven, is acknowledged at block eleven. The entry anchor of epoch two, eighteen, is never an end-of-block value, because block eight carries a stamp. With the deep dive's placement, the reverse holds for this example. Eighteen is acknowledged at block seven, and seven is never acknowledged, because block eleven ends on the crossing. In both placements, the state three just after stamp B never appears, because stamp C follows it in the same block.

The next point is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. With the code's placement, an entry anchor is an end-of-block value only if the first block of its epoch carries no stamp. When that first block does carry a stamp, a lineage resting on an entry anchor would need one more lift to a later state in the same epoch before consensus accepts it. We did not find this stated in the book. It follows from combining the simulator's placement with the end-of-block rule, and it is one reason to treat this rule as unsettled.

Covering a whole epoch.

An exclusion proof has to show that a nullifier is absent from all of an epoch's stamps, and this creates a difficulty that the boundary digest exists to solve. Suppose someone builds evidence for a run of stamps and stops early. The stamps it left out may contain the nullifier it claims is absent. Every state along the way is a genuine chain state, so checking the end of the run against consensus would reveal nothing wrong, as long as that state is an end-of-block value.

Consensus can only confirm that an anchor exists, and it cannot confirm that a run ending at that anchor was complete. So the evidence has to end on something that only a complete run can reach. The step Q R Bucket Seal, which chapter twenty-one discusses in full, does this by performing the crossing itself. It takes the last state of the run, computes the boundary digest into the next epoch, and uses the result as the end of its range.

Suppose someone builds evidence for epoch zero that stops after stamp A, at anchor twenty-two, and omits B and C. That anchor is a real end-of-block value from block zero, so a consensus check on it would pass. Now perform the crossing from it. The result is fourteen, while the true entry anchor of epoch one is forty. The value fourteen appears nowhere in the chain, so nothing built on it can reach a published anchor. The code contains a test of exactly this situation. It seals a short run, gets a value the pool never published, and asserts that value's absence from the published chain.

The argument depends on the chain having only one boundary digest into each epoch. A different input to the crossing gives a different output unless the hash collides. Because our stand-in has only forty-three outputs, we can count the inputs that would rescue the short evidence. Three of the forty-three possible states land on forty, namely the true final anchor twenty-six and the colliding values eight and thirty-seven. A collision like this is what a cheater would need, and on the real field finding one means breaking Poseidon.

This is why both ends of an evidence range are entry anchors, and why the range excludes its upper end. An entry anchor belongs to the epoch it opens, so the range from one epoch's entry anchor up to the next epoch's entry anchor covers exactly that one epoch.

The anchor page also warns about a related gap. A boundary digest into an epoch can be computed from any input at all, so a proof that checks an anchor is a boundary digest has not shown that it is that epoch's entry anchor. The seal step has the same limitation at its lower end, where its input for the previous final anchor is an unchecked witness. The next part explains why this is acceptable.

Moving a proof along the chain.

Several proof steps take their starting anchor as a free witness, which at first looks like a hole, since a prover can put anything there. It works because the proof's obligations pass along the chain until they reach consensus.

Suppose a proof shows that a run of folds leads from one state to another, and consensus later confirms that the later state is in the chain. By collision resistance, the preimage of that state can only be its true predecessor, and the same argument applies to that predecessor in turn. So one consensus check at the far end pins every state, epoch, and set commitment along the way. A wrong witness does not break the proof, and it only leads to an anchor that consensus does not recognize. For instance, a wallet could witness the predecessor twenty-three in place of twenty-two for stamp B. The fold then gives six, which is not in the chain. The proof runs without complaint, and the error surfaces only when consensus looks up the anchor.

The same logic covers the epoch witnessed in the Anchor Span Seed step, where a span built with the wrong epoch holds anchors that are not on the published chain. It also covers the lower end of the seal, because the whole lineage must eventually pass through the note's spend anchor, and consensus checks that.

This structure lets a stamp's anchor move forward. A wallet may move its stamp closer to the chain's latest anchor before publishing, and aggregators need all their inputs at the same anchor. Moving a stamp requires a proof that the old anchor lies on the path to the new one, and an anchor span provides it. A span's header holds a start, an end, and a commitment to a members polynomial, whose roots are the anchors produced inside the span.

The steps reuse the tools from earlier in this chapter. Anchor Span Seed folds one stamp and commits to X minus the new anchor. Anchor Span Fuse requires the first span's end to equal the second span's start, and it proves that the combined members polynomial is the product of the two. The lift then evaluates the members polynomial at the stamp's anchor, multiplies by that anchor minus the span's start, and requires zero. The first factor vanishes for a member and the second for the start, so the product vanishes exactly for anchors in the span. The polynomial does not record the order of its members, so the lift always outputs the span's end rather than a chosen member.

Take epoch two from our run. A span starts at the entry anchor eighteen and folds stamps D and E, producing the members seventeen and seven. A stamp anchored at eighteen passes because it is the span's start, and it lifts to seven. A stamp anchored at seventeen passes as a member and also lifts to seven. A stamp at seven passes too, and stays where it is. A stamp anchored at twenty-six, the final anchor of epoch zero, gives a nonzero product of thirty-five and is rejected. Among all forty-three field elements, exactly three pass this check.

A span cannot cross an epoch boundary, and this is deliberate. The seed step folds only with the stamp hash, so no span contains a crossing. In our run, a span over epoch two ends at seven, and a span over the stamp in epoch three starts at thirty-eight. The fuse requires those two values to match, and they do not, because the crossing sits between them. The code tests this failure, with an error saying the spans do not share a vertex.

A stamp needs this restriction because each spend beneath it derived its nullifier pair at the anchor's epoch. If a lift could cross a boundary, the published pair would no longer match the epoch the anchor implies. To move a note into a later epoch, a wallet instead carries its spendable through the steps of chapter twenty-two, which prove the crossed nullifier absent, and it builds a new stamp at the end.

The consensus window.

We can now state what a validator does with a stamp and why it needs a window. The deep dive lists most of the steps, and we rephrase them, adding the duplicate check and the absorption. No source fixes the order across all six, and no validator code exists to check it against.

First, the validator looks up the stamp's anchor in the chain and obtains its epoch. Second, it requires that epoch to be the epoch of the current block or the one before it. Third, it checks the set commitment against the published tachygrams, and it checks the action digests in the same way. Fourth, it verifies the proof against the action commitment, the set commitment, and the anchor. Fifth, it checks each tachygram against the retained window, inserting each as it passes. Sixth, it absorbs the set commitment into the chain under the current block's epoch.

The fourth step establishes the note's history, but only up to a limit. For an older note, the proof shows that the note's nullifiers were absent in every complete epoch before the anchor's epoch. It makes no claim about the anchor's epoch itself, so the anchor does not mark the end of an exclusion range, even when it sits late in the epoch. The rest of the protection has to come from the second and fifth steps.

The window needs to span two epochs, and the tachygrams page gives two reasons. The first is that a stamp may have been lifted, so it could have been built at any earlier point in its anchor's epoch. Consensus must therefore compare against the whole of that epoch, and not only the blocks after the anchor.

The second reason concerns the pair of nullifiers from chapter one. A transaction built for one epoch may still be in the mempool when the next epoch begins, so a stamp anchored in one epoch stays valid through the next. That stamp publishes the next epoch's nullifier as the second of its pair. A later spend targeting the next epoch must publish that same value, and a validator catches the collision only if it still remembers the preceding epoch.

The window moves with the block being validated, so old values leave it at each boundary. In our run, the window during epochs zero and one holds stamps A, B, and C, nine tachygrams. In epoch two the window covers epochs one and two, so it holds only D and E, six tachygrams. In epoch three it holds D, E, and F, nine again. So the value nineteen from stamp C is rejected if a stamp republishes it in epoch one, and it passes this check in epoch two, because epoch zero has left the window. The exclusion proof keeps this safe for spends, as the next part shows.

Enumerating double spends.

We can check the deep dive's case analysis by enumeration, going through each pair of spends and naming the rule that stops the second one. The first spend targets epoch five, the note is older than every epoch involved, and each spend lands in its target epoch or the next. That gives twelve cases.

Suppose the first spend lands in epoch five. A second spend targeting epoch four or five shares at least one nullifier with it, and the duplicate window stops it. A second spend targeting epoch six and landing in epoch six is stopped twice, by the window and by the exclusion proof over epoch five. If it lands in epoch seven instead, the window has moved on, and the exclusion proof over epoch five stops it alone. A second spend targeting epoch seven shares no nullifier, and the exclusion proof over epoch five stops it wherever it lands.

Now suppose the first spend lands in epoch six. A second spend targeting epoch five or six shares a nullifier, and the window stops it. A second spend targeting epoch seven shares nothing, and the exclusion proof over epoch six stops it.

All twelve cases are stopped, seven by the window and six by the exclusion proof, with one stopped by both. The exclusion proof works in these cases because the first spend publishes the nullifier of the epoch that accepts it, whichever of its two acceptance epochs that is. That value lies inside the range a later target must prove absent.

The cases with no shared nullifier show why the window can be short. Once two spends are far enough apart to share nothing, the later one has to prove the earlier epoch clean, and it cannot. The cases stopped only by the window show why the window cannot be shorter, because the exclusion proof makes no claim about the target epoch.

Validators process candidates in a fixed order, checking and inserting each value before the next. So a candidate can conflict with the retained window, an earlier bundle in its block, or an earlier value in its own bundle. This also gives a useful property to the set polynomials. A set polynomial is a multiset encoding in general, but no accepted epoch can repeat a tachygram, so the code treats each epoch's product as square-free.

The second validator step also gives aggregators a deadline. A stamp anchored in one epoch cannot be published after the end of the next, and since lifts cannot cross a boundary, an aggregate in progress at that point has to be rebuilt.

What validators keep.

A validator keeps two things, and they grow differently. The first is the window, whose size chapter one already estimated, and which stops growing because it holds only two epochs. The second is the anchor chain, which the deep dive says validators keep unpruned, so that they can look up any anchor a stamp names. Each state is thirty-two bytes.

The chain's growth depends on how many stamps reach the chain, and this gives aggregation a second benefit. At the seventy-five second block target there are four hundred twenty thousand four hundred eighty blocks per year. Take the hypothetical one hundred transactions per second from chapter one, which is seven thousand five hundred transactions per block. These inputs are illustrative.

If every transaction is an autonome, the chain folds seven thousand five hundred times per block. That is three billion one hundred fifty-three million six hundred thousand anchors per year if every state is kept, or one hundred point nine gigabytes. With one aggregate per block, the chain folds once per block, which comes to thirteen point five megabytes per year.

The larger figure is an upper limit. If consensus acknowledges only end-of-block states, a validator could store one state per block in both cases, although it still has to compute every fold in the block. The sources we read do not say what validators store in that case, so we treat the storage format as unsettled. Either way, one fold costs one Poseidon hash, and the chain grows with the number of stamps rather than the number of tachygrams.

The pruned tachygrams still have to exist somewhere, or nobody could build exclusion proofs. That is the data availability question left open in chapter one.

Why cheating fails.

Let's collect the assumptions behind the attacks we've met, all of which also assume that the stamp's proof cannot be forged. Publishing a list that differs from the committed polynomial fails at recomputation, which needs only correct computation. A false merged product fails at the late challenge, which rests on Fiat-Shamir, Schwartz-Zippel, and commitment binding.

An invented history, a wrong epoch, a stamp fold passed off as a crossing, and a seal before the epoch's end all rest on Poseidon collision resistance. Lifting from outside a span rests on commitment binding and on anchors being distinct from one another. Lifting across a boundary rests on commitment binding. Rejecting a stale stamp assumes correct validators. Stopping a double spend assumes that the note and epoch determine the nullifiers, as chapter nineteen explained.

Most of these rest on the same mechanism. A proof may witness history freely, but a false history leads to an anchor that differs from the one consensus holds. Reaching the real anchor from a false history would take a Poseidon collision.

Where this chapter leaves us.

A stamp's tachygrams become the roots of a polynomial, a Pedersen commitment shrinks that polynomial to one point, and products and quotients combine sets at a late challenge. A Poseidon chain absorbs each commitment with its epoch, and boundary digests mark where epochs meet, so evidence for a whole epoch must end on the crossing it computes. Proofs may witness anchors freely, because one consensus check at the far end pins everything earlier. The two-epoch window and the exclusion proof together stop every double spend in our enumeration.

The exclusion proof has been a black box throughout this chapter, and the epoch polynomial is too large to open directly. The next chapter, Oblivious Sync, shows how an untrusted service proves absence over a whole epoch by splitting that polynomial into small buckets, and what the service learns while doing so.
