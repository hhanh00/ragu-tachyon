Chapter twenty-two. Oblivious Sync.

Chapter twenty showed that a spend must prove one nullifier absent from each closed epoch since the note's creation, and chapter twenty-one built the chain of anchors that those proofs refer to. This chapter opens the black box they left behind. It covers how an untrusted service can prove absence over a whole epoch without knowing which note the values belong to. It also covers how the wallet later ties that work to its note, and what the service learns along the way.

Two problems run through the chapter. The first half shows how the wallet can tie values that a stranger tested to its own nullifiers at the right epochs. The second half deals with size, since an epoch's polynomial is far too large to open directly. Near the end we take stock of what the service sees.

The status of these designs.

Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and this part of its design is still changing. Everything here describes the Tachyon repository at the commit this site pins, which uses a different revision of Ragu from the one in the Ragu chapters. The ZIP for the syncing service is an empty outline, so no request format, service interface, or privacy policy is specified, and we found none in the crate.

The crate does contain the proof steps, but it builds Ragu with its mock feature and calls its proof system the mock Ragu application, as chapter twenty recorded. These steps compute natively, and comments mark circuit work that remains, such as boolean constraints on the sides. So what we say a step enforces describes the design the steps declare, and we did not find real circuits enforcing it.

The work a spend needs.

Take the running note of chapter twenty, created in epoch three and spent with target epoch seven. Its nullifiers for epochs four, five, and six must each be tested for absence from the epoch of the same number. Its nullifier for epoch three, the creation epoch, is tested the same way, but in a bind of its own that we meet later. Either the wallet or a service can do that reading.

Its nullifiers for epochs seven and eight are different. The spend publishes them, and consensus checks them against its window.

Each test needs someone to read a whole epoch's tachygrams, which is exactly the work that wallets were supposed to stop doing. Handing that work to a service is safe only if the values it receives are useless later. Evolving nullifiers provide this, because the pair published at the spend is unrelated to the values tested for earlier epochs, assuming Poseidon behaves as a pseudorandom function. Chapter twenty explained why the wallet sends explicit values and no key.

We call this party the sync service, or the service. The book also says delegate and oblivious syncing service for the same role.

Two branches that share no secrets.

The service knows nothing about the note, so it cannot prove anything about the note. All it can prove is a statement about arbitrary field elements, saying that these values, at these epochs, do not appear in the pool. The code's name for such a proof is Arbitrary Unspent. Its doc comment says that the tested values remain arbitrary until the wallet attributes them to a derivation.

The wallet holds the other half. As chapter twenty showed, it proves that a range of nullifiers really derives from the master key of a note with a given commitment, which gives a Note Nullifiers proof. Neither proof says anything about the other, so a final wallet step has to connect them. The proof tree page calls that step Unspent Bind, and it is the only place where the two branches meet. Its output, Note Unspent, then feeds a step called Spendable Lift.

The service's output needs a name, because the rest of the chapter works with it. An Arbitrary Unspent header records three things. The first is a range of epochs that includes its start and excludes its end. The second is the pair of anchors on chapter twenty-one's chain where that range starts and stops.

The third is a commitment to a polynomial that the code calls elapsed, and that we'll call the service's polynomial. It is a product with exactly one factor for each epoch in the range, and each factor is built from the value tested at that epoch. We call such a header a segment. For a segment over epochs four to six, the service's polynomial has three factors, one for each of those epochs.

Later sections choose the form of each factor, and show how the service builds a segment one epoch at a time. Both branches build their commitments step by step, because neither fits in one proof step. So the binding has to compare two values that were accumulated separately, by different parties, over different ranges.

The wallet's range is wider than the service's, because it also covers epochs the service never saw. So the binding must test containment and not equality. What we need is a commitment that grows one epoch at a time and that supports a proof that one committed collection sits inside another.

The deep dive calls this a ranged nullifier commitment. It rules out the vector commitment schemes it surveyed, because they rely on RSA or bilinear groups. Neither fits Tachyon. The Pasta curves have no pairing, which is the bilinear map those schemes need. And RSA groups need a modulus whose factors nobody knows, which brings back the trusted setup that Tachyon avoids, as chapter six explained for KZG.

The sources name these objects differently. The nullifier page and the deep dive each use their own letters, and the code calls the three polynomials elapsed, nullifier sequence, and complement. We'll say the service's polynomial, the wallet's polynomial, and the complement. Our ranges exclude the upper end, as the code's do, while the nullifier page's include both ends.

Testing containment with polynomials.

Chapter twenty-one already has a tool for containment, since a polynomial divides another exactly when its roots form a sub-multiset, and a late random challenge checks the division. So the first thing to try is to give the service's values as roots. The service commits to the product of X minus each tested value. The wallet commits to the product of X minus each nullifier. The wallet then shows that its polynomial equals the service's polynomial times a witnessed quotient.

Our small examples use the field of forty-three elements, so we reduce the nullifiers modulo forty-three. That gives twenty-nine at epoch three, nine at epoch four, twelve at epoch five, and forty-one at epoch six.

This proves that the tested values are the note's, but it says nothing about which value was tested against which epoch. A later proof looks for each nullifier only in the epoch with the same number, as chapter twenty stressed, so that loss is fatal.

Here is the attack. Suppose an earlier spend of this note had target three and was accepted during epoch four, so epoch four contains twenty-nine and nine. Say epoch four publishes twenty-nine, nine, and seventeen, and epoch five publishes thirty-three, six, and thirty-eight. A value is absent from an epoch when that epoch's polynomial is nonzero there. A second spend must fail, and the honest test does fail, because nine is a root of epoch four's polynomial.

The cheater swaps the two values, testing twelve against epoch four and nine against epoch five. Both tests pass, since epoch four's polynomial gives forty at twelve and epoch five's gives twenty-four at nine. The tested multiset is still nine and twelve, so its polynomial did not change, and it divides the wallet's polynomial just as the honest one does. So each entry of the collection must carry its epoch as well as its value.

Putting the epoch into the factor.

We want a polynomial factor that encodes a pair of an epoch and a value, such that a product of factors determines which pairs went in. Polynomials over a field factor uniquely into irreducible pieces, up to multiplying the pieces by constants, so two goals follow. Each pair's factor should be irreducible, and the leftover constants should give a cheater no freedom.

The natural first attempt is a linear factor, the epoch plus one, times X, plus the value. The shift by one keeps the coefficient of X nonzero at epoch zero. A linear polynomial is always irreducible, so the first goal holds, and the second one fails.

For epochs four and five the linear factors are five X plus nine and six X plus twelve. Move a constant from one factor to the other, multiplying the first by twenty-seven and dividing the second by twenty-seven. The product stays the same, and the factors become six X plus twenty-eight and five X plus ten. That reads as value ten at epoch four and value twenty-eight at epoch five. Neither is the genuine nullifier, and the product is the same polynomial, so it passes every challenge.

To remove this freedom, Tachyon cubes the linear term and subtracts a constant. So the factor is the value plus the epoch term times X, all cubed, minus the constant, where the epoch term is the epoch plus one. We'll call this the indexed factor. The constant does two jobs, and we can derive both.

First, the constant removes the scaling freedom. Suppose a second pair gives the same factor up to some multiplier. Write the first pair's linear term as Y, and express the second pair's linear term through it as a scale times Y plus a shift. Expand the cube and compare coefficients, one power of Y at a time.

The Y squared term forces the shift to be zero, because the scale is nonzero. The constant term then says that the subtracted constant equals the multiplier times that same constant, so the multiplier must be one, as long as the constant is nonzero. The Y cubed term says that the scale cubed is one. So the two factors can agree only when the second pair is the first pair scaled by a cube root of unity.

Without the subtracted constant, the constant term would say nothing, and any multiplier would do, which is the same trick as in the linear case. The cube roots of unity that remain get their own section shortly.

Second, a non-cube makes each factor irreducible. A cubic over a field can only split by having a root. The indexed factor has a root exactly when the linear term at some point cubes to the constant, which requires the constant to be a cube. So if the constant is not a cube, the factor has no root in the field and is irreducible.

Non-cubes exist only when three divides the field size minus one, because otherwise cubing permutes the nonzero elements. Pasta satisfies this, and the code fixes the constant at two, which it calls the smallest cubic non-residue.

In the toy field, fourteen of the forty-two nonzero elements are cubes. Two is one of them, since twenty cubed is two here, so the toy field cannot use the code's constant. Its smallest non-cube is three, and we use that. The real field does have two as a non-cube, which we checked for the Pallas base field.

Take epoch four and value nine, so the epoch term is five, with the constant three. Expanding the cube gives thirty-nine X cubed, plus thirty X squared, plus eleven X, plus thirty-eight. Those four coefficients appear in the same order in the code.

With irreducible factors and no spare constants, a product determines its multiset of pairs, which the code calls an indexed multiset. We can test this on the toy field. Epochs three to six and all forty-three values give one hundred seventy-two pairs, and they produce one hundred seventy-two different factors. Multiplying any two of them, repeats allowed, gives fourteen thousand eight hundred seventy-eight products, and no two of those are equal. So rescaling cannot produce a second set of pairs here.

The non-cube requirement has a concrete purpose, because a factor that splits can divide a product without being one of its members. If the constant is a cube, then the linear term can cube to the constant, and multiplying that solution by a cube root of unity gives another. The toy field contains all three cube roots of unity, so every factor splits into three linear pieces. Roots borrowed from different members can then regroup into a factor that belongs to none of them.

Here is the failure on the toy field. Take our four pairs and build their product with the constant two, which is a cube here. Then try every candidate pair with an epoch from zero to fifteen, which are the sixteen epochs of one derivation batch in the code, and every value.

The product has eleven distinct roots, and nine false pairs have all three of their roots among them. Epoch zero with value thirty-five is one, whose roots are twenty-eight, forty, and forty-two. Its factor divides the product although it is not a member.

With the constant three, the product has no roots in the field at all. The only other pairs whose factors divide it turn out to give exactly the same polynomial as a member, for a reason that the next section explains.

The next explanation is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The deep dive's sketch states that this cubic form is irreducible and stays so under affine substitution, and that collisions need a cube root of unity. The rescaling attack on linear factors, the coefficient comparison, the attack with a cube for the constant, and the comparison with a square are this site's own explanation of why the form takes this shape.

A square might seem to do as well, and it is cheaper. Run the same comparison on the square of the linear term, minus a non-square constant. The Y term forces the shift to be zero, the constant term again forces the multiplier to be one, and the Y squared term says the scale squared is one.

So the only collision is a scale of minus one, which pairs an epoch term and value with their negatives. Keeping the epoch term below half the field size already rules that out. By this argument the square works too. The sources do not say why they chose the cube, so we leave that question open.

When two pairs share a factor.

The derivation leaves one escape. If we scale both the epoch term and the value by a cube root of unity other than one, the cube does not change, so the two pairs have the same factor. In the toy field the cube roots of unity are one, six, and thirty-six. So epoch zero with value nine and epoch five with value eleven give the same polynomial, because their epoch terms, one and six, are one scaling apart.

A collision like this would matter if a valid epoch term happened to equal a cube root of unity times another. The code states a bound that prevents it, which keeps the epoch term below the square root of one third of the field size, rounded down. The reason takes three steps.

Suppose both epoch terms lie between one and that bound, and one is a cube root of unity other than one times the other. Such a root satisfies the equation that its square, plus itself, plus one, equals zero. Multiplying by the square of the first epoch term shows that a sum of three terms is a multiple of the field size. Those terms are the second epoch term squared, the product of the two, and the first one squared.

As an ordinary integer, that sum is positive, and each of its three terms is below one third of the field size. So the sum is below the field size, and a positive integer below the field size cannot be a multiple of it. No such pair exists.

On the toy field the bound is only three, and our collision has one plus six plus thirty-six, which is forty-three, exactly the field size. For the Pallas base field the bound is about two to the one hundred twenty-six, while the code has only two to the twenty epochs. Our toy epochs three to six lie above the toy bound, so we checked them directly, and the earlier test found no collision among them.

The code states the bound without a derivation. So this three-step argument is reconstructed by this site, and the authors have not confirmed it.

A full run on the toy curve.

We can now run the whole binding. A wallet derives its nullifiers for epochs three to six, and it asks the service for a segment over epochs four to six. So the service tests the three values for those epochs. Real batches are larger, and we'll use the true size in the next section.

Epoch three is the note's creation epoch, and this bind does not cover it. A separate segment and a separate bind handle that epoch, as chapter twenty-three shows. We still include epoch three in the wallet's range here, because it shows what the complement is for. The complement holds the factors of derived epochs that this particular segment does not test, and the bind certifies nothing about them.

A spend is complete only when every epoch from creation up to the target has a factor in some segment that the wallet has bound to its note.

Over a delegated range, the nullifier page defines the three polynomials. The service's polynomial is the product of the indexed factors for the delegated epochs, each built from the value tested there. The wallet's polynomial is the product over all derived epochs, each built from that epoch's nullifier. The complement is the product over the derived epochs that were not delegated.

The claim is that the wallet's polynomial equals the complement times the service's polynomial. The honest service tests the values it was given, so each tested value is the nullifier for its epoch. Each factor has degree three, so the service's polynomial has degree nine, the complement has degree three, and the wallet's polynomial has degree twelve.

We built the service's polynomial in one go here, so that the identity is easy to see. In practice the service builds it one factor per epoch, through steps that come later in the chapter. Chapter twenty-three runs those steps end to end on the running note.

A header has room only for short values, so each party's header holds a commitment. Unspent Bind first checks that its witnessed polynomials match the commitments on its two input headers. Each polynomial commits on the toy curve as in chapter six, with extra generators of the same kind as in chapter twenty-one. A stand-in hash of the three commitments gives the challenge twenty-nine.

The step then checks the product at the challenge. The challenge absorbs all three commitments, and the proof system later verifies one opening per polynomial.

At twenty-nine, the service's three factors evaluate to thirteen, thirty-two, and five, so its polynomial gives sixteen. The wallet also has eight for the complement and forty-two for its own polynomial. The identity holds, since sixteen times eight is forty-two modulo forty-three.

The output is a Note Unspent header. It keeps the segment's anchors and epochs, and it carries the wallet's note commitment, so the anonymous claim now belongs to one note.

A dishonest service gains nothing, because its proof is about whatever it tested, and the wallet's check leaves a diverging proof useless. It can refuse to serve, and it cannot fool the wallet.

A cheating wallet is the more serious case. A wallet that wants to spend a note twice needs an absence proof for a nullifier that is already published, and nothing forces it to use an honest service. The service's steps run on public data, so the wallet can run them itself on any values it likes. It therefore controls the segment it brings to the bind, and it also chooses the complement.

The wallet cannot choose its own polynomial, though. That polynomial comes from its Note Nullifiers proof, which derives every factor from the note's master key, so the note fixes it. The argument against this wallet runs through the order of events, as in chapter three.

The three commitments are fixed before the challenge exists. If the wallet's polynomial differs from the segment's polynomial times the complement, their difference is a fixed nonzero polynomial. The check then passes only when the challenge lands on one of its roots.

We tried each kind of false segment in two ways. First we asked whether any complement at all, chosen freely, would make the identity hold exactly, and in every case none does. Then we fixed the honest complement and counted the challenges at which the check passes anyway.

Using a wrong value at epoch five passes two of the forty-three challenges, for each of the forty-two wrong values. The swap from earlier passes three. Testing epoch four twice, standing in for epoch five, passes three. Using a genuine nullifier of epoch seven, which the batch does not hold, in place of epoch five, also passes three.

The difference has degree at most twelve, so no attempt can pass more than twelve challenges, and the worst one here passes three. A wrong value passes so few because the difference factors into the complement and the other two indexed factors, which have no roots in the field, times the difference of two factors for epoch five.

The constants cancel in that last difference, which leaves a difference of two cubes. Because the toy field contains the cube roots of unity, it splits into three pieces. One is a nonzero constant, and each of the other two is linear with one root. So the difference has at most two roots, and the count of two in every case shows that they never coincide here. The proportion looks large only because the field has forty-three elements.

The repeated-epoch attempt needs one more fact. The wallet's polynomial has each epoch once, because Nullifier Fuse requires contiguous ranges, which keeps it squarefree. So a repeated factor in the segment's polynomial cannot divide it.

The real window size.

The code's derivation unit is wider than our toy range. Nullifier Derive always produces a batch of sixteen epochs that starts at a multiple of sixteen. The code calls this unit a window, but it is unrelated to the two-epoch consensus window, so like chapters twenty and twenty-three we say batch. It gets those sixteen nullifiers from four sponge calls of four outputs each, and Nullifier Fuse joins adjacent batches by multiplying their polynomials.

Our note needs epoch three covered, so its first batch runs from epoch zero to epoch fifteen. That batch includes epochs zero to two, which come before the note existed, and epochs seven to fifteen, which come after the target. Nobody tests those epochs. The code lets the batch run past the end of the tested segment, and the factors for both groups go into the complement, which stays with the wallet. Epoch three lands there too, for the reason we just gave.

The stand-in range of chapter twenty is the prime sixty-five thousand five hundred thirty-seven, and it cannot host this encoding. One less than that prime is not divisible by three, so cubing permutes that field and every element is a cube. So we work modulo the next prime with the right shape, sixty-five thousand five hundred thirty-nine. It is one more than a multiple of three, and its smallest non-cube is two. The nullifier values carry over unchanged.

The service tests epochs four, five, and six, which gives a polynomial of degree nine. The complement covers epochs zero to three and seven to fifteen, thirteen factors with degree thirty-nine, and the wallet's polynomial has all sixteen, with degree forty-eight. At the stand-in challenge the identity holds.

Suppose the service instead tests sixty-three thousand ninety-four at epoch five, one more than the genuine sixty-three thousand ninety-three. Trying all sixty-five thousand five hundred thirty-nine challenges then finds two that pass, against the degree bound of forty-eight. The batch is well inside the injectivity bound here, which is one hundred forty-seven.

Notice what Unspent Bind does not compare. The proof tree page says it compares no bounds between the segment and the derivation. It does not need to, because each factor carries its own epoch, and a segment holds exactly one factor for each epoch in its range.

If the segment covered an epoch outside the derivation, its factor for that epoch would have to divide the wallet's polynomial, which has no factor with that epoch to match it. So the identity can hold only when the segment's range lies inside the derivation's range, and coverage follows from the identity. Chapter twenty-three runs this same sixteen-epoch bind with the real steps.

The book and the code differ on the steps around the bind. In the deep dive, Unspent Bind requires the segment's range to sit inside the derivation's range, and the creation epoch goes through separate steps of its own, with other step names around them. In the proof tree page and the code at the commit this site pins, Unspent Bind compares no bounds beyond a defensive check that the segment is not empty. The creation epoch uses an ordinary bound segment, as we described. This site follows the code.

Why one opening per epoch needs buckets.

With the binding in place, we turn to what the service proves. From here on the toy numbers come from chapter twenty-one's epoch zero in the field of forty-three elements, and no longer from the running note.

A nullifier is absent from an epoch exactly when the epoch polynomial is nonzero there, so the direct approach opens the epoch polynomial at the value. The obstacle is its degree, because opening a commitment with Ragu's linear-time verifier costs time in proportion to it.

Take chapter one's hypothetical load of one hundred two-input, two-output transactions per second, with nine tachygrams each. An epoch of the code's four thousand ninety-six blocks lasts three hundred seven thousand two hundred seconds at seventy-five seconds per block. So it holds two hundred seventy-six million four hundred eighty thousand tachygrams, and the epoch polynomial has that degree.

The deep dive estimates more than sixteen minutes of verification at its own higher figure, and so it drops this approach. We reconcile its counts with ours a little later.

The fix is to split the epoch into buckets small enough to open, so that a query touches one. This only works if a value can find its own bucket without any list of the epoch's contents, and if nobody can leave a tachygram out of the bucket where it belongs. So the partition has to be a public rule computed from the value alone, and it has to be cheap to prove that a whole bucket obeys the rule.

One candidate rule would hash each member and use the hash to pick its bucket. Proving that a bucket obeys it would cost one hash per member inside a proof. The deep dive does not make this comparison, so the cost is our own count. Tachyon instead classifies values by quadratic residuosity. That rule is cheaper to prove, because one polynomial identity attests many members at once.

We'll call the constant used in this rule the non-residue, to keep it apart from the cube constant, because the sources use the same letter for both.

In the toy field, twenty-one of the forty-two nonzero elements are squares, which is half. The smallest non-residue is two, and multiplying by it swaps the two halves. For example, nine is a square, and eighteen is not. Three is not a square, and six is. So every nonzero value has a root either of itself or of twice itself, and never both. The code uses five, which is the smallest non-residue of the real field.

Classifying a value.

One split of the field gives two classes, and we need many. A discriminant is an offset that gives a different split, because a value is classified by whether the value plus the offset is a square. The rule sets the bit to one when the shifted value is a square or zero, which we call the residue side, and to zero otherwise.

A run of discriminants then gives each value a string of bits, its Q R profile. Tachyon uses consecutive offsets, counting up by one from a first discriminant that a builder picks.

Here is chapter twenty-one's epoch zero, with its stamps A, B, and C and nine tachygrams. The search for a readable small-field example chose a first discriminant of twenty-six, so the second is twenty-seven.

At twenty-six, the values twenty-two, twenty-four, twenty, thirteen, and nineteen fall on the non-residue side. The values forty, twenty-three, forty-one, and twenty-one fall on the residue side.

To show a value's side, a prover supplies a root. On the residue side its square is the shifted value, and on the other side its square is the non-residue times the shifted value. For twenty-two, the shifted value is five, and fifteen is the root, because fifteen squared is twice five modulo forty-three. The next section needs these roots.

One value needs special handling, which is the one where the shifted value is zero. At our discriminant that is seventeen, because seventeen plus twenty-six is forty-three. Zero squared is zero on either side, which means a root alone would let this value sit on either side.

The rule files it on the residue side, so a step that claims the non-residue side must also check that the shifted value is nonzero. Without that check, a cheater could move this value to whichever side suited it.

Proving a split.

A builder holds a set of tachygrams with a committed polynomial, and it wants to split them into a non-residue side and a residue side. A product of the two sides equal to the original shows that they partition the set, and that check works as in the binding. What remains is to show that each member sits on the side it claims, without one check per member.

Suppose every member of some side has a root of the kind we just described. Interpolate a polynomial we'll call U through the pairs of each member and its root. Then U squared, minus the side's multiplier times the sum of X and the discriminant, vanishes at every member.

Consensus forbids repeats within two epochs, so the side's polynomial is squarefree. A polynomial that vanishes at every root of a squarefree polynomial is divisible by it, so a quotient exists. That gives an identity, saying that U squared minus the multiplier times the shifted X equals the side's polynomial times the quotient.

The reverse also holds, and that is what gives the identity force. If the identity holds for some U and some quotient, then evaluating at any member shows that U at that member is a root of the kind that only the member's true side allows. One identity, checked at one random point, therefore covers all the members at once.

The code splits this into two steps. Q R Intake Split checks the product, and it opens the non-residue side at the negated discriminant to show that it is nonzero there, which handles the exceptional value. Q R Side Descend then extracts one side as a child, and it runs the identity on the other side, the sibling.

Attesting the sibling protects the child through two facts in turn. The product check says that every member of the set is a root of exactly one side. The sibling's identity says that every root of the sibling has the sibling's class. So a member of the child's class cannot be in the sibling, and it must therefore be in the child.

Take stamp A as an intake, with members twenty-two, twenty-four, and forty. At twenty-six its non-residue side holds twenty-two and twenty-four, and its residue side holds forty. To descend into the residue side, the prover attests the sibling, using the non-residue two as the multiplier.

The roots fifteen and ten determine a line for U. The quotient comes out as the constant seventeen, which is constant here because the sibling has degree two. A stand-in hash of the three commitments and the discriminant gives the challenge ten, where both sides of the identity equal eighteen.

Now we can see why cheating fails. An absence proof goes wrong only if a published value is missing from the bucket that its own profile selects. Suppose a cheater wants forty missing from the residue child, because later it will claim that forty is absent. It moves forty into the sibling, so the sibling's polynomial gains the root forty.

The identity then needs U at forty to square to twice the sum of forty and twenty-six, which is three. Three is not a square, so no such U exists. Trying all forty-three values for U at forty and taking the quotient by long division, none of them divide exactly, and the best attempt passes two of the forty-three challenges. The crate's tests reject a child short of a member in this way.

The attack the other way is harmless. If the cheater left a stray non-residue member in the residue child, then the sibling's identity would still hold, and the child would have one root too many. A stray can only make a later nonzero opening harder to pass.

Here the book and the code differ. The deep dive has one decomposition relation prove both sides pure, using four polynomials, and it says the output contains exactly the members of each class. The proof tree page and the code at the commit this site pins have a split and a separate descent for each child, each attesting only the sibling, and the child may carry a stray member. This site follows the code. The deep dive's exactly is stronger than what each descent enforces, and the weaker form is enough for absence.

The challenge in the descent step also absorbs the discriminant, through a curve point computed from it. The discriminant is chosen by the prover and is not one of the committed polynomials. So without this, a prover could solve the identity for the discriminant after seeing the challenge. Chapter three showed the same failure in general.

Routing an epoch into buckets.

Splitting one big polynomial in one step is impossible, since no step can hold it, so the partition has to grow out of small pieces. Each stamp or short run of stamps starts as a root intake, a bounded set of tachygrams at depth zero with a range of anchors. An intake can hold at most a fixed number of tachygrams, its capacity, because its polynomial has to fit in one step. Our toy network uses a capacity of four.

Routing proceeds in rounds, and each round does three things. First it splits every intake that is not yet final, using the next discriminant, into a side-zero child and a side-one child. Each child's profile is its parent's profile with one more bit.

Second, it merges two intakes when they have the same profile, their anchor ranges meet, and the union fits the capacity. We merge from left to right, in anchor order, and the merge is a polynomial product, as in chapter twenty-one. Third, an intake whose anchor range spans the whole epoch holds every tachygram of its profile, so it stops descending. It becomes a final bucket, ready for sealing.

The proof tree page splits only intakes over capacity. With a capacity as small as ours, no two root intakes would ever fit together under that rule. So our toy network splits every intake that is not final, a choice we made so that a nine-value epoch shows more than one round.

Round one uses the discriminant twenty-six. Stamp A splits into a zero intake holding twenty-two and twenty-four, and a one intake holding forty. Stamp B gives twenty on side zero and twenty-three and forty-one on side one. Stamp C gives thirteen and nineteen on side zero and twenty-one on side one.

The merge then works through each profile from left to right. Among the zero intakes, A and B meet and hold three values together, so they merge. Adding C would make five, which is over the capacity of four, so C stays apart. The three one intakes hold four values in all, so they merge into one intake covering A, B, and C.

That one intake spans all three stamps, so it becomes final. Profile zero still has two intakes, neither spanning the epoch, so only that branch goes through round two, with the discriminant twenty-seven. Both zero intakes split, the two zero zero pieces merge, and the two zero one pieces merge. Each result spans the epoch, which ends the network.

So the network ends with three buckets. Bucket one holds forty, twenty-three, forty-one, and twenty-one. Bucket zero zero holds twenty-four and nineteen, and bucket zero one holds twenty-two, twenty, and thirteen. Multiplying the three bucket polynomials gives back the epoch polynomial of chapter twenty-one, and no bucket has degree above four, where the epoch polynomial has degree nine.

The buckets have different depths, which the deep dive calls a jagged frontier. The final profiles form a prefix partition, which means that no final profile is a prefix of another, and every bit string starts with one of them. So every value matches exactly one final bucket.

A sealed bucket must cover every stamp of the epoch, or a tachygram of that profile could be missing from it. The finish rule claims this, and Q R Bucket Seal is the step that proves it. It uses the boundary digests of chapter twenty-one, which mark where one epoch ends and the next begins.

The step checks that the intake's anchor range starts on a boundary digest into its epoch. It also computes the boundary digest from the range's last anchor into the next epoch. Chapter twenty-one ran both attacks that these two checks stop, a walk that stops early and a walk that starts late, on this same epoch. The step cannot check the previous epoch's final anchor on its own, and chapter twenty-one explains how consensus pins it later.

For our epoch, the step takes zero as the previous final anchor and checks the start against the entry anchor twenty-nine. The last intake ended on the final anchor twenty-six, so the step computes the boundary digest forty. The bucket covers the range from twenty-nine up to forty, using the stand-in values of chapter twenty-one. An epoch with no stamps seals an empty bucket whose polynomial is one, so it costs no more than any other.

The depth stays modest at real scale. The deep dive sizes this for fifty thousand two-input, two-output transactions per second over a two-week epoch, at eight tachygrams per transaction. A tree of profiles with that many leaves needs depth at least twenty-six. Two to the twenty-five is thirty-three million five hundred fifty-four thousand four hundred thirty-two, which is fewer than the bucket count, and two to the twenty-six is enough. A balanced network reaches exactly that depth, well inside the thirty-two positions the header allows.

The choice of first discriminant does not affect soundness, because a proof certifies the buckets under whichever value the builder used. So each builder samples its first discriminant privately and keeps it unpublished until the epoch closes. A biased network hurts only its builder, and a wallet may use any valid network.

The evidence tree.

Sealing leaves one proof per final bucket. A builder would rather not store them all, nor make every wallet track which proof holds which bucket. So it folds them into a Merkle tree built with Poseidon, whose nodes have four children, and keeps one proof for the root.

Each leaf digests the bucket's header, namely its epoch, both anchors, first discriminant, depth, bits, and commitment. A builder short of four leaves repeats one, so with our three buckets the slots hold buckets one, zero zero, zero one, and zero one again.

The node over these four slots hashes to the stand-in root thirty-three thousand four hundred forty-four, and this one value stands for all the buckets. To serve a query for bucket zero one, a proof step takes the four children as witnesses and rehashes them to that root. It then checks the bucket's fields against slot two's digest.

In the code, a step called Evidence Tree Descend does this rehashing, walking four levels of the path per step. Once the walk reaches the leaf, Evidence Tree Open re-emits the leaf's bucket as a Q R Bucket header, the same header that the seal step emits. That header is what the absence test consumes, so the data flows from sealing, through the tree, to the test. This flow is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it.

Testing a value against its bucket.

Q R Unspent Init takes the bucket header that the tree opening produced, together with a value, and proves the value absent from that bucket's epoch. First it opens the bucket's polynomial at the value and requires a nonzero result.

Test the value four, which chapter twenty-one found absent from this epoch. Its shifted values are thirty and thirty-one, and these give it the sides zero and one. So its bucket is zero one, whose polynomial gives thirty-one at four, which is nonzero. One opening of a polynomial of degree three has replaced an opening of degree nine.

The step must also show that this bucket is the right one, because any other bucket would open nonzero too. Now test forty-one, which stamp B published. Its sides lead to bucket one, where the opening gives zero, so the nonzero check fails and no proof exists. Opened against the other buckets, forty-one gives thirty in zero zero and thirty-five in zero one, both nonzero. So without a profile check, a cheater could prove absence of a value that was published.

To force the match, the step witnesses the value's side and root at every one of thirty-two positions. At each position it computes the shifted value, using the first discriminant plus the position. It checks that the root squared equals the shifted value when the side bit is one, or the non-residue times the shifted value when the bit is zero. A zero bit also requires a nonzero shifted value.

Since the non-residue is not a square, only the value's true side has a root, so this is the root check from earlier. The step runs all thirty-two positions because a circuit's shape cannot depend on secret values, and buckets differ in depth.

The bucket's depth selects how many sides to compare, through a mask of thirty-two bits. The proof tree page gives the checks. The number of set mask bits must equal the depth. Twice the sum of the positions of the set bits must equal the depth times one less than the depth.

The step also requires each mask bit and each side to be a bit, zero or one, because the arguments hold only for bits. The code still marks the constraint on the sides as remaining work, as the status note recorded. With bits, the first two checks force the mask to be the first positions up to the depth. Among bit lists with that many ones, only the leading positions reach the smallest possible sum of positions.

A running integer then starts at zero. Where a mask bit is set, the step doubles it and adds the side bit, which appends one bit, and elsewhere it leaves the integer unchanged. The result must equal the bucket's bits, so the integer is built out of the value's own sides.

For depth two there are four hundred ninety-six ways to set two mask bits among thirty-two positions, and only the first two positions pass. For the value four, the first four sides are zero, one, zero, zero. The running integer goes through zero, one, one, one, ending at one, the integer that encodes zero one. The sides past the depth are computed and ignored.

The profile check compares the value's sides with the depth and bits in the bucket header, and that header came out of the evidence tree. So the check is only as good as the label in the leaf, which is why the leaf digests the profile.

Suppose the leaf did not bind the profile, and a builder wanted to prove the published value forty-one absent. It could offer the contents of bucket zero zero under the label one. The profile check would pass, because the label is forty-one's true profile, and the opening would give thirty, which is nonzero. With the profile in the leaf, that combination digests to a value matching none of the slots, so the path check rejects it.

The output of Q R Unspent Init is a one-epoch segment. Its elapsed polynomial holds the single indexed factor for that epoch and the tested value, which is what the full run multiplied up. The step must also tie that factor to the value it opened. It does so at one point, evaluating the committed polynomial at a challenge and comparing with the factor computed from the opened value.

A check at one point accepts any other value whose factor happens to agree with the right one at the challenge. A cheater who could choose that value after seeing the challenge would always find one.

Take the one-factor polynomial for value twelve at epoch five, so the epoch term is six, and let the challenge be five. Suppose the challenge did not depend on the value. A cheater could take the cube root of unity thirty-six and pick the twin value twenty. Then the inner terms differ by that root, their cubes agree, and both factors give thirty-nine at five.

The step would open the bucket at twenty, while the elapsed polynomial records the factor for twelve. That direction is the harmful one, because the bucket can report twenty absent while the segment vouches for twelve, which might be a published nullifier. So the challenge absorbs a curve point computed from the value as well as the elapsed commitment, and then depends on the value. The crate has a test for exactly this twin.

Composing segments and keeping evidence.

A segment for one epoch has to grow to cover many. Unspent Lift appends the next epoch in one step, testing a value against that epoch's bucket, and Unspent Fuse joins two segments on a shared boundary. Both check that the anchors and epochs meet, and that the new elapsed polynomial is the product of the old ones.

Q R Unspent Init and Unspent Lift each add exactly one factor, labelled with the epoch they test. Unspent Fuse only multiplies segments whose ranges meet without overlapping. So a segment holds one factor for each epoch in its range, which is the property the binding relied on.

Here is a segment over chapter twenty-one's epochs zero and one. Testing the value four against bucket zero one of epoch zero gives a segment over epoch zero, with anchors from twenty-nine up to forty. Epoch one published nothing, so its bucket is empty and any value passes the opening. The value tested there is an arbitrary stand-in, thirty-three. Lifting over that empty bucket gives a segment over epochs zero and one, with anchors from twenty-nine up to eighteen, and an elapsed polynomial of degree six.

That anchor range ends on a smaller number than it starts on, and that is not an error. Anchors are hash values, so a range of anchors runs in chain order, and its endpoints need not increase as numbers.

The segment stops at the entry anchor of the first epoch it does not cover, so the following epoch's nullifier is someone else's to test. For the running note, the segment over epochs four to six stops at the entry anchor of epoch seven, which consensus scans directly. That is the handover promised in chapter twenty.

The creation epoch's segment ends the same way, on the entry anchor of epoch four, where the segment for epochs four to six picks up. On the wallet's side, each bound segment extends the note's lineage, and chapter twenty-three defines that term and shows how the lineage moves across a segment.

A builder keeps the one proof for its tree's root and answers queries from it. The tree claims nothing about which buckets it holds. A builder that omits a bucket can fail to answer a query, and it cannot answer wrongly, since each bucket carries its own whole-epoch claim. All of this evidence is built from public data, so anyone, including a wallet, can check or replace a builder.

What the service learns.

Oblivious refers to what the service does not know. Let's separate what comes from the proof steps from what depends on policy.

The service sees each value it was given, with its epoch, because the code's witness needs both. It sees when a wallet asks and for which range, and the deep dive leaves timing and range selection to wallets. It does not learn the note commitment, the nullifier trapdoor, the master key, the nullifier key, or the note's value, because no step it runs touches them.

It cannot compute any nullifier beyond its list, because it has values and no key, assuming Poseidon acts as a pseudorandom function. It cannot tell from the values alone whether a request is real or a decoy, because nothing it receives is bound to a note. And it does not learn the eventual spend anchor, because the wallet lifts and spends alone.

The service can compare its list with everything published, so the wallet must never delegate a value that it will later publish. Alice's spend sits in the bundle of chapter twenty, which publishes nine values. A service holding her values for epochs four to six compares that list with the bundle and finds no match. If Alice had also delegated epoch seven, the service would hold fifty-five thousand one hundred thirteen and would see it published, which would tie her request to this bundle.

So a wallet that spends with a given target has to keep that target's nullifier and the next one out of every request, as chapter one noted. That means a wallet that delegates ahead of time must spend with a target above everything it has delegated.

Beyond that, the sources point to measures and do not settle them. A range starts near a note's creation, so a request can suggest the note's age, and a stream of requests can reveal when a wallet stops asking. The deep dive suggests standard ranges, decoys, and local continuation to give different requests the same shape. It also suggests different ranges to different services, so that none of them sees the whole sequence. None of this is specified.

The rule about delegating ahead of a spend, and the inferences about age and stopping, are reconstructed by this site from the steps at the commit it pins and from the deep dive's remark on timing. The authors have not confirmed them, and the sources do not spell them out.

All of this covers what the service's inputs reveal. What a proof itself reveals is a separate question, because chapter two records that Ragu's book and code do not yet establish zero knowledge.

The sources also differ on what the service holds. The nullifier analysis says in its threat model that the service can derive nullifiers for a finite range. Its attempts say in one place that the service receives a proof attesting them, and in another that it blindly trusts the list.

The deep dive and the code say that the service receives values and a range with no note-binding evidence. Q R Unspent Init takes the value as a free witness and consumes no proof from the wallet, although a doc comment still says that a delegate receives proven value windows. This site follows the code on what the service receives.

Why cheating fails.

Each defence here assumes that proofs cannot be forged, which rests on the proof system of the earlier chapters. The steps do not yet run as real circuits, so this describes the design. We've met each attack already, so here we collect what stops it and what that rests on.

A value that is not the note's nullifier fails because the segment's polynomial must divide the derivation's polynomial. That rests on Schwartz-Zippel, Fiat-Shamir, commitment binding, and irreducible factors. A skipped or repeated epoch fails the same way, because a segment has one factor per epoch and the wallet's polynomial is squarefree.

A value tested at the wrong epoch fails because each factor binds its epoch, which rests on a non-cube constant and epochs below the bound. A twin that matches only at the challenge fails because the challenge absorbs the value, which rests on Fiat-Shamir.

Hiding a tachygram from its bucket fails because the sibling's identity has no solution, which rests on Schwartz-Zippel and a true non-residue. Opening the wrong bucket fails because of the profile check and the profile in the leaf, which rests on Poseidon collision resistance. Stopping an epoch's evidence early fails because the boundary digest misses the chain, which also rests on Poseidon collision resistance.

A harmful first discriminant affects balance only, and wallets may use another network, assuming at least one honest network exists. Linking a request to a spend fails because the published pair lies outside the list. That assumes Poseidon acts as a pseudorandom function and the wallet follows the rule.

Where this chapter leaves us.

A spend needs one absence proof per closed epoch, and the wallet can hand the reading to a service by sending explicit values. The service proves a statement about arbitrary values, the wallet proves its derivation, and Unspent Bind joins the two branches through a divisibility check.

Plain roots lose the epoch and linear factors can be rescaled. So each pair of epoch and value becomes an irreducible cubic with a non-cube constant, which makes a product determine its pairs. The check runs at a challenge fixed after all three commitments. The complement holds the factors that this bind does not certify, such as the creation epoch, which gets a bind of its own.

An epoch's polynomial is too large to open, so builders route its tachygrams into buckets by a profile of quadratic-residue bits that any value can compute for itself. A polynomial identity attests all of one side's members at once, and each descent attests the sibling, so a child cannot lack a member of its own class.

Sealing makes each bucket cover the whole epoch, and an evidence tree lets a builder keep one proof for all of an epoch's buckets. An opening at a value with a matching profile proves absence for that epoch, and segments then compose across epochs.

The service sees values, epochs, and timing. It learns no field of the note as long as those values are never published, although a range and its timing can still hint at the note's age. The policies that would hide such patterns are unspecified at this commit.

All of this describes one commit of a proposal that has not activated, whose service ZIP is empty, and whose steps still run against a mock of Ragu.
