Chapter twelve. Split Accumulation.

The last chapter kept one claim alive across a chain of proofs and left the expensive check to a single decider at the end. That worked because the accumulator carried everything the next prover needed. It had a price, though, since every step had to run a complete inner product argument for the proof it verified and another for the merge.

This chapter removes those arguments from the recursive path. The idea comes from noticing that the recursive verifier never needs to read most of a proof. So we divide both the proof and the accumulator into a short part that the circuit reads and a long part that only a prover and a decider ever touch. No circuit reads the long part, so its size does not affect recursion, which is why a step can use the non-succinct proof of chapter nine, called a nark. Bunz, Chiesa, Lin, Mishra and Spooner formalized this under the name split accumulation, and the Ragu Book says it is the form Ragu implements.

Much of what follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The book gives the syntax and a simplified scheme for evaluation claims. The explicit formulas, the choice of what the toy circuit reads, and the numeric run are this site's own assembly of that scheme with chapters eight and nine. The site could read only the abstract of the paper, so the description of the scheme follows the book. The section on Ragu near the end compares all of this with the code.

A note on words. This chapter says that claims fold into an accumulator, because the book uses accumulation and folding interchangeably for what Ragu does. A fuse is still the proof-carrying data operation of chapter two, which contains folds. A merge still means the operation on Halo's claims from chapter eleven.

What atomic accumulation still costs.

The last chapter named its scheme atomic, because the accumulator alone lets anyone produce and check the next update. To see what that property costs, we count the group work in one recursive step. Take eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators, the size chapter nine calls four n, and a step that holds one earlier accumulator and one new proof.

The circuit batches the proof's claims, which takes six scalar multiplications. Five of them form the batched commitment, and one more scales the batched value by the inner product argument's extra point. The circuit then runs the proof's inner product argument up to the point where it hands off the folded generator, which takes twenty-eight. The merge takes twenty-nine more, for sixty-three in all.

Outside the circuit, the prover commits to the quotient combination with eight thousand one hundred ninety-two scalar multiplications. It also runs two inner product arguments at thirty-two thousand seven hundred sixty-four each, so it spends seventy-three thousand seven hundred twenty at every step. When the chain ends, the final verifier does the same work outside any circuit. It splits the last proof's argument for twenty-eight, checks a merge proof for twenty-nine, and decides the merged claim once with a multi-scalar multiplication of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two.

Most of this comes from the two inner product arguments, and both exist for the same reason. An accumulation verifier sits inside a circuit, so anything it reads becomes part of that circuit, and an atomic verifier reads the whole proof it is given. The proof therefore has to be short, which forces an argument at every step. The output of each argument is one more claim about the folded generator for the merge to handle.

If the verifier could leave most of a proof unread, the proof could stay long, and the arguments could go. Some other party would have to check the unread part eventually, and the decider is already such a party. So we need to find out which parts of a proof the recursive verifier has to read, and we can do that by inspecting the verifier of chapter nine.

Sorting the verifier's work.

Take chapter nine's verifier steps for openings and the revdot product, and sort them by the data each one touches. Two groups appear. The first group is cheap. It computes two geometric sums from the challenges, does a few field operations on instance values, and derives the quotient combination's value with one inverse per claim. Its largest job is five scalar multiplications on instance commitments and prover messages.

The second group is linear, and every step in it reads a witness list. It recomputes the wiring commitment and the commitments to a and b, with three multi-scalar multiplications that total twenty-four thousand five hundred seventy-six scalar multiplications. It computes the revdot product, recomputes the batched commitment from its list, and evaluates the batched polynomial. Each of these three costs eight thousand one hundred ninety-two operations.

So the cost lines up with what each step reads. Chapter nine drew this same division between the instance part and the witness part of the proof, though only as a matter of size, and now we can use it as a plan. The circuit runs the cheap group. The linear steps cannot run there, but they do not all go to the same place.

Evaluating the batched polynomial and recomputing its commitment go to this chapter's decider. Recomputing the commitments to a and b goes nowhere separately, because their claims enter the fold as ordinary inputs, so the decider's single multi-scalar multiplication covers them.

The wiring commitment splits in two. Its multi-scalar multiplication is handled in the same way as those for a and b, while the check that the wiring polynomial comes from the right circuit goes to chapter fifteen. The revdot product goes to chapter fourteen, which folds revdot claims. The book lists the same three subprotocols as the verifier's linear work.

So this chapter makes only the opening claims sound. It shows that every commitment opens to the value its proof states, and it leaves the revdot product and the wiring to those two later chapters.

Instances and witnesses.

Chapter three introduced instances and witnesses for a whole statement, and the same words apply to one evaluation claim. Its instance is the triple of commitment, point and value. Its witness is the polynomial behind the commitment, together with the blinding factor. The relation between them has two conditions. Committing to the polynomial with that blinding factor must give the commitment, and evaluating the polynomial at the point must give the value.

Take the first claim of chapter nine, about the polynomial a. Its instance is the commitment, the point two and the value nine, three things that anyone can hold. Its witness is a list of eight coefficients with the blinding factor seventeen. Checking the claim takes both parts, since the witness commits to that commitment and evaluates to nine at two.

The instance has a fixed size, so a circuit can read it at a fixed cost. The witness is as long as the generator list, and checking the first condition takes a multi-scalar multiplication of that size. The instance alone also says nothing conclusive, because any prover can write down a triple. It becomes meaningful only when somebody with the witness runs the check.

The word witness needs a warning. The book calls this a secret witness, held by the prover. In this chapter the word describes who needs the data, which is different from a promise that the data stays hidden. The section on Ragu returns to this point.

An accumulator with two parts.

Chapter eight turned a list of evaluation claims into a single triple, and a triple is again an instance of this relation. So the output of that reduction has the right shape to serve as an accumulator, if we keep the output polynomial with it. Following the book's definition, the accumulator's instance is a commitment, a point and a value, and its witness is a polynomial and a blinding factor.

The circuit reads the first part, and the second part travels beside the proof. This is what separates the scheme from chapter eleven. There the polynomial could be rebuilt from public challenges, whereas here the point and the value do not determine it.

For the point seventeen and the value twenty-three, trying all seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven polynomials with three coefficients finds one thousand eight hundred forty-nine that pass through this point. Each extra coefficient multiplies that count by forty-three, so with eight coefficients there are forty-three to the seventh of them. In chapter eleven, by contrast, the challenges fifteen and ten gave one polynomial and only one.

The commitment does not narrow this down for anyone other than the prover. It is consistent with a vast number of lists, and its blinding factor hides which one the prover used, so no amount of computation recovers the polynomial from the instance. Binding only stops a prover from switching to a second list once it has committed.

So a party who holds only the instance cannot run the next fold and cannot decide the accumulator. Whoever continues the computation has to receive the witness too. That is the price of the split, and we measure it later in the chapter.

The three algorithms.

A scheme of this kind has three algorithms. The book gives the first two in this form, and it names the third in its description of accumulation. The accumulation prover reads proofs and accumulators, both parts of each, and runs outside any circuit. It produces a new accumulator, both parts, and a proof of the update, which we call the accumulation proof.

The accumulation verifier reads the instances of the inputs, the new instance, and the accumulation proof, and it runs inside the next circuit. The decider reads one accumulator, both parts, and runs once, outside any circuit. Each of the last two answers accept or reject.

The accumulation verifier is the defining feature of the scheme, because a verifier that reads only instances has no need for a short witness. To turn chapter eight's reduction into these algorithms, we only have to decide what goes in the input list and which messages form the accumulation proof.

The input list.

A step holds an earlier accumulator and a new proof. The accumulator's instance is itself one evaluation claim. The proof contributes chapter nine's claims, about the polynomials a and b, the wiring polynomial s, and the statement polynomial k. That gives five claims about five polynomials, with the old accumulator first.

The book's list has two more entries for the constant wire, which our toy circuit does not use. The proof in chapter nine also carried its own batched triple. A recursive step has no need to batch the proof's claims separately and then fold the result, because it can place them directly in this one list.

The accumulation prover.

The prover follows the steps of chapter eight with this list. We keep that chapter's weights, so the first claim takes the highest power of alpha, and the quotient combination takes the highest power of beta. The five quotients take alpha to the fourth, alpha cubed, alpha squared, alpha, and one, in order.

After receiving alpha, the prover divides each polynomial, minus its claimed value, by the variable minus the claim's point. It combines these quotients with the powers of alpha, and it sends a blinded commitment to that combination. After receiving a fresh point, which we call u, it sends the five polynomials' values at u.

After receiving beta, the prover forms the new witness. It weights the quotient combination by beta to the fifth and the five polynomials by beta to the fourth down to one, and adds them up. The blinding factors combine with the same weights, where the wiring and statement polynomials have none.

The commitment and the five values make up the accumulation proof, which matches the book's. The book describes it as an internal value for the circuit's prover. So it goes into the next circuit's witness and is not an output of the recursive step.

The accumulation verifier.

The verifier derives what the new instance has to be, using only the old instances and the accumulation proof, and its arithmetic never mentions a coefficient. For each claim, it subtracts the claimed value from the reported value and divides by u minus the claim's point. Weighting those ratios by the powers of alpha gives the quotient combination's value at u.

Weighting the commitments by the powers of beta gives the new commitment. Weighting the values the same way gives the new value. The verifier accepts if the claimed new instance equals this commitment, the point u and this value, where alpha, u and beta are the challenges the transcript produced.

This takes one scalar multiplication per commitment and one inverse per claim, however large the polynomials are. No list and no inner product argument appears in the calculation.

The two sides agree for an honest prover. Commit to the new witness and apply linearity, and the commitment comes apart into the same weighted sum of the quotient commitment and the input commitments, which is the verifier's commitment.

For the value, substitute u into the definition of the new polynomial. The prover sent each polynomial's value at u, so only the quotient combination's value remains to be written in terms the verifier knows. Chapter eight showed that each quotient's value at u is the reported value minus the claimed value, divided by u minus the claim's point. Substituting this gives exactly the verifier's value.

The decider.

The decider checks the relation on the accumulator, so it computes one multi-scalar multiplication and one evaluation. It accepts if the witness commits to the commitment and evaluates to the value at the point. Chapter nine's verifier performed this same check at the end, when it recomputed the batched commitment from a revealed polynomial.

That chapter said the reveal fits how the proof will be used, and we can now say how. The revealed polynomial is the accumulator's witness, and the reveal gives the decider its input.

In order, then, the exchange runs as follows. Both sides start with the old instance and the proof's claims. The verifier sends alpha, and the prover answers with the quotient commitment. The verifier sends u, and the prover answers with the five values. The verifier sends beta, then computes the new instance, while the prover computes the new witness.

A run on the toy curve.

We now run a chain whose step function cubes its input and adds the input and five, starting from three, with chapter nine's eight generators. All challenges are picked by hand, and all arithmetic takes place on one curve.

Three simplifications apply. Each proof covers only its step's circuit, because a circuit that also held the accumulation verifier would be far too large for the toy curve. We also ignore the fact that a circuit modulo forty-three cannot do arithmetic on points whose coordinates are modulo thirty-one, which the next chapter addresses.

Finally, in a real chain the circuit that proves one step is the one that folds the previous step's proof, because a proof does not exist until its step is finished. We fold each proof right after making it instead. This gives the same arithmetic and keeps each fold next to its proof.

The first fold has no earlier accumulator, so its list holds only the first proof's four claims. Running it with alpha eleven, the point seventeen and beta twenty-three repeats chapter nine's batching with no changes. The first accumulator's instance has the point seventeen and the value twenty-three. So chapter nine's batch was the first accumulator all along.

The second step applies the step function to thirty-five and outputs one. Its proof has its own challenges and its own four polynomials and claims, and the site's script confirms that its revdot product and the check on the split of b hold. The list now has five claims, with the first accumulator's polynomial and instance in front.

The verifier sends alpha equal to twenty. The first quotient divides the old polynomial, minus twenty-three, by the variable minus seventeen. That one quotient is the only difference from chapter nine's list. The prover combines the five quotients and sends their commitment, with a blinding factor of seven.

Next the verifier sends u equal to thirty, and the prover sends the five values. The verifier derives the quotient combination's value from those values and the instances, and gets thirty-seven. The prover gets the same number by evaluating the combination directly.

The old accumulator's term is the fold's link to the past. Its claimed value now sits inside the formula for the combination's value, so a false claimed value would make this number wrong.

The verifier sends beta equal to twenty-five, and each input carries its weight into the commitment, the value and the blinding factor. The accumulation verifier has now finished. It read no polynomial, and its recomputation gives the new value thirty-one at the point thirty, with blinding factor thirty-nine.

The prover computes the new witness from the polynomials with the same weights, and that list still has eight entries. It kept its length even though it absorbed a new round of claims, because each fold takes a linear combination of lists that all have the same length. This is why a proof's size does not depend on the length of its history, as chapter two required.

The third step repeats the procedure with alpha twenty-four, u twenty-six and beta thirty-eight, giving the value twenty at the point twenty-six. Suppose the chain stops here, so a decider takes over. It commits to the third witness with blinding factor forty-two and gets the commitment in the instance. It evaluates the polynomial at twenty-six and gets twenty, so it accepts.

One multi-scalar multiplication over eight generators and one evaluation of eight coefficients stand in for opening all twelve polynomials of the three proofs.

Picture this chain as two lanes. In the upper lane, the accumulator's instance passes through accumulation verifiers inside circuits, with each proof's instance entering from above. In the lower lane, the witness passes through accumulation provers outside circuits, with each proof's witness entering from below. New instances and accumulation proofs cross upward from prover to verifier, and both lanes end at the decider.

A tree needs no new idea. A node with two children holds two accumulators and two proofs, and all of their claims go in one list, as chapter ten described. Suppose a second chain has proved that the step function maps eight to nine, and has reached its own accumulator.

A node takes that accumulator and the second accumulator of our chain, along with one new proof from each side. The input list has ten claims, two accumulators plus four claims from each proof. The fold outputs a new instance, the accumulation verifier accepts, and the decider accepts the witness. The accumulation proof grows to one point and ten values, while the output keeps the same three-part instance and eight-entry witness.

What the split costs and saves.

Now compare the two schemes for the opening work alone, at eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators and thirty-two bytes per value. Both schemes also need the commitments to the proof's polynomials and still have to deal with the revdot product and the wiring. Those parts are the same for both, so we leave them out.

In the circuit, with one proof and one accumulator, the atomic scheme takes sixty-three scalar multiplications and the split scheme takes six. With two proofs and two accumulators, the counts are ninety-nine and eleven. The atomic count grows logarithmically with the polynomial size, and the split count does not grow at all.

The prover's group work for this opening work falls from seventy-three thousand seven hundred twenty to eight thousand one hundred ninety-two, which is the commitment to the quotient combination. The accumulator data the circuit reads falls from four hundred forty-eight bytes to ninety-six. The final verifier's count falls from eight thousand two hundred forty-nine to eight thousand one hundred ninety-eight, plus one evaluation.

The cost shows up in what passes between provers. The atomic handoff is a short proof of one thousand two hundred forty-eight bytes and an accumulator of four hundred forty-eight, for one thousand six hundred ninety-six bytes in all. The split handoff grows to one million forty-nine thousand two hundred sixteen bytes.

That split handoff is chapter nine's proof, except that the batched polynomial and blinding factor at its end are now the accumulator's witness. A proof here has no batch of its own, because its claims go straight into the fold. The accumulator's witness has the same length as that batch, so the size does not change. The handoff is therefore three hundred eighty-four bytes of instance and one million forty-eight thousand eight hundred thirty-two bytes of witness. Two hundred sixty-two thousand one hundred seventy-six bytes of that witness belong to the accumulator.

The saving and the cost both come from one change. The verifier's count falls and stops depending on the polynomial size, since the inner product argument's L and R points have gone. The prover's group work falls, since it no longer folds generators. In return, the data handed from step to step changes from a few short values to whole lists, because the next prover needs every one of them.

These byte counts describe our protocol at Ragu's polynomial size, and Ragu's own proofs have a different layout, as the section on Ragu shows. Because the witness never enters a circuit, the growth affects storage and bandwidth and does not affect recursion. That trade suits a system like Ragu, where a proof stays large while it is being fused and only needs to become short when it leaves the tree.

The decider's linear work has not disappeared, and it has the same size as in chapter eleven. It occurs once for a whole history in both schemes. The difference lies in what the steps before it had to do.

Why a nark is enough.

We can now rewrite the recursive relation of chapter ten. The circuit no longer checks the previous proof. It checks that the accumulator was updated correctly to take that proof in. Following the book's relation, with the inputs narrowed to what the accumulation verifier reads, the public part is the new state and the new accumulator's instance.

The witness holds the old state, the previous proof's instance, the old accumulator's instance, and the accumulation proof. The circuit makes three checks. The step function maps the old state to the new one. The previous proof's instance has the old state and the old accumulator's instance as its public input. And the accumulation verifier accepts the fold.

The book lists the whole previous proof and accumulator in the witness, and the accumulation verifier reads only their instance parts. The witness parts go to the accumulation prover, which runs outside the circuit, so a proof of this relation can be a nark.

The middle check ties the accumulator that the circuit folds to the one the previous proof committed to, and the section on cheating shows what goes wrong without it. The output handed onward is the new accumulator and the new proof. The circuit for each step folds the proof of the step before, which is the one-step lag our toy run set aside.

When the computation ends, a final verifier outside any circuit receives that pair. No later step will fold the last proof, so the final verifier does that fold itself, the way chapter eleven's final verifier merged the last proof's claim. The prover attaches an accumulation proof for folding the last proof's claims into the last accumulator, together with the witness of the result.

The final verifier runs the accumulation verifier on the instances, which takes a few scalar multiplications, and then decides the resulting accumulator once. That is all it does with the last proof in this chapter. It holds the whole proof, so it could also check that proof's revdot product and wiring directly, as chapter nine's verifier did. We leave those checks to chapters fourteen and fifteen, which handle them for every proof in the same way.

The paper states the general result. Any non-interactive argument of knowledge with a split accumulation scheme yields proof-carrying data, without requiring the argument to be succinct.

The reasoning runs backward from the end, in the same way as chapter ten's walk, and it takes three steps. First, the decider accepts the accumulator that the final fold produced, so that accumulator is true. Its commitment opens to a polynomial that takes the stated value at the stated point.

Second, the accumulation verifier passed on that final fold. A passing fold with a true output means, except with small probability, that its inputs were true. So the last proof's opening claims and the last accumulator were true. The section on cheating examines why this holds.

Third, the last proof is valid, so its circuit was satisfied, and that circuit ran the accumulation verifier on the fold that produced the last accumulator. Since that accumulator is true, the argument of the second step applies one step back, and it repeats down to the base case.

The third step has a gap, and it is the scope we set earlier. A valid proof includes chapter nine's checks on the revdot product and on the wiring commitment, and so far nothing performs them. As written, the construction makes the openings of every proof sound, which means each commitment really opens to the value the proof claims. It stops short of a complete proof-carrying data construction.

Chapter fourteen adds the revdot check as a second fold inside this same relation, and chapter fifteen adds the check on the wiring commitment. Once both are in place, the third step holds for every proof, and the backward walk covers the whole history.

Chapter nine listed two linear costs as targets for this part of the site. Computing the folded generator does not arise on this recursive path, because the path contains no inner product argument. Computing the wiring polynomial's value remains. In the toy run the site's script recomputes it from the circuit, because the folds establish what its commitment opens to and nothing about whether it belongs to the right circuit. The wiring chapter deals with this.

The base case and the record of initial states follow chapter ten. Our run began with no accumulator at the first fold, which keeps the numbers equal to chapter nine's. The book uses a placeholder accumulator instead, while the code uses one bootstrap step, and chapter ten compares them. The Merkle root of initial states in the book's description has no counterpart in the code, so we leave it out.

Folding schemes.

Nova introduced a related formalization called a folding scheme, which its abstract describes as a reduction from checking two instances of a relation to checking one. The site could read only that abstract, so this part rests on it and on the book. The book says the two formalizations are equivalent for all practical purposes.

Elsewhere the book calls folding a related alternative, one that merges statements themselves without using any proof system's verifier during accumulation. Those wordings fit together once we write our scheme as a folding scheme. Take the relation with the commitment, point and value as instance and the polynomial and blinding factor as witness. Our fold reduces checking five instances of it to checking one, with a verifier that sees only instances.

In folding terms, the accumulator's instance and witness become the running instance and running witness. The proof's claims become the fresh instances, and the accumulation proof becomes the folding proof. The decider becomes the check of the final instance against its witness.

So our scheme is a folding scheme for evaluation claims, and a verifier that reads nothing of a proof beyond its instances is what the book means by using no verifier. What differs is mainly the point of view. Accumulation starts from a proof system and asks what can be postponed, whereas folding starts from a relation and asks how to combine instances of it.

Not every relation folds as cleanly as this one, and the reason explains why the accumulation proof can take different forms. The book says an accumulation proof contains the cross terms the verifier needs. Evaluation claims at one point combine linearly and need no such term, which is why our accumulation proof only has to bring the claims to a common point.

A relation with a product does not combine linearly. Suppose a claim says that the inner product of two lists equals some value, and we fold two instances with a challenge. Multiplying out gives the first value, plus the challenge times a middle term, plus the challenge squared times the second value. That middle term is the cross term. The verifier cannot compute it from instances, so the prover has to supply it.

Here is a small case, with old values twenty-nine and nine, at the challenge nine. The folded lists have inner product two. The two old values alone give only twenty-seven. The prover supplies a cross term of two before it learns the challenge, and including it gives two, which matches.

Nova's folding of R one C S is commonly described with a term of this kind. Ragu's revdot claims have the same structure, so chapter fourteen develops this example into a complete protocol.

Why cheating fails.

A prover in this scheme can lie in more places than in chapter eight, because the verifier no longer sees any polynomial. The toy curve permits forgeries, since every discrete log is known. So the counts below model a prover who holds real polynomials and does not exploit those relations, except in the last example, which shows what happens if it does.

Altering the output.

The verifier recomputes the new instance from the inputs, so the prover has little freedom in stating it. At the second fold, the site replaced the new commitment with each of the forty-two other points, and the new value with each of the forty-two other values. Separately, it replaced the quotient commitment with each other point while keeping the output.

It also shifted each of the five values at u by each nonzero amount while keeping the output, which makes two hundred ten more cases. The accumulation verifier rejected two hundred ninety-four of the three hundred thirty-six altered runs.

The other forty-two are shifts of the value for a, and a short calculation shows why. Shifting that value has two effects on the new value. It changes the derived quotient value, which enters the new value weighted by beta to the fifth, and it changes the value's own term, weighted by beta cubed.

The two effects cancel exactly when beta squared times alpha cubed equals the claim's point minus u. Here beta squared is twenty-three and alpha cubed is two, whose product is three, and thirty-three minus thirty is also three. So every shift of this one value leaves the output unchanged, and it stays the same true claim. The prover gains nothing.

The coincidence comes from our hand-picked challenges. With u fixed at thirty, only forty-two of the one thousand seven hundred sixty-four pairs of nonzero alpha and beta satisfy the condition, about one in forty-two.

The instance is therefore a function of the inputs and of the prover's messages. The prover keeps the freedom to change those messages consistently, and the verifier cannot detect that. For example, a prover can send a false quotient commitment and output the matching new commitment. The verifier recomputes that same point and accepts, and this happens for all forty-two false values, because the verifier cannot see what the quotient commitment commits to.

A prover who wants to survive the decider as well has to know a list behind its false commitment. Say it raises the constant coefficient of the quotient combination by one. Combining that with the other polynomials gives a new witness, and the decider's multi-scalar multiplication accepts it, since it commits to the stated point. The evaluation check rejects, because the witness evaluates to twelve at thirty while the output states thirty-one.

This shows the limit of what the accumulation verifier does. It confirms the arithmetic of the update and says nothing about whether the new claim is true. For a false quotient commitment, the multi-scalar multiplication catches nothing once the prover knows the list it committed to, so the evaluation carries the whole check.

That check fails unless the false combination happens to take the value at u that the verifier derived. This is the case chapter eight analysed as the cheater committing to a different polynomial. The prover fixes its combination before u is drawn, so it can arrange that equality only by luck.

A false claim travels forward.

Now suppose an input is false. Say the prover states the old accumulator's value with some error added, and it otherwise follows the protocol, including honest division, which ignores claimed values. The polynomials and the quotient commitment stay honest, so the new commitment commits to the true polynomial.

The error enters at one place, the old accumulator's term in the derived quotient value. There it gets multiplied by alpha to the fourth and divided by u minus the old point, and the quotient value enters the new value weighted by beta to the fifth. Every factor is nonzero as long as alpha and beta are nonzero, so a false input produces a false output.

Each later fold repeats this calculation, with the previous gap in place of the error. Take our run, but let the prover state twenty-four for the first accumulator's value, where the true value is twenty-three, a gap of one. Every later step is honest, with the same challenges.

After the second fold the stated value is twenty and the true value is thirty-one. After the third they are nine and twenty, so the gap is thirty-two both times. The accumulation verifier accepts both folds, because the output instance is what it recomputes. The decider rejects the last accumulator, since its value differs from the polynomial's.

The gap stays the same at the last two folds only by coincidence, because at the third fold the multiplier in the gap formula happens to equal one. For the second fold, the formula gives thirty-two, which matches.

The site also ran all sixty-eight thousand seven hundred ninety-six choices of nonzero alpha, nonzero beta and an allowed u for that fold. The allowed points leave out the four claim points, where a denominator would vanish. No run gives a zero gap.

So the gap survives every fold and reaches the decider. A chain that never runs the decider has established nothing about its openings, which was also the conclusion of chapter eleven.

A prover can do better than stay honest after the lie, for example by altering the values at u or by committing to something other than the true quotients. Chapter eight counted these options in full, and the same bounds apply to each fold, because the fold is that protocol with the accumulator as one more claim. For a real field, each fold's chance of hiding a false claim is negligible. Those chances add across folds and grow with private retries under Fiat-Shamir.

Dropping the accumulator.

A prover with a false accumulator might try to avoid carrying it at all, by folding some other accumulator in its place. Nothing in the fold's arithmetic forbids this. Suppose the prover holds the false first accumulator and folds an unrelated true accumulator in its place. If the circuit reads the swapped instance, the accumulation verifier and the decider both accept. Run against the false accumulator's instance, the same output fails.

The fold cannot detect this by itself, so the protection has to come from the circuit. The old accumulator's instance has to be part of the previous proof's public statement, which ties it to that proof through the statement polynomial. The wires holding it in the next circuit must feed both the accumulation verifier and that computation. Chapter ten met the same shared-wire requirement for the state.

The order of the messages.

Chapter eight showed that each challenge must follow the message it tests. In a non-interactive proof, this means the transcript has to absorb everything the challenges depend on. The accumulator's instance belongs on that list, and it is the one input chapter eight did not have, so it is the easiest to forget.

Suppose the transcript leaves it out, so the prover knows all three challenges before it fixes the old value. It states the false value twenty-six, an error of three. It sends twenty-four in place of the true value twenty-eight at u, a shift that solves one linear equation.

The two errors cancel in the new value, so the output is the true claim. The accumulation verifier accepts and the decider accepts, although the old accumulator was false. The remedy is for the transcript to absorb the old accumulator's instance before it draws alpha, so that its value is fixed before any challenge exists.

Even a weaker ordering leaves the prover little. If it fixes the same two messages after seeing alpha and u but before beta, then only two of the forty-three values of beta pass, zero and twenty-five. Beta zero is the case chapter eight described, where beta to the fifth removes the error along with almost everything else, and a random beta avoids it.

Binding at the decider.

The decider's check means something only because the commitment can be opened in one way, and the argument above assumed this throughout. Using the toy curve's known discrete logs, a prover can state the false value thirty-two for the second accumulator. It presents a different witness that commits to the same point and evaluates to thirty-two at thirty, so the decider accepts it. On a real curve, such a witness would give a discrete log relation among the generators.

The assumptions.

This reasoning is informal and is not a security proof. It relies on the discrete log relation assumption for the generators, and it treats the transcript hash as a random oracle, with each challenge drawn after the data it tests. It assumes that the circuit ties each input instance to the previous proof, and that the decider runs honestly on the final accumulator.

The paper states that its construction is secure in the random oracle model under the hardness of discrete logarithms. Recursion adds the further concerns that chapter ten listed, and we do not analyse Ragu's scheme here.

Split accumulation in Ragu.

What follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The code has a proof type that contains this accumulator, and its fuse folds it in the way we described, with the claims about the children's accumulators at the head of the list. One line of the fuse adds a child's polynomial and commitment to the running combination, which is the beta step for one input.

The code has no separate accumulator object for this claim. The proof type keeps the polynomial as a field and computes the value by evaluating it at u, so instance and witness travel together as one value.

A fuse folds far more than our five claims, with the children's polynomials in the first two places, and chapter nineteen goes through the list. The quotient combination is marked as ephemeral, used only during the fuse step, which matches the book's description of the accumulation proof as internal. The values at u go into a committed stage that the proof keeps, which chapter seventeen explains.

A child's value is a slot of the unified instance, whose fields are written in the statement polynomial's order, and the statement polynomial's computation covers that instance. The site reads this as the protection that the dropped-accumulator attack called for.

The documentation of the uncompressed verifier says that this verifier is the decider, and calls it a test oracle rather than a production path. In production, compression turns the decider's check into an opening that ends in an inner product argument. So chapter seven's argument comes back once, at the point where a proof leaves the tree. Chapter nineteen covers the details.

The Ragu Book and the code differ in several places. The book's accumulator has a blinding factor, while the code's proof stores the polynomial with its commitment and no such factor, in line with chapter eight's note on blinding. Our run includes one so that its numbers match chapter nine.

The book's claim list covers the commitments to a, b, the wiring polynomial and the statement polynomial. The code's list covers the folded a and b, registry polynomials in place of the wiring polynomial, and stage polynomials. The site found no commitment to a statement polynomial among its inputs, and circuits compute its value directly.

The book gives the accumulator on one curve as the wiring commitment, the commitments to a and b, the revdot value, and the batched commitment, point and value. The code's unified instance holds more, as chapter eighteen shows. The book has one fuse circuit per curve, while the code has many internal circuits.

This site follows the code. The simplified single-curve scheme remains the book's own way of conveying the core idea, and this chapter uses it in that spirit.

The fold itself differs from ours in two further ways, which later chapters cover. A circuit cannot compute the new commitment cheaply, so Ragu divides the fold between two circuits on a curve cycle, as the next chapter describes. Those circuits have to agree on their challenges, which the endoscalars chapter covers.

The witness also raises a question about privacy, because a proof that carries polynomials in the clear reveals what they contain. Neither the book nor the code claims otherwise at this commit. The book states that the current construction does not yet establish zero knowledge, in part because it lacks a randomizer at the level of the fold. Chapter two records the same status.

Tachyon, which has not activated on mainnet and whose design is still changing, depends on fusing proofs of this kind through a different Ragu revision.

Where this leaves us.

Atomic accumulation forces an inner product argument at every step, because its verifier reads the whole of what it is given. Sorting the verifier's opening and revdot steps shows that every linear one reads a witness list and every cheap one reads only the instance.

An evaluation claim divides into an instance and a witness, and so does an accumulator, which is a claim of the same form. The accumulation prover runs chapter eight's reduction on the old accumulator and the new proof's claims. The accumulation verifier recomputes the new instance from instances and the accumulation proof, and the decider runs one multi-scalar multiplication and one evaluation.

The witness keeps a fixed length across any number of folds, and the circuit's work on the instance does not grow with the polynomial size. The data handed between provers grows to the size of the polynomials. Since the witness never enters a circuit, each step can use a nark, and the opening claims of a whole history rest on one decider.

Written as a reduction between instances of one relation, this scheme is a folding scheme, and relations with products also need cross terms in the accumulation proof. A false input leads to a false output at all but a few challenges, so it reaches the decider. This depends on the circuit tying each input instance to the previous proof and on the transcript absorbing the accumulator.

We set aside one problem throughout, which is that the accumulation verifier performs group operations on points whose coordinates live in a different field from the circuit's wires. The next chapter introduces a pair of curves that removes that mismatch, and it shows which circuit folds what.
