Chapter eighteen. Recursion in Ragu.

Part four built the pieces one at a time. We have a way to fold revdot claims, a registry that makes wiring cheap to check, endoscalars that let challenges cross the curve cycle, and stages that let circuits share data. This chapter puts them together into the operation that Ragu runs at every node of the tree.

That operation is one fuse. We list what it has to do, name the stages and commitments it produces, and follow the order of its messages. Then we look at the circuits that divide the work, the split of the transcript between two of those circuits, the public inputs they share, and what a proof hands to its parent. The next chapter takes the proof that leaves the root of the tree and makes it short.

This chapter is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins and from the book's recursion chapters, and the authors have not confirmed it. The numeric runs use the toy sponge and small stand-in values, so they show the structure of the code and not its real contents. We did not build or run the code.

A few names will recur. This site says fuse for the operation at a node of the tree, and native and nested for the two sides of the cycle. The decider is the check that runs when the recursion ends. The code uses the name pre-beta for the challenge from which the short challenge beta is extracted. We call the two challenges of the revdot fold tau and tau prime, as in the revdot folding chapter, where the code calls them mu and mu prime.

What one fuse has to do.

A fuse receives two child proofs and a step, and it has to produce a new proof that a parent can fuse in turn. The recursive proofs chapter described the idea as a proof about a verifier. The children's verifier cannot run in full, so each child's expensive work joins an accumulator, and a fuse checks the cheap remainder inside its circuits. That remainder is still large compared with one circuit, which may hold at most two to the eleven gates.

Replaying the transcript alone takes two thousand three hundred four gates, more than one circuit holds, as we count later in the chapter. The walk that folds the other curve's commitments fills twenty-three circuits of its own, a count that the endoscalars chapter derived.

So the work has to be divided. The application circuit runs the step, and two circuits called hashes one and hashes two replay the transcript. A circuit called collapse folds the scalars of the revdot claims, and compute V checks the batched evaluation. The bind circuits carry the challenges across the cycle, and the endoscaling steps fold the other curve's commitments.

Dividing the jobs among circuits creates a new need, because the circuits have to agree with each other. They must use the same challenges, and they must read the same child data. Stages give them shared witness data, and the public inputs we reach later give them shared public data. So a fuse produces many small traces where a textbook recursion step would produce one large one. We count them once each one has a name.

The order of the messages.

Each challenge has to depend on everything it tests, so the order of the commitments is the backbone of a fuse. Before we list that order, we need names for what gets committed, because the code refers to each group of commitments by a short name.

The stages and their names.

Most of the shared data of a fuse sits in stages, the partial traces that several circuits read. The native side has five stages that carry the recursion's data, and a few more that serve the endoscaling walk.

The preamble holds the children's data, which is their headers, their unified instances, and their circuit identifiers. The inner error stage holds the error terms of the first layer of the revdot fold. The outer error stage holds the error terms of the second layer, the values the first layer produced, and the sponge state that we meet when we split the transcript.

The query stage holds claimed values of polynomials at the points x and x times z, and the values of the registry polynomial that the circuits use. The eval stage holds the value of every queried polynomial at the point u, and running sums for the challenge binding.

Five points stages hold the nested-curve commitments that the walk combines, grouped by the challenge each must come before. The walk stage holds the bits of the endoscalar and the intermediate points of the walk. Here the walk is the sequence of point additions that multiplies a point by an endoscalar in gates, as in the endoscalars chapter.

The stages also form a tree, which matters for the circuits. The staging chapter showed that a stage names a parent and starts in the layout where its parent ends. At the root of the native tree sits the points binding stage, and it has two children, the preamble and the points children stage.

Under the preamble sit outer error and query. Inner error sits under outer error, and eval sits under query. Under points children runs a single chain, from the points stage for the registry restrictions, to points ab, to points f, and finally to the walk.

The order in the layout is separate from the order of commitment. So inner error sits under outer error in the layout, although the fuse commits it first.

Not everything a fuse commits to is a stage. The restrictions of the registry polynomial, the new accumulator pair A and B, and the batch quotient f are ordinary polynomials. The code still gives each group a short name, s prime for the restrictions, a b for the pair, and f for the quotient. It does so because each group has to reach the transcript in the same way as a stage.

That way is the bridge. The transcript runs in the native field, and a native commitment has coordinates in the other field, so the transcript cannot absorb it directly. The staging chapter solved this by writing the coordinates into a stage of the nested circuit and committing to that stage on the nested curve, whose coordinates are native.

Ragu does this once for each of eight groups, named preamble, s prime, inner error, outer error, a b, query, f, and eval. The nested stage for a group is its bridge stage, and the nested commitment to it is its bridge commitment. A bridge stage can carry more than coordinates. The inner error bridge, for instance, also holds the nested side's own first-layer error terms, which then reach the transcript at the same moment.

The schedule.

The code follows one schedule of eleven phases. In each phase the prover commits to something, the transcript absorbs a bridge commitment, and the transcript then releases the challenge that tests it.

In phase one, the prover commits to the trace of the application's step, which includes the child and output headers, and no bridge or challenge follows. In phase two, it commits to the preamble and two points stages, points binding and points children. The transcript absorbs the preamble bridge and releases the challenge w.

In phase three, it commits to each child's restriction of the registry polynomial at w and that child's x, and to the points stage for those restrictions. The s prime bridge then releases y and z. In phase four, it commits to another restriction and the inner error stage, and the inner error bridge releases tau.

In phase five, it commits to the outer error stage, whose bridge releases tau prime. In phase six, it commits to the new accumulator pair and points ab, and the a b bridge releases x. In phase seven, it commits to one more restriction and the query stage, and the query bridge releases alpha.

In phase eight, it commits to the batch quotient f and points f, and the f bridge releases u. In phase nine, it commits to the eval stage, whose bridge releases pre-beta, and after that it commits to the nested challenge stage. Phase ten commits to the walk stage and the new batch polynomial p, and phase eleven commits to the traces of the internal circuits, with no challenge after either.

Nearly every phase is a step of an earlier chapter. Phases two to four and phase seven run the registry consistency check from the chapter on wiring and the registry. Phases four to six run the quotient fold from the revdot folding chapter. Phases eight to ten run the split accumulation chapter's method.

Each points stage is committed in the phase where its points first exist, so that its commitment rides in the bridge absorbed in that phase. Points binding and points children hold the children's nested commitments, and they are fixed before w. The other three hold the nested side's own new restrictions, accumulator pair, and quotient, fixed before y, x, and u in turn.

Phases nine and ten need a further word. Phase nine ends with pre-beta, which has to lie in the range from which the endoscalars chapter extracts an endoscalar. When a squeeze lands outside that range, the prover re-randomizes the eval stage and squeezes again.

The chapter on statements and challenges called this kind of retrying grinding, and it showed that each retry already costs a cheater a hash call. The retry gives a prover nothing it lacked, because a cheating prover could always re-randomize and squeeze again on its own. An honest prover almost never retries, since the values outside the range are a vanishing fraction of the field. The code grinds no other challenge.

The nested challenge stage of phase nine holds the lifts of all nine challenges to the nested field, which the endoscalars chapter used to carry the challenges across the cycle. The prover can fill it only once pre-beta exists, so the code commits it after the squeeze, without blinding. Its commitment enters no transcript in this fuse, and it first reaches one in the parent, through the parent's points binding stage.

The circuits that check that stage face a timing problem. The bind challenges circuits sum the stage's terms for the first eight challenges into a partial commitment, and that partial sum sits in the eval stage. But the eval bridge is what pre-beta hashes, so the eval stage is committed before pre-beta exists, and its partial sum cannot contain pre-beta's term. The parent's bind beta circuit adds that term later. The walk stage comes in phase ten for the same reason, because it holds the bits of pre-beta.

Phase one has no challenge, because nothing in this fuse's transcript tests the application trace. The internal circuits come last because they prove statements about everything before them, and their traces feed no challenge of this fuse either. Instead both kinds of trace commitment enter the parent's preamble bridge, which puts them on the parent's transcript.

The internal circuits.

We can now say what each native circuit does. Hashes one replays the first part of the transcript to get w, y, and z, and saves the sponge state for hashes two. It also computes the children's public input values for the internal circuits, and checks that each child's circuit identifier is a point of the registry's domain. Hashes two resumes from the saved state and replays the rest, to get tau, tau prime, x, alpha, u, and pre-beta.

Collapse computes the children's public input values for the application circuit, and checks both layers of the revdot fold down to the new value c. Compute V computes the batched value v from the values held in the query and eval stages. It needs beta as a native field element, which it gets from the bits of pre-beta.

Four bind challenges circuits each take two of the transcript's challenges, and each checks that the nested side's challenge stage holds them, by walking that stage's generators. For each child, bind beta adds the pre-beta term to the partial sum that the child's bind challenges circuits exported, and compares the result with the child's challenge stage commitment. The child could not add that term itself, because its eval stage was committed before pre-beta was squeezed. Bind beta also checks that the points the child's walk used are the ones the child's public inputs name.

Bind endoscalar checks that the bits driving the walk are the bits of pre-beta and that the walk's points lie on the curve, and it makes the walk's final point public. Twenty-three endoscaling step circuits each perform four steps of the walk that combines the nested commitments with powers of beta.

A circuit reads a stage by including it in its sum, as the staging chapter described. In the layout, the stages a circuit reads sit along one path of the stage tree, and the circuit's own final trace takes the gates after the last stage on that path. The circuit's final mask covers those gates, so it is named after that last stage. Circuits that end on the same stage share one final mask.

Some constraints on a stage, such as a point lying on its curve, have to be written by one of the circuits that read it. The staging chapter explained that the code marks that circuit by loading the stage enforced. The other readers load it unenforced and rely on that one.

Hashes one reads the preamble and outer error, and hashes two reads outer error alone, so both end on the outer error mask. Collapse reads the preamble and both error stages, and it loads the error stages enforced. Compute V and the bind challenges circuits read the preamble, query, and eval, and compute V loads the preamble enforced. Bind beta reads points binding and the preamble. Bind endoscalar and the endoscaling steps read the points stages and the walk, and bind endoscalar loads the points stages enforced.

The collapse circuit shows why the final mask needs separate attention. It reads outer error, but inner error follows outer error in the tree, so collapse ends on inner error even though the fuse commits inner error earlier. In the code, the final mask for inner error covers collapse alone.

Each enforced load needs a reason. The preamble's points and some of its bits must be well formed, and compute V checks them, although the code gives no reason for that choice and any single reader would serve. The points stages need every point on the curve, and bind endoscalar checks this once for the endoscaling steps.

The other stages are pinned in other ways. Bind endoscalar compares the walk's bits wire by wire with pre-beta, and each intermediate point must equal what an endoscaling step computes from enforced inputs. The query and eval values need no check to be well formed, in our reading. If one is false, the claim that p at u equals v is false, and a later fold or the final verifier catches it.

One example shows why stages were worth building. The hashes one circuit computes each child's public input value and needs the child's data, which sits in the preamble. The collapse circuit reads that same data. Both read one polynomial, so a prover cannot show them different children, and only the preamble's commitment has to enter the transcript.

The nested side has a shorter list, with no transcript and no application circuit, as the curve cycles chapter found. Its collapse checks the nested fold, its compute V computes the nested v, and twenty-six endoscaling steps combine the native commitments, a count that the endoscalars chapter also derives.

The application's circuit uses no stages. All of the machinery above sits beside it and leaves its budget of gates to the application.

The book and the code differ on one stage chain. The book's recursion chapter gives hashes two the chain preamble, then outer error. In the code at the commit this site pins, the claim for hashes two sums only its own trace and the outer error stage, and its documentation marks the preamble as skipped. The layout still reserves the preamble's gates, which is what skipping a stage means. This site follows the code.

What one fuse produces.

With the stages and circuits named, we can count what one call to fuse commits. The count matters in two places. The end of this chapter argues that a proof keeps the same shape at every depth, and the next chapter measures how large that shape is.

In the standard profile, the native side commits thirty-four circuit traces, which are the thirty-three internal circuits and the application. The nested side commits twenty-eight, which are twenty-six endoscaling steps, collapse, and compute V. Each side commits eleven stages. On the nested side those are the eight bridge stages, the challenge stage, and one stage each for the endoscalar's bits and the points it walks.

Each side also commits four accumulators, which on the native side are A, B, the registry restriction at x and y, and p. And each side commits four polynomials used only inside the fuse, which are the three restrictions taken at w and the quotient f. That makes fifty-three native and forty-seven nested polynomials, so the schedule commits one hundred in all.

A proof carries ninety-two of them to its parent, which is the count the code records. The other eight serve only the fuse that made them. Their values at the fuse's points sit in the query and eval stages, and the fuse folds the polynomials themselves into its new batch polynomial p. So a parent that holds p never needs them again, and the code marks them as ephemeral.

The claims follow the same split. The native side folds eighty-six revdot claims, a count that the staging chapter derived. Its sixteen mask claims are one for each of the eleven stages and one for each of five final masks, which are outer error, inner error, eval, preamble, and walk. The nested side folds seventy-two claims, which are two raw claims, twenty-eight circuit claims per child, and fourteen mask claims.

Splitting the transcript.

The transcript has to be replayed in circuit, because the Fiat-Shamir challenges would otherwise be numbers that the prover merely asserts. One circuit cannot hold the whole replay, as a count of gates shows. A permutation costs two hundred eighty-eight gates, and the code documents three permutations in the first half and five in the second.

Together that makes eight permutations and two thousand three hundred four gates, which already exceeds the limit of two thousand forty-eight gates per circuit before any other check runs. The two halves cost eight hundred sixty-four and one thousand four hundred forty gates, so each fits.

So the replay has to cross a circuit boundary, and the sponge's state has to cross with it. The second circuit cannot recompute the state without repeating the permutations it is trying to avoid. Passing the state as a public input would add wires to every public input polynomial, so the code places the state in a stage.

The state is known once the transcript absorbs the inner error bridge. The outer error stage is the next one committed, because it holds values folded with the tau that the state produces. So hashes one replays the first three bridge commitments, hashes two replays the last five, and the state passes between them through the outer error stage.

We run this on the toy sponge from the chapter on statements and challenges. The bridge commitments are points on the toy curve E prime, whose coordinates lie in the field of forty-three elements, the field the sponge works in. The first point is the one the staging chapter built, and the others are stand-ins.

The preamble bridge leads to w equal to eleven. The s prime bridge leads to y equal to four and z equal to ten. Hashes one absorbs the inner error bridge, which leads to tau equal to two, and saves the state at that point. Hashes two replays the rest, which gives tau prime equal to ten, x equal to four, alpha equal to twelve, u equal to twenty-five, and pre-beta equal to thirty-three.

The first half ends by permuting and saving the three cells of the state, which are two, forty-two, and twelve. The second half loads those cells and squeezes tau with no further permutation, so its five permutations begin when it absorbs the outer error bridge. We ran the sponge once straight through and once split at this point, and all nine challenges agree.

The toy sponge takes three permutations in the first half and five in the second, as the code does. That happens because we leave out the domain tag, and the sponge's rate of two fits one point per permutation.

Reading the state from a stage leaves one question, which is what forces the stage to hold the right value. Only hashes one knows that value, having computed it, so the check goes in that circuit. Its last transcript step saves the state and enforces that the result equals the sponge state held in the outer error stage.

Without that check, a prover could pick the state and with it the challenges. Suppose a prover wants tau to be nine, and it writes the state nine, forty-two, twelve into the outer error stage. The second half alone would resume from this state and produce tau equal to nine and tau prime equal to twenty.

Of the seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven possible states, one thousand eight hundred forty-nine give the wanted tau. But the first half compares the stage with the state it computed, and exactly one state passes that comparison, the honest one. Both circuits read one committed stage polynomial, so the prover cannot show them different values.

Public inputs.

Each circuit also has a public input polynomial, and a parent has to compute its value at the challenge y for each of a child's circuits. If every internal circuit exposed its own values, a parent would compute many different polynomials. The code avoids that with a unified instance. That is one structure of values that nearly all internal circuits expose in full, so that one computed value serves them all.

Sharing an instance raises the question of who checks each value, since a circuit that merely exposes a value proves nothing about it. The code assigns each slot to exactly one owner circuit, which constrains the value, and soundness rests on those constraints. The prover code also asserts that every slot was filled, but that is a consistency check for honest provers and adds nothing to soundness. Other circuits read a slot as a witness, and the shared public input value forces them to agree with the owner.

Hashes one owns the first three bridge commitments and w, y, and z, and hashes two owns the other five bridge commitments and its six challenges. Collapse owns c, and compute V owns v. The last bind challenges circuit owns the challenge stage's partial commitment, and bind endoscalar owns four nested points. The slots add to thirteen points and eleven elements, which is the code's thirty-seven wires.

Recursion uses three forms of public input polynomial over these values, plus a fourth with no wires of its own. All of them are Horner evaluations ending in the constant wire's one. The unified form runs over the unified instance and then a zero. The headers form runs over the unified instance, the left and right child headers, and then a zero. The application form runs over the left header, the right header, and the output header, with no zero.

Horner's rule multiplies the running value by Y and adds the next wire, and it ends by adding the one. So the last wire before that one becomes the coefficient of the first power of Y. The next section uses that coefficient.

The hashes one circuit uses the headers form because it also exposes the child headers. The application circuit exposes the same headers through the application form. A parent computes both values from the one preamble, so together the two claims stop a proof from using one pair of headers in the step and another in the recursion.

The fourth form belongs to the endoscaling steps. An endoscaling step exposes nothing but the zero, so its polynomial is the constant one. The code calls the headers form by a name containing the word bridge, but that name has nothing to do with the bridge stages and bridge commitments, so we use a different one here.

A toy run shows the forms side by side. Take a unified output of five wires, which are the first bridge point's two coordinates and the challenges w, y, and z from the run above. Take two child headers, eight followed by three and twelve followed by three, whose second entry is the suffix, and the output header twenty followed by three. We evaluate everything at y equal to four.

Horner's rule reaches forty after the five unified wires, and the unified and headers forms both continue from that value. The unified form ends at thirty-nine, the headers form at twenty-six, the application form at six, and the endoscaling step's form at one. The coefficient of the first power of Y is zero for every form except the application form, where it is three, the output header's suffix.

These values reach the fold through the parent. Its hashes one and collapse circuits compute each child's values from the preamble stage with gates, at the y of the current fuse, and those values become the targets of the claims being folded.

The book and the code count the unified instance differently. The book's public inputs chapter says that it holds twenty-nine wires, from eight commitments and thirteen field elements. The book's recursion chapter lists nine challenges and two final values, which makes eleven elements. The public inputs chapter also says that four internal circuits share the unified form.

The code, at the commit this site pins, has thirteen points and eleven elements, which makes the thirty-seven wires above. The five additional points are the challenge partial and the four points that bind endoscalar owns. Nine circuits share the unified form, because bind challenges has four instances. This site follows the code.

Keeping internal and application circuits apart.

The registry holds internal and application circuits side by side, and a proof names its application circuit by a domain point the prover selects. So a prover could try to name an internal circuit and claim its trace as an application step. To rule that out, the code makes the two kinds of public input polynomial unable to coincide. Each internal circuit ends its output with a zero, and each application header ends with a suffix that is never zero.

We saw that the last wire before the final one becomes the coefficient of the first power of Y. For every internal circuit that wire is the zero, so the coefficient is zero. For every application circuit it is the suffix, so the coefficient is not zero. The difference of an internal and an application polynomial therefore has minus the suffix as that coefficient, which means it is never the zero polynomial, whatever the other wires hold.

A nonzero polynomial has at most as many roots as its degree, so the two values agree at only a few y, and a random y tells the two kinds of circuit apart. A small example checks this count and also shows what goes wrong without the suffix.

Let an internal circuit expose two wires, a and b, and its zero suffix, so its polynomial is a Y cubed plus b Y squared plus one. Let an application circuit expose a header with a value d and a suffix s, so its polynomial is d Y squared plus s Y plus one. Their difference is a Y cubed, plus b minus d times Y squared, minus s Y.

If an application could use the suffix zero, the difference would vanish whenever a is zero and b equals d. That happens for forty-three of the seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven triples of a, b, and d, and for those the swap would pass at every y.

For the suffixes three, four, and five, nothing can cancel the coefficient of Y, and our search of all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of a and b minus d found no identical polynomials. At a nonzero y the difference vanishes only where a quadratic in y does. So the two values agree at no more than two of the forty-two nonzero values of y, and the worst pair reaches that bound.

This also explains why an endoscaling step needs the zero although it exposes nothing. Its comments say that the suffix is what stops a step from satisfying an application instance, given that the application's circuit identifier may name any native slot. The nested registry holds internal circuits only, so it omits the zero.

The book and the code differ on the first application suffix. The book says application-defined suffixes begin at two, with one reserved for the trivial header. The code, at the commit this site pins, reserves three values, with zero for fixed circuits, one for the unit header, and two for the dummy header of the base case, so application suffixes begin at three. This site follows the code. The book's argument is unaffected, since it needs only that the suffix is nonzero.

The value two is reserved for a reason. It marks the base case from the recursive proofs chapter, where collapse waives its final comparison. So a step whose suffix is a witness, and not a constant, has to constrain that wire away from two, or it could claim the waiver.

What a proof carries to its parent.

A parent does not repeat the checks in a child's circuits. It folds the child's claims into its own accumulators and reads the child's public data. Here is what it takes from each child.

It takes the headers and unified instance, holds them in the preamble, and computes the child's public input values. It takes the revdot accumulator, which is a pair A and B with a value c, and adds the pair as a raw claim with target c, folded into the new accumulator. It takes each trace and stage commitment, and adds a circuit claim or mask claim to the same fold.

It takes the batch accumulator, which is a commitment P, a point u, and a value v. It adds the claim that p at u equals v to its batch, folded into a new batch accumulator. It takes the child's registry restriction and checks it against its own, as we describe shortly. And it takes the nested points and the challenge partial, walks the points, and completes the challenge binding in bind beta.

Three of these are accumulators, namely the revdot accumulator, the batch accumulator, and the registry restriction. A parent receives two of each, one from each child, and it has to leave exactly one of each. Otherwise its own parent would receive more than two, and the work per fuse would grow with depth.

A toy run shows each pair becoming one. We reuse the worked runs of the revdot folding, split accumulation, and wiring chapters, with one side of the cycle only. So the challenge values here come from those chapters' runs, and not from the transcript run above.

For the revdot accumulator, the children's pairs are the first two proof claims of the revdot folding chapter, with values thirteen and thirty-five. The parent folds them with one layer of that chapter's quotient fold, the fold the schedule runs in phases four and five. For two claims the quotient has a single coefficient, nineteen. At tau equal to twenty, the challenge of that chapter's run, the new value comes out as nineteen, and the folded vectors' revdot product is also nineteen.

For the batch accumulator, the left child holds the first accumulator of the split accumulation chapter's run, and the right child holds a fresh one. The parent batches the claims that the left polynomial is twenty-three at seventeen and the right polynomial is six at thirty. With alpha equal to seven, u equal to twelve, and beta equal to nineteen, the new batch polynomial evaluates to ten at twelve.

For the restriction, the run is the registry run of the wiring chapter, with the parent's fresh w equal to fourteen. The restriction step packs two openings into one, so we take them in turn. The parent does not keep the child's restriction. It opens that polynomial at the parent's fresh w, and the chain of restrictions from the wiring chapter carries the result into the parent's single new restriction. In the toy run the left child's restriction gives ten at w, the right child's gives seven, and the parent's new restriction gives twelve there, which closes the chain.

The parent then opens its new restriction at the child's circuit identifier. That gives the wiring value of the child's circuit, which the parent needs for the child's circuit claim. In the toy run the two children's circuits are A and B from the wiring chapter, at the domain points one and forty-two. The parent's restriction gives two at the first, which is circuit A's wiring value, and seventeen at the second, which is circuit B's.

All of these values sit in the query stage, and only one restriction leaves the fuse.

The real fuse folds far more into each accumulator than the toy run does. The children's circuit and mask claims join the same revdot accumulator, and every queried polynomial joins the same batch accumulator. The count of inputs changes, but the output is still one of each, per side of the cycle. So a proof has the same shape at every depth. A child's internal circuits do not run again in the parent, and they cannot be skipped either, since their claims are among those folded.

Why cheating fails in a fuse.

The earlier chapters counted cheating at each building block, and those counts still apply here. This chapter added a new risk, which comes from dividing one verifier among many circuits. A prover might try to show different circuits different data, or to exploit a check that one circuit assumes another has made. We collect the attempts this chapter met.

A prover might write a sponge state into the outer error stage that gives the challenges it wants. Hashes one rejects every state except the one it computed itself.

A prover might try to show hashes one and collapse different children. Both read the one preamble polynomial, whose mask stops a circuit's final trace from overwriting it, so they see the same children.

A prover might try to leave a shared value unchecked. Every slot of the unified instance has one owner circuit that constrains it, and the shared public input value makes the other circuits agree with the owner. In the same way, every stage that needs a check of its own has one circuit that loads it enforced.

A prover might try to pass off an internal circuit as a step. The zero at the end of an internal output and the nonzero suffix of an application header keep the two kinds of polynomial apart, except at the few chance agreements we counted.

Finally, a prover might hope that a child's checks get skipped. A parent does not rerun a child's internal circuits, but it folds their claims into its accumulator, and the fold's error terms are committed before the challenges that weight them.

The argument is informal, and it rests on the assumptions of the earlier chapters. Nobody knows a relation among the generators on either curve, so commitments bind. The transcript behaves as a random oracle, and each challenge comes after the bridge commitment that carries what it tests. The checks that cross the cycle rely on the endoscalar lift being injective, as the endoscalars chapter found for Pasta.

Where this chapter leaves us.

A fuse divides its checks among many small circuits, which share data through stages and public inputs. Each group of commitments reaches the transcript through a bridge stage on the nested curve, and one transcript runs through eleven phases. Hashes one and hashes two share the replay, with the sponge state held in the outer error stage and checked by the first circuit.

The internal circuits expose one unified instance, with each slot owned by one circuit. A zero suffix keeps internal circuits apart from application circuits. A parent folds three accumulators per side from each child, so a proof keeps its shape at every depth.

That shape has a cost. Every accumulator, stage, and trace is a polynomial with two to the thirteen coefficients, and a parent needs all of them to compute its fold, so a proof that leaves the root of the tree carries every one. A verifier outside the tree wants something far smaller, and it also still owes the deferred work that the accumulators postponed. The next chapter, on compression, replaces the polynomials with commitments and a few values, and it runs the final checks on the toy curve.
