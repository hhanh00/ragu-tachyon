Chapter fifteen. Wiring and the registry.

The revdot fold of the previous chapter accepts any right-hand commitment whose vector has the right product. It never asks whether that vector was built from the right circuit's wiring. The check that would answer this is the computation of the wiring value. It is the last remaining linear step that the nark chapter's verifier listed, and it requires reading the whole circuit.

So this chapter has two problems to solve. The wiring is unchecked, and checking it directly costs as much as the circuit. A third problem comes with them, which is how different nodes of a proof-carrying data tree can run different circuits.

All three problems have the same answer. The prover commits to pieces of the wiring polynomial, a cheap random check ties each new piece to the old ones, and a single expensive check at the very end covers the whole history. The Ragu Book calls this idea post-processing, and it credits the idea to Halo. We start with one circuit, then extend the method to a polynomial that holds many circuits, called the registry polynomial.

The protocols in this chapter are reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed them. They come from the book's chapters on wiring consistency and on the registry. The toy circuits, the attacks, and the link between a chosen circuit and its wiring value are the site's own assembly, and we read that link from the code. We could not read the Halo paper, so what we say about Halo follows the book. A section near the end compares everything with the code.

What the verifier still computes.

Recall the check from the nark chapter that ties the right factor to the trace. The right factor's value at a point must equal the sum of three things, which are a trace value, the wiring value, and a gate value. The gate value has a short closed form. The wiring value has none, because the wiring comes from an arbitrary circuit.

Our toy circuit has eight nonzero wiring coefficients, so computing its wiring value at nine and seven, which is twenty-three, takes eight terms. A real circuit has at least one coefficient for each wire it uses. So at full size, where four times the number of gates is two to the thirteen, a circuit needs on the order of eight thousand one hundred ninety-two terms for this one value.

A recursive verifier would have to do this inside a circuit, so the circuit would have to contain a description of itself and grow with every wire it adds, as the chapter on recursive proofs found. The accumulation chapter escaped the same problem for the value called G hat by testing a claim at a random point. That worked because G hat's polynomial has a short product form. The wiring polynomial has no such form.

A natural next idea is to let the prover supply a commitment to the wiring polynomial, with its constraint variable fixed at the challenge. The accumulator would then carry this claim forward, as split accumulation does for evaluation claims. That chapter showed that folding establishes what a commitment opens to. It says nothing about whether the commitment holds the right polynomial, and this gap is exploitable.

Take the nark chapter's false trace, the one with input four, at the challenges z equal to five and y equal to seven. With the true wiring the revdot product is eighteen, and the target is thirteen. The prover adds thirty-one times X to the fourth to its wiring polynomial. The trace entry that this coefficient meets is four, and four times thirty-one is thirty-eight modulo forty-three, so the revdot product moves by thirty-eight and reaches the target.

The prover commits to this altered polynomial, which gives a different point from the true commitment. It builds the right factor from the same polynomial, so the check on the right factor holds at all forty-three values of x, and every evaluation claim is true of the committed polynomials. The only thing wrong with this proof is that the commitment does not hold the real wiring.

So the claim that this commitment holds the wiring has to be checked somehow, and we want the check to cost far less than computing the wiring. Polynomial identity tests are the usual tool for this. But a test needs a polynomial on the other side to compare with, and the verifier does not want to compute any of them. The next section finds that second polynomial inside the wiring polynomial itself.

Restricting a polynomial of two variables.

The wiring polynomial has two variables, which we'll call capital X and capital Y. Its coefficients form a table, with one row for each constraint, marked by a power of Y, and one column for each position in the trace, marked by a power of X. Our toy circuit's table has four rows and eight columns, and only eight of its cells are nonzero.

We can fix either variable. Fixing Y gives a polynomial in X, and fixing X gives a polynomial in Y. Each of these is called a restriction. Both directions take the same table and add it up along different lines, so the two restrictions agree where they cross.

Fixing Y at seven collapses the rows into one polynomial in X with eight coefficients. Fixing X at nine collapses the columns into a polynomial in Y. These are different polynomials, and they meet at one number. Evaluating the first at nine and the second at seven both give twenty-three.

This crossing is what the protocol uses. A commitment to a restriction in X and a commitment to a restriction in Y make two evaluation claims that must have the same value. The chapter on batched openings already knows how to check evaluation claims cheaply, and the verifier needs no knowledge of the table to compare the two claims.

Restricting in Y only makes sense if the result fits the commitment scheme, and this explains a limit we met earlier. The commitment has four n generators, where n is the number of gates, so the restriction in Y must have degree below four n. The book gives this as the reason for the bound of four n constraint rows.

Consistency for one circuit.

The first section ended with a commitment that nobody has checked against the circuit. A recursive step cannot afford to check it, so it carries the commitment forward as a claim. The claim pairs a commitment with a value of Y, and it says that the commitment holds the restriction of the wiring polynomial at that value. Suppose a node has two children, and each child's accumulator holds such a claim.

Each child's commitment is the wiring accumulator that the child received from its own fold, or a starting value if the child is a leaf. It is not the free commitment of the first section. That per-proof commitment is how the book presents a single proof, and the next paragraphs explain why a tree never needs it. The section on Ragu's code records this difference between the book and the code.

Before building anything, we need to know where in a tree the check of the first section runs, because that decides which wiring value a node has to supply. A child does not finish the check of its own trace. Its trace commitments travel up, and the parent folds the check into its own work, in the same way that the revdot chapter defers revdot claims to the next fold.

The parent checks each child's trace at challenges that it draws itself. Those challenges are valid tests, because a challenge tests a commitment only if it is drawn after the commitment is fixed, and the child's trace commitments were fixed long before. That check needs the child's wiring value at the parent's fresh point, which no earlier claim covers. So the accumulator that leaves the parent's fold has to supply it. The chapter on recursion in Ragu lists everything else a child passes up.

The node therefore wants to replace the two claims with one new claim of the same form, at a point of its own. Keeping one of the old claims would not work. The old claims sit at different values of Y, so neither speaks for the other, and neither sits at the node's fresh point. So the node makes a new claim, and it needs some commitment that links the old claims to the new one.

We call that linking commitment the bridge. It is unrelated to the bridge stages of the curve cycles chapter and the chapter on recursion in Ragu, which are stages of the secondary circuit that carry commitment coordinates. We want three things from the replacement. It should tie each old commitment to the true circuit, it should produce the new claim, and it should leave no work that depends on the size of the circuit.

The book's protocol achieves this with a single restriction in Y. A restriction in Y at one value of x crosses every restriction in X at that x, so one bridge meets both old claims and the new one. Both parties start with the two old claims. The verifier sends a random x, and the prover sends the bridge, a commitment to the restriction in Y at that x.

The verifier then sends a fresh random value of Y, and the prover sends the new commitment, to the restriction in X at that fresh value. Both parties then add six evaluation claims to the batch from the batched openings chapter.

Those six claims form three pairs. Each pair says that a restriction in X and the bridge agree at their crossing. The first two pairs concern the old claims, which cross the bridge at their old values of Y. The last pair concerns the new claim, which crosses the bridge at the fresh value.

This one-circuit version shows how claims chain, but it does not yet place the new claim where a trace check can read it. The trace check needs the wiring value at an x drawn after y, because the prover commits to the right factor once it knows y, and x then tests that commitment. Here x comes before the fresh y, so the right factor could have been built to pass at that x.

We cannot repair this by drawing the fresh y first, because the bridge is read at that fresh y. Reading a commitment at a point tests it only if the point was drawn after the commitment was fixed. With only two variables, x has to come before the fresh y. The registry adds a third variable, and that gives room to reorder the challenges so that the new claim lands exactly where the trace check reads.

We now run this on our circuit, using two accumulators with values we have already met. The first holds the nark chapter's y, which is seven, and the second holds the y of the second proof in split accumulation, which is thirty-four. The verifier sends x equal to twenty-one, the prover sends the bridge, the verifier sends the fresh y equal to twelve, and the prover sends the new commitment.

The prover states one value for each pair, which gives six claims about four polynomials. The first pair shares the value thirty-five, the second shares six, and the third shares two. The verifier never computes these values. It treats them as claims that share polynomials and places them in the batch, so one number in each pair appears twice.

The batch runs in three moves, as in the batched openings chapter. A challenge called alpha weights the claims' quotients, and the prover commits to their weighted sum. At a second challenge, the prover reveals each polynomial's value, so the verifier can derive the weighted sum's value without opening it. A third challenge merges everything into one claim, which a decider settles by recomputing the vector behind it.

In our run the prover reveals four values, one for each polynomial, and the decider's recomputed commitment and value both match. Checking the crossing identity at all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of x and y confirms that an honest prover passes at every challenge.

A batched check tells us that the claims are true of the committed polynomials, and it says nothing about the circuit. Something still has to connect the last commitment to the real wiring. That job falls to the end of the chain, in the same way that a single multi-scalar multiplication ended the accumulation chapter's chain.

If the chain stops here, the decider computes the restriction at Y equal to twelve from the circuit, which takes eight terms. It commits to the result and gets the new commitment. That one linear computation stands in for computing the wiring at each of the three points.

Why a false accumulator fails.

The argument runs backward from the decider. Suppose the decider has confirmed that the new commitment holds the true restriction at the fresh y. Then the third pair of claims fixes the bridge's value at the fresh y, and the prover committed to the bridge before it saw that value. Two different polynomials of degree below four n agree at no more than four n minus one points. So the bridge is the true restriction, except with probability about four n divided by the size of the field.

That makes the bridge correct at the old values of Y, so the first pair fixes the old commitment's value at x. The prover fixed that old commitment before it saw x. The same reasoning then shows that the old commitment holds the true restriction.

We can follow this in the toy run by giving the prover a false old commitment and counting what it can do at each step. Say it holds a commitment built for the attack of the first section. The first check it faces is the pair at x.

The most favourable false polynomial differs from the true restriction by a multiple of the polynomial with roots at one through seven. That is the difference the nark chapter found best for its cheater. The prover scales this multiple so that it still moves the revdot product by thirty-eight, since otherwise the false commitment would no longer carry the attack.

Against an honest bridge, the pair of claims at x can hold only where this difference vanishes. Trying all forty-three values of x finds seven that pass, which are the values one through seven. No polynomial with eight coefficients has more roots.

Outside those values the prover must lie in the bridge to keep the pair true, which moves the error one step along. At x equal to twenty-one, the false commitment opens to fifteen, while the true value is thirty-five. The error is minus twenty, which is twenty-three modulo forty-three.

The prover can absorb it by adding to the bridge a polynomial in Y that equals twenty-three at the first old y, seven, and zero at the second, thirty-four. Then both old pairs hold. This added polynomial can have at most seven roots, and one of them has to be thirty-four. Taking the other six at one through six, we tried all forty-three values of the fresh y against an honest new commitment, and seven pass.

Outside those values the error moves once more. At the fresh y of twelve, the added polynomial equals twenty, so the prover has one option left. It adds twenty to its new restriction, which then opens at twenty-one to the value that the false bridge requires. All six claims are true of the committed polynomials, and the batched check accepts.

The prover has moved its error into the new accumulator. The decider computes the true restriction at twelve itself, gets a different commitment, and rejects.

So a false accumulator does not disappear. At each step it either hits a rare root or it passes its error into a new commitment, and the decider exposes the last one. A chain that never runs the decider has established nothing about its wiring, in the same way as the evaluation claims of split accumulation.

All of this depends on the order of the messages. Suppose the prover knows x equal to twenty-one before it fixes the old accumulator. It can use the difference thirty-five times X cubed times X minus twenty-one, which vanishes at twenty-one and still gives the revdot shift of the first section's attack.

That false polynomial passes with an honest bridge and an honest new commitment, and the decider accepts the accumulator that leaves the fold. This is why the old commitments have to enter the transcript before the transcript produces x.

A polynomial for many circuits.

The protocol so far assumes that every accumulator refers to the same wiring polynomial. Now suppose a tree has two kinds of step. We use the running circuit as circuit A, which proves that x cubed plus x plus five equals thirty-five. We add a circuit B with the same number of gates, which proves that x to the fourth plus two equals forty.

Circuit B differs from A in two constraints. One says that a wire of the second gate equals the first gate's output, and the other says that the second gate's output equals thirty-eight. The constant thirty-eight is B's output minus two. The public input polynomial supplies it as its coefficient of Y cubed, in the same way that circuit A's public input supplies thirty-five minus five for A's last constraint. Later in the chapter both circuits change to read the output itself.

The two accumulators may now refer to different circuits, and the bridge stops working, since one bridge cannot be a restriction of both. Let the first accumulator hold circuit A at y equal to seven, and the second hold circuit B at y equal to thirty-four. If the bridge holds circuit A's restriction, the first pair holds. The second pair compares circuit B's value, twenty-four, with circuit A's value, six.

Over all forty-three values of x, these agree only at zero. So an honest prover with two different circuits fails almost everywhere.

We could avoid this by merging A and B into one large circuit with a selector, as the arithmetization chapter did for a branch. Then every step would pay for the gates of all its branches. The tally from the introduction to proof-carrying data shows the cost. It has two steps, called Entry and Join, and a model with a single rule at every node would merge them into one circuit with a selector. So every leaf of the tree would pay for a Join it never runs, and every inner node would pay for an Entry.

A better way is to keep the circuits apart and to put the choice of circuit into the polynomial itself, as one more variable, which we'll call W.

Lagrange interpolation works on any set of distinct points, but roots of unity give two properties that later sections use. Testing whether a value belongs to a domain of two to the k points takes only k squarings. And a domain of two to the k points sits inside the domain twice its size, which lets a registry grow without moving its circuits.

So pick one point for each circuit, made of the successive powers of a root of unity whose order is a power of two. For each point, take the Lagrange polynomial in W that equals one there and zero at the other points, as in the polynomial encoding chapter. The registry polynomial multiplies each circuit's wiring polynomial by its Lagrange polynomial and adds up the results.

Setting W to a circuit's point makes every term vanish except one, so the registry polynomial becomes that circuit's wiring polynomial. The field needs enough roots of unity for this. The toy field has forty-two nonzero elements, and forty-two is two times twenty-one. So its only roots of unity whose order is a power of two are one and minus one, which is forty-two, and our toy registry holds exactly two circuits.

With two points, the first Lagrange polynomial is one plus W, divided by two, and the second is one minus W, divided by two. At W equal to one, the registry polynomial is circuit A, and at W equal to forty-two it is circuit B. The script compares them at all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of x and y, and they agree at every pair.

At W equal to fourteen, the two weights are twenty-nine and fifteen, and the result is a blend that is neither circuit. Since the two Lagrange polynomials add up to one at every W, these two weights also add up to one, modulo forty-three, and we return to this fact later. The blend belongs to no single circuit, and the protocol uses it as a random fingerprint of the whole set.

An accumulator now has to carry a claim that does not name one circuit, so that accumulators from different circuits can merge. The form the book chooses leaves W free. The claim is made of a commitment, an x value and a y value. It means the commitment holds the polynomial in W that gives every circuit's value at that x and y.

Leaving W free pays off when a step needs one circuit's value. Opening this polynomial at a circuit's point gives that circuit's wiring value at x and y, for any registered circuit, and a later section puts that to use. Since this polynomial is committed with the same generators, it needs degree below four n in W, and the code enforces that a registry has at most four n entries.

Consistency for the registry.

The two old claims now differ in two coordinates, x and y, while the one-circuit claims differed only in y. From here on, each child's old x and y are its own values, and the new challenges of this node are plain w, x and y. So the new y plays the part of the fresh y from the one-circuit protocol.

A single bridge cannot reach both old claims. The order of the bridges follows from one constraint. Each commitment holds a polynomial in one variable, so a bridge that moves one variable has to fix the other two. The old claims are polynomials in W, with their x and y fixed. The book's protocol makes four moves in a fixed order.

The first move fixes W. A bridge in Y needs a value for W, so the verifier draws w first, and each old claim crosses its bridge at that w.

The second move changes Y. The two bridges in Y are both read at one new y. After this, the children's restrictions share w and y and differ only in X. Moving Y before X leaves x as the last challenge, which the trace check needs, as the one-circuit section explained.

The third move changes X. One bridge in X, at w and the new y, now crosses both children at their old values of x, and it is read at one new x.

The fourth move frees W again. A last restriction in W, at the common x and y, returns the claim to its original form. That form lets the next node repeat the walk, and it is what a step opens at a circuit's point.

Here are the steps of the protocol. Both parties hold the old claims. The verifier sends a random w. The prover sends two bridge commitments, each holding a polynomial in Y at that w and one child's x. The verifier sends a random y, and with it the challenge z that the trace check uses.

The prover then sends a commitment to the polynomial in X at w and y, which we'll call the middle commitment. The verifier sends a random x. The prover sends the new commitment, which holds the polynomial in W at x and y. Finally, both parties add ten evaluation claims to the batch.

Each move along this walk is a crossing of two restrictions, and each crossing gives a pair of claims, so five crossings give the ten claims. The first two crossings tie each old commitment to its bridge at w. The next two tie each bridge to the middle commitment at the new y. The last ties the middle commitment to the new one at x.

Two bridges are needed at the start because the children have different values of x, and a restriction in Y is taken at one value of X. Only one middle commitment is needed afterward. By then both children's restrictions are read at the same y, so both land on the same polynomial in X.

For the toy run, let the left child have used circuit A at the nark chapter's challenges, x equal to nine and y equal to seven. Let the right child have used circuit B at the x and y of split accumulation's second proof, thirty-seven and thirty-four. The choice of circuit does not show up in the children's commitments, because each of them holds every circuit's value at its child's point. It matters only when a step opens the new accumulator at a circuit's point.

The verifier draws w equal to fourteen, then y equal to twelve with z equal to sixteen, and then x equal to twenty-one. The new commitment holds a polynomial in W with two coefficients, thirty-one and fourteen. It uses only the first two generators, because on this domain the polynomial has degree one in W.

The left child's commitment happens to be the same point as the false accumulator from the earlier attack, only because the toy group has forty-three elements. The polynomial behind it here is honest.

The five crossings share the values ten, seven, forty-two, twenty-six and twelve. The script repeats all five comparisons at all seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven triples of w, x and y, and an honest prover passes every one. The accumulator that leaves the fold has the same form as the two that entered, so the next node can repeat the walk.

Selecting a circuit.

The walk keeps the accumulators consistent with the registry, but the point of all this was to obtain a wiring value for the trace check, for whichever circuit a child used. As the one-circuit section explained, the parent checks each child's trace at its own challenges. It adds a revdot claim for each child to its revdot fold.

The parent builds each child's right factor from its own y and z. The right factor is the sum of three polynomials, which are the child's trace polynomial with its variable scaled by z, the child's wiring restriction at y, and the gate polynomial at z.

The parent's prover commits to this right factor after y and z are drawn and before x is, so the spot check at the parent's x tests it. That check needs the child's wiring value at x and y, which is exactly the point of the new accumulator. The new commitment holds the registry polynomial in W at that x and y. So opening it at a circuit's domain point gives that circuit's wiring value there.

Each circuit's value is one evaluation claim on the same commitment, and the prover names the circuit by naming the point. The check of the right factor then reads a claimed value that the batch will test, in place of a value the circuit would have to compute.

In our run the new polynomial is thirty-one plus fourteen W, and x times z is thirty-five. The left child used circuit A, at the point one, so its wiring value is two. Adding its trace value at thirty-five, which is twenty-one, and the gate value twenty-three gives the right factor's value, three.

The right child used circuit B, at the point forty-two, so its wiring value is seventeen. Adding its trace value seven and the same gate value gives four. The script also confirms that each child's revdot product at the new y and z equals its own target, with different wiring and different public inputs on the two sides.

The two children used different wiring, and the same step of the protocol handled both. Only the evaluation point changed. These two openings bring the list to twelve claims on six polynomials, and the batch combines them into one claim as before. This time the prover reveals six values, one for each polynomial, and the decider for the merged claim accepts.

The wiring decider then evaluates the accumulator at a fresh point. At W equal to thirty, the polynomial gives twenty-one. Weighting the two circuits' own values by their Lagrange polynomials at thirty, and adding, also gives twenty-one. That comparison is the registry's form of the decider. A later section counts what it costs, after two sections on how circuits get their points and on which points a prover may name.

Assigning domain points.

So far each circuit got a power of a root of unity, but that root depends on the size of the domain, and so on the number of circuits. A registry that grows from four circuits to five moves from four points to eight. The root changes, and so does the point of every circuit except the first.

Circuit code that refers to another circuit's point would then need to know the final count, and such references exist. The recursive verifier's internal circuits open the registry at fixed internal-circuit points, and a step reads the point of the circuit its child used.

The toy field has no room to show this, so this section uses the field of ninety-seven elements, for arithmetic only. Its multiplicative group has order ninety-six, which is thirty-two times three, so it has a root of unity of order thirty-two. With four points, the naive assignment gives circuit one the point twenty-two. With eight points, circuit one gets sixty-four, and circuit two gets twenty-two instead.

To avoid this, we take one maximal domain, whose size is the largest power of two dividing the group order, and assign each circuit a point there. Write the circuit's index in binary across the full width, reverse its bits, and use the result as the exponent of the root. The book calls this rolling domain extension. Circuit one then gets ninety-six, and circuit two gets twenty-two, whatever the size of the registry.

Reversing the bits puts the low bits of the index at the top of the exponent, so small indices give exponents that are multiples of a large power of two. In five bits, circuit one reverses to the exponent sixteen, and circuit two reverses to eight. The element orders for the first eight circuits come out as one, two, four, four, eight, eight, eight, eight.

Those orders show why this works. The first four circuits always land on elements of order at most four, which are exactly the fourth roots of unity, and the same holds for every power of two. So the circuits fill the small domain first, and growing the registry adds new points without moving old ones.

The element also stays the same when written in the smaller domain, which the script checks at every size. So a circuit can compute its own point, or another circuit's, from its index alone. A function in the code does this, and a test in the code asserts the same orders that we just listed.

On the toy field the largest available order is two, so circuit zero gets the point one and circuit one gets forty-two, the two points our registry has used all along. The degree of the registry polynomial in W still depends on the domain size, because the Lagrange polynomials change with the domain. What stays fixed is the element that names each circuit.

What the prover may select.

The prover chooses the point at which the new commitment opens for each child, and a verifier should worry about a free choice like this. We'll call it the named point, because the prover might name a point that holds no circuit at all. We go through three kinds of point the prover might choose. These are a point outside the domain, a domain point that holds no circuit, and a point that holds the wrong registered circuit.

All three cases turn on the lowest row of the wiring, the row for Y to the zero. So this section first switches to circuits that have a meaningful lowest row. The two-gate circuits A and B used so far have no system gate, and the nark chapter's constant wire adds one.

That section added a system gate at position zero, with two of its wires at zero and a fourth wire that holds one. It also added a constraint in the lowest row, saying that this fourth wire equals the first coefficient of the public input polynomial, which the instance sets to one. Because that constraint has the single coefficient one, it makes the restriction at Y equal to zero the constant one.

So this section uses three-gate versions of A and B with the constant wire. In these versions both circuits read the output itself from the public input polynomial, whereas the earlier versions read a constant derived from the output.

A point outside the domain.

Recall that the Lagrange weights add up to one at every W. So at a point outside the domain, the registry polynomial is a blend of the circuits whose lowest row is still the constant one. The blend behaves as a third circuit that nobody registered.

The decider's blend at a random w does no harm, because nobody checks a trace against it. But a blend at a point the prover chose can serve as the wiring of a child's trace check.

Take the public input whose first coefficient is one and whose second coefficient is the public output, thirty-five. Let the prover name the out-of-domain point four. There the weights are twenty-four and twenty, which add up to one, so the mixture's lowest row is the constant one and it looks like a circuit.

The input seven does not satisfy x cubed plus x plus five equals thirty-five. Yet a trace for input seven, with one wire set to thirty, satisfies the consolidated equation at all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of z and y. The same trace passes against circuit A at only forty-three pairs, and against circuit B at forty-three.

So the protocol has to check the named point. A value is in the domain exactly when squaring it k times gives one, where the domain has two to the k points. An internal circuit called hashes one enforces this on both children's named points, and the uncompressed and compressed verifiers make the same check on the root's point. Here four squared is sixteen, which is not one, so the check catches this selection.

A domain point with no circuit.

The domain usually has more points than circuits, because its size is the next power of two above the number of circuits. The book treats every domain point as a wiring polynomial. So an unassigned point holds the zero polynomial, and a prover might name it in place of a circuit.

The two differ in one coefficient. A circuit has the constant wire's constraint in its lowest row, so its restriction at Y equal to zero is one, while the zero polynomial gives zero there. The registry also holds polynomials of another kind, called bonding polynomials, which the staging chapter introduces. They too give zero at Y equal to zero.

For example, a registry with three entries on a domain of four points might hold circuit A, circuit B, and a bonding polynomial, with the fourth point unassigned. The two circuits give one in the lowest row, and the other two entries give zero.

The verifier always sets the first coefficient of the public input polynomial to one when it expects a circuit, and this rules out every entry whose lowest row is zero. To see why, read the consolidated equation at y equal to zero, as a statement about the constant term in Y. Such an entry contributes nothing there, so the only terms left on the left side are the gate terms from the arithmetization chapter.

Those gate terms would have to equal one for every z. The only term that contains z to the zero comes from the system gate's product of its output wire and its fourth wire. That term always brings z to the power four n minus one with it, so a constant one cannot be formed.

In the toy run, take the trace with input three and the public output thirty-five, and let the prover name the zero polynomial. Its gates hold, so the left side is zero, and the equation becomes zero equals one plus thirty-five y. That holds for one value of y, so over all the pairs it passes at forty-three.

A prover can try to manufacture the missing one from the system gate's product, by setting its output and fourth wire to one, with an instance that has no public outputs. Then the left side is one plus z to the eleventh, because the term at z to the zero always comes with its partner. It equals one only at z equal to zero, which is again forty-three of the pairs.

These entries also change the blend from the previous case. When some domain points hold entries whose lowest row is zero, the weights on the circuits alone need not add up to one. So a prover would have to search for a point at which they do, and the domain check rules out such a point in any case.

A different registered circuit.

The two checks above leave the prover a free choice among registered circuits. That choice is safe only if the statement says which circuit it expects. Suppose the verifier wants a proof that some x has x to the fourth plus two equal to thirty-five. Trying all forty-three values shows that no such x exists, so this statement is false. The verifier builds the public input for output thirty-five, and the prover selects circuit A in place of circuit B, with input three. Every check passes at all the pairs, because the instance is the same as that of the true statement that three cubed plus three plus five equals thirty-five.

Now add a row to each circuit that sets the linear coefficient of the public input polynomial to a value fixed for that circuit, two for A and three for B. The output moves up to the next coefficient. Circuit B with a true statement still passes everywhere, for example input three with output forty. The same substitution with circuit A now passes at only forty-three of the pairs, all of them at y equal to zero.

The extra row gives each circuit a label that the verifier writes into the public input, so a proof from one circuit no longer matches a statement meant for another. Ragu calls this label a suffix. The book's section on substitution says that an application circuit's suffix fills the linear coefficient of the public input polynomial and is never zero. That also keeps internal circuits apart from application circuits, as the chapter on recursion in Ragu explains.

Header types and suffixes.

In the code a suffix belongs to a header type and not to one circuit. So, unlike our toy row, it leaves steps with the same output header interchangeable. Coming right after the substitution attack, this could look like a hole in Ragu, but the structure of proof-carrying data makes it safe.

A step's check reads only its input headers and their types, as the steps in the introduction to proof-carrying data do. So a parent accepts a child produced by any step that outputs a valid header of the type it expects. Each such step enforces its own check on its own inputs. Substituting one for another can therefore only produce a header that some valid step could have produced, and proof-carrying data asks only that every producible header be true.

The substitution attack worked because the verifier's statement named circuit B, and circuit A produced an output that no valid run of B could produce. In proof-carrying data the statement names a header type rather than a circuit.

What the suffix still has to separate is headers of different types. That matters even when two types hold the same data, because a step must accept only the types it was written for. Extend the tally from the introduction to proof-carrying data with a final step called Charge that adds a flat fee of two. It consumes the header zero, eight, forty-seven and outputs zero, eight, forty-nine.

If Charge accepted its own output, a prover could apply it again to reach a sum of fifty-one, even though the fee is meant to apply once. The input and output are three numbers of the same form, so the numbers cannot tell the two states apart.

Giving the input and output different types, say Tally and Charged, gives them different suffixes. A Charge step that accepts only a Tally then has no valid input for a second run. Tachyon's Actions and Stamp headers work this way. They contain identical fields, and only the suffix tells them apart. The step that applies a bundle's fee accepts only Actions and outputs a Stamp, so it cannot run twice on one bundle.

What the registry costs.

The cost divides among three parties. The recursive circuit handles claims only, so its cost does not depend on the size of the circuits. It does a little field arithmetic per claim, as in split accumulation, and the fold adds six commitments to that chapter's list, the children's two and the four new ones. It grows with the size of the registry only through the domain check, which takes k squarings per child.

Compare the options for a hypothetical tree with two hundred fifty-six application circuits of two to the eleven gates each, which is the gate limit that the recursive proofs chapter recorded. The registry also holds internal circuits and bonding polynomials, so it has more than two hundred fifty-six entries, and its domain needs more than two hundred fifty-six points.

Computing the wiring values directly takes at least sixteen thousand three hundred eighty-four multiplications for two full circuits. One merged circuit with selectors puts five hundred twenty-four thousand two hundred eighty-eight gates on every step. The registry takes twelve claims, six commitments in the fold, and at least eighteen squarings.

The prover pays more than this comparison suggests. Each of the four new polynomials is a Lagrange-weighted sum over the circuits, so computing one touches every registered circuit, including those the step did not use. We have not measured these costs.

The decider pays too. To test the new commitment, it computes the registry polynomial at a fresh w, which means one wiring value for each circuit. That cost is linear in the total size of the registry, but it is paid once for a whole history.

Adding a circuit therefore leaves the recursive circuit almost unchanged. So the book encourages applications to split work into many small circuits that communicate along the tree, and it speaks of hundreds or thousands of circuits. Tachyon, which has not activated on mainnet and whose design is still changing, has a few dozen steps at the revision we read, and those steps run against a mock of Ragu.

Post-processing and the registry tag.

We can now say what is unusual about this design. Many recursive systems preprocess each circuit into a verification key, a short commitment to the circuit's polynomials, and the verifier reads this key. With many circuits, a recursive verifier would have to handle a table of keys.

Ragu has no such keys, as its book states. A verifier holds the registries themselves, built from the circuit definitions, and a prover computes the restrictions it needs at each step. That is the sense in which the work moves from before the proof to after it.

Without keys, one question remains, which is how the transcript comes to depend on the circuits. Fiat-Shamir requires the statement to enter the transcript before any challenge, and the circuits are part of the statement. Here is what goes wrong otherwise.

Fix the false trace from the first section, and suppose the transcript has produced z equal to five and y equal to seven from data that does not depend on the circuit. At these values the trace misses its target, because its last constraint is off by thirty-eight.

An author who has seen y can now publish a slightly different circuit, whose first constraint has one coefficient raised by thirty-one. That constraint fails on the trace by thirty-one times four, which is thirty-eight. Its weight is y to the zero, which is one, and the last constraint's weight is y cubed, which is forty-two, so the two errors cancel exactly.

Two constraints are broken, and the equation holds at y equal to seven. Counting over all the pairs, it holds at one hundred twenty-nine, namely those with y equal to seven, thirty-seven or forty-two. So the verifier was misled only because the circuit came after the challenge.

The usual fix is to hash the circuits into the first transcript state. That does not work directly here, for a reason the code documents. The registry contains the recursive verifier's own circuits, and those circuits replay the transcript. So a transcript that starts from a digest of the registry would need that digest inside the circuits that it summarizes. Keyed systems avoid the cycle because they generate the key beforehand and carry it in public inputs.

Ragu breaks the cycle with a registry tag, a field element we'll call kappa. The tag adds kappa times the highest power of X times the highest power of Y to the registry polynomial, at the one slot that no circuit uses. Both of those powers are four n minus one. We call the tag kappa because k already names the public input polynomial.

We should first check that this cannot break an honest proof. Read as a constraint, the term sits in the last row, and in the revdot product its partner is the system gate's output wire, at position zero of the trace. So it adds the constraint that kappa times that wire equals zero, which holds for every kappa, because that output is zero.

The code reserves that row by refusing a circuit that would reach it. The term has no factor of W, so it appears at every domain point. It also vanishes when Y is zero, so it does not disturb the distinction between circuits and the entries whose lowest row is zero.

Take kappa equal to nineteen. On the trace of the nark chapter's constant-wire circuit, the system gate's output is zero, and the tagged wiring polynomial still satisfies the consolidated equation at all the pairs. The tag cannot be added to our earlier two-gate circuit, whose wire in that position is nine. There the honest trace passes at only forty-three pairs, those with y equal to zero.

The tag reaches every commitment a fold makes to a restriction. For x equal to twenty-one, take the commitment to the constant-wire circuit's tagged restriction in Y. Going through all forty-three values of kappa gives forty-three different points. So a transcript that absorbs this commitment gives challenges that the circuit's author could not predict before kappa was drawn.

To see that this reaches the challenges the attack needed, follow the transcript of the registry walk. The fold absorbs the children's commitments in its first stage, before it produces w, and it absorbs the two bridges before it produces y and z. Each of these commits to a restriction of the tagged polynomial, so each depends on kappa. So y and z, the two challenges that the adaptive attack had to know in advance, depend on kappa too.

The order of events provides the protection. The whole system description, including every circuit, is fixed and published first, and then kappa is sampled independently and without bias. An author cannot adapt a circuit to challenges that depend on a number drawn after the circuit was published. The code keeps one tag for each of its two registries.

This mechanism is temporary. The book and the code both call the caller-supplied tag a temporary workaround for issue seventy-eight, which replaced an earlier tag derived from evaluations. Each application has to supply its tags, for example from a public randomness beacon, and the interface does not verify how they were produced. Any argument that needs a nonzero tag has to include the small chance that uniform sampling returned zero. Expect this part of the design to change.

Why cheating fails.

This argument is informal. It assumes the discrete log relation assumption for the generators, it treats the transcript hash as a random oracle with each challenge drawn after the data it tests, and it assumes that someone runs the decider.

It also assumes that the circuit ties each child's claim and the point named for it to that child's proof, for the reason split accumulation gave about dropping an accumulator. A prover who could restate a child's x and y freely could prove consistency for coordinates that have nothing to do with the check they were meant to support.

The backward reasoning from the one-circuit protocol applies to the registry link by link. Each commitment is tested at a challenge drawn after it was fixed, and a false commitment passes only at a root of its difference from the true polynomial.

Running the toy registry with the best difference we could build at each link gives the same count every time, seven passes out of forty-three. That holds for the old commitment, tested at w, for the bridge, tested at y, and for the middle commitment, tested at x. It also holds for the new commitment, tested at the next node's w or by the decider. The right child's commitments behave in the same way.

Those rates are large because the field is small and the polynomials have eight coefficients. At full size, where four n is two to the thirteen, the field has about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements. Each link then passes with probability at most four n minus one, divided by the field size.

Adding this over the six commitments of one fold gives forty-nine thousand one hundred forty-six divided by two to the two hundred fifty-four, which is below two to the minus two hundred thirty-eight.

Now take the attack that opened this chapter and try it against the full protocol. The prover no longer gets to supply a free commitment, because its wiring value has to come out of the accumulator at the point of the circuit it named. Replay the attack at y equal to twelve and z equal to sixteen. The false trace needs a wiring value of twenty-six at x equal to twenty-one, while circuit A's true value is two.

That value comes from the new commitment at W equal to one, so the prover has to change this polynomial. It adds nine times W minus fourteen, times W plus one. That addition vanishes at fourteen and at forty-two, so it disturbs neither the claim at w equal to fourteen nor the right child's value.

All twelve claims are true of the committed polynomials, and the batched check accepts. The false accumulator commits to a different point from the honest one. The next check tests it at a fresh point. Over all forty-three values, the only ones that pass are fourteen and forty-two, the two points the prover built into its difference.

That false accumulator then behaves as in the one-circuit attack. It moves from node to node, it changes form with each fold, and at the end of the chain the decider rejects it. As split accumulation showed, this conclusion relies on binding, because a prover who knows a relation among the generators could open the same point to a different polynomial.

Wiring and the registry in Ragu.

The code at the commit this site pins runs the registry walk inside the function called fuse, on both of its registries. Its test-oracle verifier states what each stage must hold, as a list of seven registry evaluations. That list locates all five shared values from our run and the two openings at the children's circuit points.

The code calls the bridges registry w x zero and registry w x one, the middle commitment registry w y, and the new commitment registry x y.

What follows is reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. On message order, the fuse produces w after the preamble, which carries the children's stored commitments, and then commits to the two bridges. It produces y and z together and commits to the middle polynomial. It later produces x and commits to the new polynomial. That order matches the walk, with the revdot messages of the previous chapter in between.

On the claims, a static query list names all ten claims of the walk and then the two openings at the children's circuit points. The fuse also opens the new polynomial at each internal circuit's fixed point, so the real list has more entries than our twelve. Those values feed the right factor's check for internal circuits in the same way as an application circuit's value. A code function called finalize registers internal circuits first, which gives them low indices, then bonding polynomials, internal steps and application steps.

On the decider, the test-oracle verifier samples a fresh w and compares the stored polynomial with the registry's value at that w. In production, compression turns this into an opening of the commitment at w, whose value the verifier computes from the registry. That is the final linear cost, which the chapter on compression places in the larger picture.

On the two registries, the nested registry runs the same walk at the nested counterparts of the challenges, on the other curve of the curve cycle. Its query list has no openings at children's circuit points. That agrees with the curve cycles chapter's finding that application circuits sit only on the native side.

The Ragu Book and the code differ on four points here. The first is the per-circuit commitment. The book's wiring chapter opens with a commitment to the current circuit's restriction, and its nark and its claim list include one. We found no such commitment in the fuse. The code adds the restriction directly into the right factor and reads the wiring value from the registry, as we described.

The second is the list of claims. The book's registry protocol lists ten claims and stops, so it does not say how a chosen circuit's value reaches the right factor's check. The code adds the openings at circuit points, and this site follows the code.

The third is domain points. The book's wiring chapter writes each circuit at a plain power of the root and notes that this ignores the remapping. The opening of its registry chapter says that circuits go to successive powers, while its later section and the code use bit reversal. Our earlier sections use plain powers as the book's simplification does, and on the toy domain the two agree.

The fourth is memoization. The book's routines chapter describes a floor planner and memoization that let circuits with common routines share the prover's work over the registry. That is one source of the diminishing overhead the book claims. A code comment calls a multi-dimensional strategy planned under issue fifty-eight. We did not establish how much of this applies to the registry at this commit.

Where this chapter leaves us.

Without a check against the circuit, a prover can commit to wiring of its own and satisfy every other part of the proof. Two restrictions of a polynomial agree at their crossing, which turns a claim about wiring into a pair of evaluation claims. A false claim either hits a rare root or passes its error forward until a decider rejects it.

The registry polynomial holds many circuits, and one commitment can supply any circuit's wiring value. The registry walk moves W, Y and X one at a time, with four new commitments and ten claims, and each child's circuit adds one more claim. The recursive circuit's cost grows only with the logarithm of the registry size, while the prover and the decider pay for reading all of the circuits.

Bit reversal gives each circuit a point that does not depend on the size of the registry. A domain check, the constant wire and header suffixes limit what the prover can name. Because no verification keys exist, the transcript depends on the circuits through a registry tag, which the code treats as temporary.

This completes the three linear subprotocols that the nark chapter listed. These are evaluation claims, folded in split accumulation, revdot products, folded in the previous chapter, and wiring, handled here. All of them produce challenges in one field and use them on commitments handled in another. So the next chapter, on endoscalars, looks at how a challenge can cross the curve cycle cheaply.
