Chapter three. Statements and Challenges.

In the previous chapter, a toy computation added up a list of eight numbers on a tree, and each node held a header giving a range of positions and the sum of the entries in that range. Two steps produced headers, Entry from one entry of the list, and Join from two headers whose ranges meet. Attaching to each header a proof that covers its step and every proof below it lets a verifier check one proof for the whole tree. Succinctness asks that this proof, and the cost of checking it, stay small however large the tree grows. Tachyon's aggregation follows the same model, with aggregators merging stamps from independent wallets so that a validator checks one stamp.

Before building any proof, we need to say precisely what a proof is about and what it means for one to work. This chapter supplies that vocabulary.

It also introduces the three tools that every later chapter uses. The Schwartz-Zippel lemma explains why checking a polynomial at one random point tells us about the whole polynomial. Random linear combinations replace many claims with one. The Fiat-Shamir transform then removes the verifier's live participation by deriving its random choices from a hash.

Statements, instances, and witnesses.

Suppose a prover wants to convince a verifier of a claim, which the next chapter uses as its running example. The prover says, I know a number in the field of forty-three elements such that its cube, plus itself, plus five, equals thirty-five.

This claim has a public part and a secret part. The number thirty-five is known to both parties, and we call this public part the instance. The secret number is known only to the prover, and we call this secret part the witness. The rule connecting them is a relation, which is a test that takes an instance and a witness and answers yes or no. Here the relation holds exactly when the cube of the witness, plus the witness, plus five, equals the instance. A statement is then a claim that a given instance has a witness in the relation.

The relation has to be cheap to evaluate, since it is the test the verifier would run if it were handed the witness. Finding a witness from the instance can be much harder. In our example the field is small enough to search. Evaluating the cubic at all forty-three elements finds that it reaches thirty-five only at the number three, so three is the only witness for this instance. The number four gives thirty, so four falls outside the relation. Some instances have no witness at all, and the instance two is one of them.

Later chapters use a second relation, the one behind every elliptic curve commitment. Its instance is a point on a curve, so we first need to say how points combine. We work on the toy curve where y squared equals x cubed plus three, with coordinates computed modulo thirty-one. It has forty-three points.

Points on the toy curve.

To add two points on an elliptic curve, draw the line through them. The line meets the curve at one more point, and reflecting that point across the x-axis gives the sum. Doubling a point uses the tangent line instead. Over a finite field there is no picture, but the same formulas, computed modulo thirty-one, define the addition.

Here is a doubling worked by hand. Take the generator, the point with coordinates one and two. The tangent slope comes out as three quarters, and dividing by four modulo thirty-one means multiplying by eight, so the slope is twenty-four. The formulas then give the point sixteen, ten, which is two times the generator. Both sides of the curve equation equal seven there, so it lies on the curve.

The curve also has an identity point, which plays the role of zero. Forty-two times the generator is the generator reflected across the x-axis. The line through these two points is vertical, so their sum, forty-three times the generator, is the identity.

Before that, the multiples from one to forty-two times the generator visit every other point once. So every point is some number times the generator, and we call that number the point's multiplier. Adding points adds their multipliers, and multiplying a point by a number multiplies its multiplier, both modulo forty-three.

So we can trace any point in this chapter by arithmetic on multipliers, followed by a lookup in a table of all forty-two multiples, while coordinates stay modulo thirty-one. For example, doubling twelve times the generator gives twenty-four times the generator, which the table lists as the point twenty-seven, thirty. The chapter on polynomial commitments returns to these two moduli.

Going from a point back to its multiplier is the discrete log problem, which the table solves only because the curve is tiny.

The discrete log relation.

The instance is a point, the witness is a scalar, and the relation holds when that scalar times a public generator equals the point. Take the public generator and the secret scalar twelve, which give the public point twelve times the generator. The relation holds for twelve, because recomputing twelve times the generator gives the public point. It fails for thirteen, since thirteen times the generator is a different point.

The two relations differ in one respect that affects what a proof must establish. Every point is a multiple of the generator, so a witness for the public point always exists, and the claim that some scalar satisfies the relation tells the verifier nothing. The useful claim is that the prover knows the scalar, and the next section gives this difference a name.

The prover could convince the verifier by sending the witness, and the verifier could run the relation. We want to avoid that for two reasons. The witness may be secret, as the spending keys in a Zcash transaction are. It may also be enormous, because in proof-carrying data the witness for one step includes everything needed to check the previous steps.

These two reasons lead to two goals. For large computations, which is the case proof-carrying data cares about, the proof should be much shorter than the witness. For small secrets, such as the single scalar above, length matters less, and the point of a proof is to convince the verifier without revealing the secret. Later in this chapter we will see a proof that is longer than its one-scalar witness yet reveals nothing about it.

Completeness and soundness.

We judge a proof system by how it treats honest and dishonest provers, and a useful system has to satisfy two requirements at once. Completeness says that a prover with a valid witness who follows the protocol always convinces the verifier. Soundness says that a prover making a false statement rarely convinces the verifier, however it deviates from the protocol. We call a dishonest prover that tries to make the verifier accept a false statement a cheater, and the rest of this site uses the word in that sense.

Each requirement alone is easy to meet. A verifier that accepts everything is complete but not sound, and a verifier that rejects everything is sound but not complete. Sending the witness satisfies both but fails the goals we just stated. So the real work is meeting both requirements with a message that is short, hides the witness, or ideally does both.

We allow soundness to fail with a small probability, called the soundness error. Accepting a small error is what makes short proofs possible, as this chapter shows repeatedly. The error has to be small enough that no one could expect to see it happen, and a large field achieves that, as we will see.

Three refinements of soundness appear in later chapters, and we state them here informally. Knowledge soundness says that a prover who convinces the verifier must actually know a witness. We need this property for the curve relation, where a witness always exists.

Computational soundness says that soundness holds only against provers with realistic computing power. A proof system with this weaker guarantee is called an argument. Ragu's proofs are arguments, because they rest on hardness assumptions that we list near the end of this chapter.

Zero knowledge says that the verifier learns nothing from the proof beyond the truth of the statement. For this site's system it is a goal rather than a requirement, because the previous chapter found that Ragu does not yet achieve it.

This site explains why cheating fails and which assumptions that depends on, but it does not give formal definitions or security proofs.

An interactive proof.

The clearest way to see these properties is to run a protocol. We use Schnorr's protocol for the curve relation, because it is short enough to follow every step. Ragu's proof is a different and much larger protocol, but it has the same shape. The prover commits to something, the verifier answers with randomness, and the prover responds.

The prover wants to show that it knows the secret scalar behind the public point. Sending the scalar would reveal it, so the prover has to send something derived from the scalar that can be checked against the public point. One candidate is the scalar times a number chosen by the verifier, which we'll call the challenge. The verifier can check this against the challenge times the public point. This fails immediately, since the verifier can divide by the challenge and recover the secret.

Adding a random mask repairs this. The prover's response becomes the mask plus the challenge times the secret. The mask hides the product in the same way a one-time pad hides a message. But now the verifier cannot check the response without some information about the mask, so the prover announces the mask times the generator ahead of time. We'll call that the announced point.

Multiplying the response by the generator shows why this is enough. Because scalar multiplication distributes, the response times the generator splits into two pieces. One piece is the mask times the generator, which is the announced point. The other is the challenge times the secret times the generator, which is the challenge times the public point.

So the check is that the response times the generator equals the announced point plus the challenge times the public point. The verifier holds all of those values, so it can run the check. This derivation also establishes completeness, because an honest prover passes for every mask and every challenge.

The protocol has three messages, and their order is part of the protocol. First, the prover picks a random mask and sends the announced point. Second, the verifier sends a random challenge. Third, the prover sends the response, and the verifier runs the check.

Here is one run with our secret twelve. The prover picks the mask twenty-nine and sends the announced point. The verifier sends the challenge sixteen. The prover's response comes to six modulo forty-three, and the verifier computes both sides of the check and gets the same point, six times the generator.

Why the order matters.

The verifier has to send the challenge after receiving the announced point. To see why, rearrange the check to isolate the announced point. It equals the response times the generator, minus the challenge times the public point. So a cheater who knows the challenge ahead of time can pick any response and compute an announced point that makes the equation hold.

Suppose a cheater holds only the public point and learns in advance that the challenge will be sixteen. It picks the response twenty-three first and then computes the matching announced point, which is three times the generator. The check passes, and the cheater never used the secret.

Sending the announced point first removes this option, because that point is then fixed before the challenge exists. Later in the chapter we will see what happens when the same ordering breaks in a non-interactive proof.

Why a cheater fails.

Consider a cheater who sends some announced point and has prepared one response. The check involves one unknown, the challenge, and it holds for exactly one value of the challenge because the public point generates the group. Testing all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine pairs of point and response against all forty-three challenges confirms this. Every pair passes for exactly one challenge, so a cheater with one prepared response succeeds with probability one in forty-three.

A cheater might instead hope to prepare a different response for each challenge. Suppose it could answer two different challenges for the same announced point.

Both answers share the same mask, so comparing them cancels the mask and leaves only the secret. Concretely, the difference of the two responses, divided by the difference of the two challenges, equals the secret. That means anyone who can answer twice can compute the discrete log of the public point. Subtracting one check from the other makes the announced point cancel, which is why the division works.

Let's see that on the toy curve. Keep the same announced point. The prover answers the challenge sixteen with the response six, and it answers the challenge thirty-one with the response fourteen. Modulo forty-three, the difference of the responses is thirty-five and the difference of the challenges is twenty-eight. Dividing thirty-five by twenty-eight gives twelve, which is the secret.

This gives us knowledge soundness in an informal sense. A prover who answers more than one challenge for a given announced point can compute the secret. Anyone who cannot compute the secret is limited to one challenge out of forty-three, which on a Pasta curve is one out of about two to the two hundred fifty-four. The argument assumes that discrete log is hard. On the toy curve anyone can solve discrete log by counting, so these numbers show the mechanism and offer no security.

What the verifier learns.

The response gives away nothing about the secret, because the mask is uniform and is used once. Fix the challenge at sixteen and let the mask range over the field, and the response takes each of the forty-three values exactly once. The mask twenty-nine gives six, for example, and the mask eight gives twenty-eight.

A second way to see this uses a simulator, which is a procedure that produces transcripts without knowing the witness. If the simulator's transcripts look exactly like real ones, then the real ones cannot carry any information about the secret.

So we compare two lists. The first holds the forty-three pairs of announced point and response that an honest prover can produce for this challenge. The second holds the forty-three pairs a simulator produces, by picking the response first and computing the announced point from it. The two lists contain the same pairs.

The comparison shows that the trick that lets a cheater forge when it knows the challenge early also lets anyone produce transcripts with the correct distribution without knowing the secret. A transcript that anyone could have produced cannot teach the verifier anything about the secret. That is the informal reason the protocol is zero knowledge for a verifier who picks its challenge honestly. Reusing a mask would destroy this, since two responses with the same mask reveal the secret through the subtraction we just did.

Counting roots and testing random points.

Schnorr's protocol relied on a linear equation in the challenge, which has one solution. Most protocols on this site check polynomial equations of high degree instead. The reason is that a long list of values can be stored as the coefficients of a polynomial. A claim about the whole list then becomes a claim that two polynomials are equal, and the chapter on polynomial encoding builds such encodings.

To bound how often a false claim of this kind survives a check, we count the roots of polynomials. From here to the end of the chapter, the field can be any finite field, and the field size means its number of elements. The toy examples use the field of forty-three elements.

Each root gives a linear factor.

The counting rests on one algebraic fact. Suppose a polynomial has a root at some point, meaning its value there is zero. Then the variable minus that point divides the polynomial with no remainder. In other words, the polynomial equals the variable minus the root, times a polynomial of degree one lower, so each root accounts for one degree of the polynomial.

The fact holds because every difference of powers, the variable to some power minus the root to the same power, is divisible by the variable minus the root. Subtracting the value at the root, which is zero, turns the polynomial into a sum of such differences. For the second power, this is the familiar difference of squares.

Here is an example over our field. Take a cubic whose value at nine is thirty-seven, and subtract thirty-seven so that nine becomes a root. Dividing by the variable minus nine leaves no remainder. The quotient is a quadratic, one degree less than the cubic.

A bound on the number of roots.

The factorization lets us count roots by removing them one at a time. Dividing out one root leaves a quotient whose degree is one lower. Every other root of the original polynomial is still a root of the quotient, because in a field a product of two nonzero numbers is never zero.

Repeating the division removes one root per step. After at most as many steps as the degree, only a nonzero constant remains, and a nonzero constant has no roots. The result, which the rest of the chapter depends on, is that a nonzero polynomial of a given degree over a field has at most that many roots.

For example, take a cubic whose roots are one, two, and three. Removing each root in turn lowers the degree by one, and the process ends at the constant two, which has no roots. Evaluating the cubic at all forty-three points confirms that its roots are exactly one, two, and three.

The field assumption matters, and the count fails without it. Work modulo eight, which is not a prime. The degree two polynomial, the variable squared minus one, has the four roots one, three, five, and seven, more than its degree allows over a field.

The step that carries the other roots into the quotient breaks here. After removing the root one, the two factors at the point three are two and four. Their product is zero modulo eight, although neither factor is zero.

The bound is a worst case, and most polynomials stay below it. To see how far below, we count the roots of every cubic over the toy field. Multiplying a polynomial by a nonzero constant does not move its roots, so it is enough to count the monic cubics, whose leading coefficient is one.

There are seventy-nine thousand five hundred seven of them. About a third have no roots, about half have exactly one, and none reaches four. Exactly two distinct roots is rare, because a cubic with two distinct roots must repeat one of them. We return to this count shortly, because a cheater does not pick a typical polynomial.

Testing at a random point.

The root count gives a cheap way to check a claim about polynomials. Suppose a prover claims that one polynomial is the product of two others. Checking this directly means multiplying the two out. The schoolbook method multiplies every coefficient of one by every coefficient of the other, so its cost grows with the square of the degree.

A cheaper check picks one point, evaluates all three polynomials there, and compares the product of the first two values with the third. Horner's rule evaluates a polynomial with one multiplication per coefficient, so the cost of this check grows only linearly in the degree. The saving comes from never writing out the product. Two polynomials that are already written out could be compared coefficient by coefficient just as cheaply.

Here is an honest prover's claim, checked at the point nine. Take two cubics over our field. Their product has degree six, and writing it out by the schoolbook method takes sixteen multiplications of coefficients.

At nine, the two cubics give thirty-seven and seven, whose product is one modulo forty-three. The claimed product also gives one at nine, so the claim passes. Horner's rule evaluates the three polynomials with three, three, and six multiplications, and one more multiplies the first two values.

For cubics the two costs are close, but the gap widens as the degree grows, since one cost is quadratic and the other linear.

Now suppose a cheater sends some other polynomial of degree at most six instead of the true product. The test compares the cheater's polynomial with the product of the first two at the chosen point. These agree exactly when the point is a root of their difference, which is the cheater's polynomial minus the true product.

We call that difference the error polynomial. The cheater never computes or sends it, and we name it only so we can reason about when the test passes. Despite the shared word, it differs from the soundness error, which is a probability.

Because the cheater's polynomial differs from the true product, the error polynomial is not zero, and its degree is at most six. By the root count it has at most six roots. So at most six of the forty-three points pass the test.

We can count the passing points for several error polynomials. A nonzero constant error has no roots, so the test never passes, and a linear error passes at one point. The error of the variable squared plus one has no roots in this field, so the test catches it at every point.

A product of three linear factors passes at three points. A product of six linear factors passes at six, which reaches the bound of six in forty-three exactly.

The cheater chooses the error and can give it as many roots as its degree allows, as in that last case. The root count showed that typical cubics have fewer roots than their degree. But a cheater need not pick a typical polynomial, so soundness rests on the worst-case bound rather than on the typical root count.

Nothing in this argument used the fact that one side was a product, so the same reasoning applies to any two polynomials. This is the one-variable case of the Schwartz-Zippel lemma. Take two different polynomials of degree at most some bound, and choose a point uniformly from the field. The chance that they agree there is at most that degree divided by the field size.

The proof repeats the product argument with the difference of the two polynomials in place of the error. That difference is nonzero with degree at most the bound, and the point passes only when it lands on one of its roots. In the product example the degree is six and the field size is forty-three, which gives the bound of six in forty-three.

When the bound applies.

Two conditions make the bound meaningful. The first is that the polynomials must be fixed before the point is chosen, for the same reason as the ordering in Schnorr's protocol. The bound counts the roots of a fixed polynomial, but a prover who sees the point first can build a polynomial with a root there.

If the prover knows the point nine in advance, it sends the true product plus the variable minus nine. This differs from the true product, yet it also gives one at nine, so the test passes. The chapter on polynomial commitments builds the tool that fixes a polynomial without revealing it.

The second condition is that the degree must be well below the field size. The variable to the forty-third power, minus the variable, is not the zero polynomial, yet it evaluates to zero at all forty-three points of our field. Its bound is forty-three over forty-three, which is one and promises nothing.

Real fields leave plenty of room. With degree two to the thirteen over a field with more than two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, such as either Pasta field, the probability is below two to the minus two hundred forty-one.

Polynomials in several variables.

The checks in later chapters often involve more than one challenge, which turns the polynomial being tested into a function of several variables. The consolidated equation in the next chapter, for example, uses two challenges. So we need the same kind of bound for several variables.

The relevant size measure is the total degree, the largest sum of exponents among the monomials. X squared times Y, for instance, has total degree three. The Schwartz-Zippel lemma covers a nonzero polynomial in several variables, with each variable chosen uniformly and independently from the field. It says the chance of a zero is at most the total degree divided by the field size.

The bound may look surprising, because it ignores the number of variables. The reason is that the variables can be treated one at a time. Call the variables X and Y, and write the polynomial as a polynomial in X whose coefficients are polynomials in Y. Take the coefficient of the highest power of X, and call it the leading coefficient.

In one case, the chosen Y is a root of the leading coefficient. Its degree is at most the total degree minus that power of X, so this case covers at most that many values of Y. In the other case, fixing Y leaves a nonzero polynomial in X whose degree is that power, so it has at most that many roots.

The two chances add up, the power of X cancels, and the total is the total degree over the field size. Repeating the step one variable at a time handles any number of variables.

To check the bound on the toy field, we count zeros instead of measuring probabilities. The grid of pairs has forty-three times forty-three points. So a probability bound of the total degree over forty-three allows at most the total degree times forty-three zeros, which we call the limit.

Picture both cases on that grid, using the polynomial Y minus five, times X squared minus Y. The only bad row is the row where Y equals five, and all forty-three of its points are zeros. The other forty-two rows hold at most two zeros each, tracing a parabola, and they contribute forty-three zeros in total. The sum of eighty-six stays below the limit of one hundred twenty-nine.

The polynomial X minus one, times X minus two, reaches its limit exactly. It ignores Y and vanishes on two whole columns of the grid, which gives eighty-six zeros against a limit of eighty-six. So the bound cannot be improved in general.

To test every case rather than selected ones, we can move to the field of five elements. All fifteen thousand six hundred twenty-four nonzero polynomials of total degree at most two stay within the limit of ten zeros, and two hundred forty of them reach it.

Random linear combinations.

A proof system has to check a large number of equations, since a circuit has thousands of gates and each one contributes a claim that the difference between its two sides equals zero. We call that difference the error of the claim, by analogy with the error polynomial. A proof that handled every equation separately would be very long. So we want one equation that fails whenever any of the original equations fails.

Adding the errors does not work, because they can cancel. The errors one and forty-two are both nonzero, but they add to zero modulo forty-three. Fixed weights known in advance do no better. With the weights one and two, a cheater picks the errors two and forty-two, and the weighted sum is again zero modulo forty-three.

The remedy for both is to choose the weights at random after the errors are fixed, so that the cheater has nothing to plan around. There are two standard ways to do this.

In the first, the verifier picks a separate uniform weight for each claim and checks that the weighted sum is zero. Suppose one of the errors is nonzero, and fix all the other weights to any values. The check then becomes a linear equation in the remaining weight, with exactly one solution. A uniform weight hits that solution with probability exactly one over the field size, however many claims there are.

Take the errors six, five, and one. Trying every choice of three weights finds that exactly one choice in forty-three gives a zero combination.

Independent weights require one random value per claim. In the second approach, a single challenge stands in for all of them, by using its powers as the weights. The weighted sum is now a polynomial in the challenge whose coefficients are the errors. If any error is nonzero, this polynomial is nonzero, with degree at most one less than the number of claims. So it has at most that many roots, and the root count bounds the soundness error by that degree over the field size.

The verifier sends one field element rather than one per claim, and the cost is a larger soundness error that a large field absorbs. The next chapter applies this form to circuit gates and constraints.

All nonzero error lists of length three are few enough to enumerate. None is hidden by more than two of the forty-three challenges, which agrees with the bound of two in forty-three. The errors six, five, and one are hidden by two values in this form, forty and forty-one.

Combining evaluation claims.

The same technique combines claims about polynomials. Suppose a prover claims a value for each of two polynomials at the same point. Evaluation is linear in the coefficients, so the first polynomial plus a random multiple of the second evaluates to the first value plus that multiple of the second value. The verifier can therefore pick the multiplier and test one combined claim. The errors in the two claims combine in exactly the two-claim form of the check we just saw.

Here the true values at the point nine are thirty-seven and seven, and a cheater claims thirty-eight and nine, with errors one and two. At the multiplier six, the combined polynomial evaluates to thirty-six while the combined claim comes to six, so the check fails. Only the multiplier twenty-one hides these errors, because that is the single solution of one plus two times the multiplier equals zero.

As with every challenge, the errors must be fixed before the multiplier is known. A cheater who sees the multiplier six first keeps the second error at two and solves for a first error of thirty-one. It then claims twenty-five and nine. Both claims are false, yet the combined claim comes to thirty-six, which matches the combined polynomial's true value.

The technique applies wherever claims combine linearly, which includes the curve points in Pedersen commitments. The chapters on batched openings, accumulation, and folding all build on it. In linear algebra terms, one row replaces a system of equations, because a random list of weights combines the rows.

Removing the verifier with Fiat-Shamir.

Interaction is a problem for proof-carrying data. A proof has to travel with its data and convince anyone who later receives it, including parties that did not exist when the prover ran. A live verifier cannot do that. A recording of an interactive run does not help either, because we saw that anyone can write a valid transcript once the challenge is known in advance.

The observation behind the Fiat-Shamir transform is that the verifier in these protocols contributes nothing but random values. Protocols with this property are called public coin, since the verifier's coins are visible and it keeps no secrets. The challenge needs only two properties, which are that it looks random and that nobody can predict it before the prover's message is fixed. A hash of the prover's message has both properties, provided the hash behaves like a random function.

So the transform replaces each verifier message with a hash of everything sent so far. We keep this running record in an object called a transcript, with two operations. Absorbing adds a value to the record, and squeezing produces a challenge that depends on everything absorbed so far. Prover and verifier run the same sequence of operations, so they obtain the same challenges.

Every transcript starts with a tag, a fixed label that names the protocol, and a later section explains why the label is needed. Our toy transcript works with elements of the field of forty-three elements, so the tag has to be written as field elements too. We encode the toy protocol's name as the pair eleven and twenty-two, which is an arbitrary choice.

The transcript should also give different challenges to different sequences of messages. Suppose a message, and the same message followed by a zero, produced the same challenge. A prover could then fix the challenge first and decide afterward which of the two messages it had sent. That is the early-challenge attack on Schnorr's protocol in another form.

General-purpose transcripts rule this out by absorbing the length of every message before the message itself. Our toy transcript follows Ragu, which prefixes only the tag. So it absorbs the tag's length, two, before the tag, and absorbs the later values without a length. The section on Ragu's transcript explains why Ragu can safely drop the other prefixes.

For Schnorr's protocol, the sequence has four parts. Start the transcript with the length of the tag and then the tag. Absorb the public point. Absorb the announced point. Then squeeze the challenge.

The toy examples use a small hash over the field of forty-three elements, which we'll describe shortly. It absorbs a curve point as its two coordinates. Each coordinate is below thirty-one, so it is also a distinct element of the field of forty-three elements, and the output is a scalar for the toy curve. On real curves, moving values between the two fields of a curve cycle takes more care, and the chapters on curve cycles and endoscalars cover that step.

Let's run it. The prover starts the transcript with the tag and the public point, computes the announced point with mask twenty-nine, and absorbs it. Squeezing gives the challenge ten, and the response comes to twenty modulo forty-three. The prover publishes the announced point and the response as the proof. The verifier replays the same transcript steps, gets the challenge ten, and confirms the equation, with both sides equal to twenty times the generator.

The proof is now a single message that anyone can check at any time. The verifier no longer picks anything, and it instead recomputes the challenge and confirms the equation.

The challenge depends on the commitment, which restores the ordering Schnorr's protocol needs. A prover cannot choose the announced point after the challenge, because a different point produces a different challenge. Changing the announced point in our run to the next table entry, thirty times the generator, moves the challenge from ten to five, and the old response no longer satisfies the check.

Later challenges also depend on all earlier messages, because the transcript keeps its state between squeezes. Suppose the protocol had a later round, which absorbed the response and squeezed a second challenge. With the same response in both runs, that challenge would be three after the original point and twenty-nine after the changed one.

Omitting data from the transcript.

The transform is safe only when the transcript includes everything the verifier would have seen before choosing the challenge. Leaving out a value lets a prover choose that value after seeing the challenge, which is the earlier attack in a new form. These mistakes have happened in deployed code. Trail of Bits named the class Frozen Heart in its disclosure of such flaws in implementations of Girault's proof of knowledge, Bulletproofs, and PLONK.

Suppose first that the transcript absorbs the tag and the public point but not the announced point. The challenge is then fixed before the commitment exists, so the early-challenge forgery works directly, with no search. Our flawed transcript gives the challenge thirty-nine. The cheater picks the response thirty-one, solves for the announced point, thirty-six times the generator, and both sides of the check agree. The flawed verifier accepts. A verifier that also absorbs the announced point derives the challenge twenty-six instead and rejects the same proof.

This breaks the protocol completely, since a cheater can forge a proof for any instance. The same mistake in a polynomial protocol has the same effect. If the evaluation point does not depend on a polynomial's commitment, the prover can change the polynomial to fit the point.

Suppose instead that the transcript absorbs the announced point but not the public point. Bernhard, Pereira, and Warinschi call this variant weak Fiat-Shamir and examine its pitfalls. The cheater can no longer choose the announced point after the challenge, but it can now choose the statement afterward, by rearranging the check to solve for the public point.

The cheater has to take the announced point from somewhere other than its own mask. If it used a mask it knew, the solved public point would be a known multiple of the generator. The cheater would then know the witness, and the proof would be honest. So the forgery needs an announced point whose discrete log the cheater does not know.

Proof systems publish a list of fixed points alongside the generator, chosen so that nobody knows their discrete logs relative to it. The chapter on polynomial commitments builds such a list for the toy curve, and a real system arranges this property by deriving the points from a hash. On a curve with forty-three points anyone could find those discrete logs by counting, so the toy list only imitates this property.

On the toy curve, the cheater takes one of those public points as its announced point. The flawed transcript gives the challenge fifteen without looking at any instance. Picking the response nineteen and solving gives a new public point, and the check holds.

The cheater now has a valid proof for that point. The construction used only public points, so the cheater does not know the new point's discrete log, and the proof claims knowledge it lacks. The cheater cannot aim at a specific point this way, because for a chosen point, finding a response that passes is a discrete log problem.

A weak transcript also lets an observer adapt someone else's proof to a related statement, and in that case the observer does choose the target. The challenge does not change when the instance changes. So the observer can add a multiple of the generator to the public point and add the challenge times that multiple to the response, and the equation still balances.

Suppose an honest prover publishes the response thirty-five under the flawed transcript, whose challenge is twenty-two. An observer shifts the public point by seven times the generator and computes a new response of thirty-five plus twenty-two times seven, which is seventeen modulo forty-three. The new response passes for the shifted point, with both sides of the check equal to seventeen times the generator.

The witness for that point is the secret plus seven, and the observer does not know it because it does not know the secret. A verifier would nevertheless accept this proof as evidence that someone does.

Tags address a less direct problem. If two protocols use the same message format, a proof made for one would also pass in the other, even when the two assign different meanings to the same equation. Starting the transcript with a tag gives each protocol its own stream of challenges, a practice called domain separation. In our run, changing the tag from eleven and twenty-two to eleven and twenty-four moves the challenge from ten to twelve, and the response twenty fails the check.

Our rule follows from these three cases. The transcript has to include the protocol tag, all public parameters that the tag does not already fix, the full instance, every prover message, and all earlier challenges. The transcript state includes those earlier challenges automatically.

Repeated trials.

The transform also changes the soundness error in a way that is easy to miss. In an interactive run, a cheater gets one attempt, because the verifier sees each failure. In a non-interactive proof, a cheater can try as many transcripts as it likes on its own machine and publish only one that works. This is called grinding.

The toy field makes grinding visible, because each attempt has a chance of about one in forty-three. A cheater repeats the early-challenge trick. It guesses a challenge, picks a response, computes the announced point, and tests whether the hash of the transcript returns its guess.

The cheater tries guesses from forty-two downward and, for each guess, responses from forty-two downward. It skips any pair that gives the identity as the announced point, because the identity has no coordinates for the transcript to absorb. Attempt nineteen succeeds, with the guess forty-two, the response twenty-three, and the announced point thirty-five times the generator. The honest verifier accepts this proof, and the search never touched the secret.

Across all one thousand eight hundred six usable pairs, exactly forty-two succeed, which is one attempt in forty-three. So a cheater with many attempts has a success probability of up to about the number of attempts times the soundness error of a single attempt. The defense is a field large enough for that product to remain tiny.

Suppose a cheater can compute two to the one hundred twenty-eight hashes, and each attempt faces the soundness error of the random-point test, with degree two to the thirteen and a field above two to the two hundred fifty-four. Each attempt succeeds with probability below two to the minus two hundred forty-one, and all attempts together succeed with probability below two to the minus one hundred thirteen.

These numbers only illustrate how grinding scales, and we make no claim here about the overall security level of Ragu. The Ragu Book raises the same point in one of its discussions, noting that a complete accounting has to include an adversary's repeated Fiat-Shamir trials.

Poseidon and the random oracle assumption.

Fiat-Shamir needs a hash function, and the choice matters more in recursive proofs than elsewhere. In a recursive proof, the verifier's work runs inside a circuit, so each challenge must be recomputed inside a circuit too. The next chapter explains that a circuit's main cost is its number of field multiplications.

A hash such as S H A two five six works with bits and thirty-two bit words, and expressing those operations as field equations takes tens of thousands of constraints for each compression. Ragu instead uses Poseidon, a hash designed to work directly with field elements and to need few multiplications.

Poseidon is built from a permutation of a small array of field elements, called the state. A construction called a sponge turns this permutation into a transcript. The state starts at zero and is split into rate cells, which interact with the outside, and capacity cells, which do not.

Absorbing adds input into the rate cells. Once they are full, or when the prover wants a challenge, the sponge applies the permutation. Squeezing reads values out of the rate cells and applies the permutation again when they run out. The capacity cells prevent a prover from controlling the whole state, because input can reach them only through the permutation.

The permutation.

Each round of the permutation performs three operations on the state. First, it adds a fixed round constant to every cell, which makes the rounds differ and blocks attacks that exploit repeated structure. Second, it applies the S-box, which raises a cell to the fifth power, to some of the cells. This is the only nonlinear step, and it provides the mixing that linear algebra cannot undo. Third, it multiplies the state by a fixed invertible matrix, which spreads each cell's influence to all the others.

The exponent has to keep the S-box invertible, because otherwise a round would lose information. Five is the smallest exponent above one that does so on the fields Ragu uses. Over the field of forty-three elements, for instance, squaring takes only twenty-two distinct values and cubing only fifteen, while the fifth power takes all forty-three.

Computing a fifth power takes three multiplications, which are the square, then the fourth power, then the fourth power times the original. So the S-box is the part of Poseidon that costs gates, which suggests applying it sparingly. Poseidon does so by running two kinds of rounds. Full rounds apply the S-box to every cell, and partial rounds apply it to the first cell only.

A permutation places half of its full rounds at the start, all of its partial rounds in the middle, and the remaining full rounds at the end. The full rounds at each end protect against statistical attacks, which track how differences in the input spread through the state. The cheaper partial rounds in the middle raise the algebraic degree of the permutation.

A toy Poseidon.

Our examples use a small instance over the field of forty-three elements, with three cells, of which two are rate cells, along with eight full rounds and eight partial rounds. We derive its round constants from S H A two five six and use a fixed invertible matrix for the mixing step.

This instance has the structure of Poseidon and none of its security, because its parameters were chosen to fit on a page and its outputs have only forty-three possible values. Tracing one round of each kind shows that the S-box changes every cell in a full round but only the first cell in a partial round.

Running the sponge over the whole transcript from our Fiat-Shamir example absorbs seven elements, which are the tag's length, the two tag elements, and the coordinates of the two points. That takes four permutations, the last of which runs when the prover squeezes. The first entry of the resulting state is the challenge ten we saw earlier.

The assumption.

The Fiat-Shamir argument treats the hash as a random oracle, an imaginary function that returns an independent uniform value for each new input. In that model, a prover has no way to predict a challenge other than computing the hash on the full transcript, and the challenges behave as a verifier's random choices would. The Ragu Book models Poseidon this way.

This assumption is stronger than collision resistance. A Pedersen commitment, for example, is collision resistant under the discrete log assumption, yet it would make a poor source of challenges. Its output moves predictably when its input changes, and a prover could exploit that structure to steer a challenge. A random oracle has no structure of this kind.

No real hash function is a random oracle, because a real hash has a short public description that anyone can analyze. So the assumption is a modeling choice with known limits. Goldwasser and Tauman built a secure identification scheme whose Fiat-Shamir signature version is secure with a random oracle but insecure under every concrete hash. More recently, Khovratovich, Rothblum, and Soukhanov gave a practical attack on a G K R based argument made non-interactive with a concrete hash, producing accepting proofs of false statements.

Neither result targets Ragu, and both show that the assumption needs scrutiny for each protocol. Confidence in Poseidon specifically comes from public cryptanalysis rather than from a proof.

Assumptions behind the protocols.

The Ragu Book lists three assumptions, and later chapters refer back to them. The first is the discrete log relation assumption, which says nobody can find a nontrivial linear relation among independently chosen curve generators. The chapter on polynomial commitments uses this for binding.

The second is the random oracle assumption, which says Poseidon behaves like a random oracle when deriving Fiat-Shamir challenges. The third is knowledge soundness, which says an efficient prover who produces a valid commitment and evaluation proof knows the underlying polynomial. This is the same kind of property we saw in Schnorr's protocol, applied to the polynomial commitment scheme.

The transcript in Ragu.

Ragu's transcript has the structure we've described, with a few differences that come from its goal of keeping in-circuit hashing cheap. The operation that combines two proofs is the one the previous chapter called fuse.

The next few points are reconstructed from the code at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed them. The transcript wraps a Poseidon sponge over the circuit field. A comment in the test vectors states that Ragu's instance has five cells with a rate of four. A test fixes the cost of one permutation with these parameters at two hundred eighty-eight gates.

The transcript constructor absorbs the length of the tag and then the tag itself in sixteen byte chunks, as our toy transcript does with its two-element tag. There are two tags, ragu P C D v one for the fuse protocol and ragu I P A v one for compression. The book explains that these tags version the protocol, so proofs made under an earlier tag stop verifying under a later one.

The fuse prover alternates between absorbing one commitment and squeezing one or two challenges, about nine times in all. This is the pattern of our Fiat-Shamir example repeated. The chapter on recursion in Ragu lists each commitment and challenge once the objects behind them have been defined.

Absorption without length prefixes.

This point is also reconstructed from the code at the pinned commit and not confirmed by the authors. We saw earlier why a transcript must not give a message, and the same message followed by a zero, the same challenge. Ragu's sponge adds input to the state and records no message lengths, so a trailing zero in the buffer has no effect on the result.

We can see this on the toy sponge, which follows the same absorption rule. Absorb the seven elements from our transcript. The seventh waits in the buffer alone, so absorbing a zero after it leaves the sum unchanged, and both runs squeeze the challenge ten.

Ragu prefixes only the tag, and its source comments give efficiency as the reason. The code argues that this is safe because the circuit code fixes how many elements are absorbed at each point and the verifier replays that exact sequence. So the verifier never accepts a message with a trailing zero where the protocol expects the message alone, and protocols with different sequences have different tags.

An honest prover that retries.

This point, too, is reconstructed from the code at the pinned commit and not confirmed by the authors. We treated repeated attempts as a cheater's tool, but the fuse prover also retries one challenge. It re-randomizes a commitment and squeezes again until the challenge falls in a range that a later step requires. The chapter on endoscalars explains that requirement.

The Ragu Book and the code differ here. The book's transcript chapter says that Ragu uses the permutation as a fixed-length hash rather than as a full sponge. The code at the pinned commit has a sponge type that alternates absorbing and squeezing over one protocol run, and its documentation restricts it to fixed-length inputs. We read the book as describing the absence of length handling, and this site says sponge, following the code.

Ragu and Tachyon are both still changing. Tachyon has not activated on mainnet, and the details here describe one commit rather than a settled specification.

Where this leaves us.

Let's collect the steps. A statement claims that a public instance has a secret witness in a relation, and a proof system has to be complete and sound, with a small soundness error. Schnorr's protocol shows the pattern of commit, challenge, and response, and it shows why the challenge has to come after the commitment.

A nonzero polynomial over a field has at most as many roots as its degree, so two different low-degree polynomials rarely agree at a random point. The Schwartz-Zippel lemma extends this to several variables, with a bound of the total degree over the field size. A random linear combination, usually built from powers of one challenge, replaces many claims with one.

The Fiat-Shamir transform replaces the verifier's challenges with hashes of a transcript, which must include all public data and every prover message. Non-interactive proofs allow repeated trials, so the soundness error has to remain small after multiplying by the number of hashes a cheater can compute. Ragu derives its challenges with Poseidon, which it models as a random oracle.

We still have no way of expressing a real computation as a polynomial claim. The next chapter takes the statement we began with and rewrites it as equations over a field, ending with a single equation that the tools from this chapter can check.
