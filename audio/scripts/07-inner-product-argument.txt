Chapter seven. The Inner Product Argument.

The last chapter reduced a polynomial evaluation to one statement about a secret list, and it finished the exchange by revealing that list. This chapter replaces the reveal with a proof whose size grows with the logarithm of the list's length. The idea is to turn a claim about a list into a claim of the same shape about a list half as long, and to repeat this until one scalar is left, which the prover can send.

We derive one halving round in full, run the argument by hand on the toy curve, and then look at what cheating would take and what the verifier pays. The last sections compare this textbook form with the version in Ragu's code, which differs in several visible ways while proving the same claim.

The claim to prove.

The last chapter ended with this statement. The prover claims to know a list such that the statement point equals that list's commitment, plus its inner product with the powers of the evaluation point, times the extra generator U. The halving step never uses the fact that the second list consists of powers. So until the powers matter again, we'll call it the public list, and we'll call the prover's list the secret list.

The verifier knows the generators, the public list, U, and the statement point, and only the prover knows the secret list. We keep the running example, the polynomial with coefficients seven, two, five, three, evaluated at nine. The public list of powers is one, nine, thirty-eight, forty-one, and the claimed value is thirty-seven. The statement point is the commitment plus thirty-seven copies of U.

The general Bulletproofs relation also has a secret second list with its own generators. The Ragu Book states that form and then sets those generators to zero for polynomial openings, because the powers are public, and we use only the reduced form. The book also writes f and Q where this site uses the secret list and U.

Halving produces cross terms.

To shorten the secret list, the prover has to combine its entries. A natural way is to split the list and the generators into a lower and an upper half, and add the halves together.

Now compare the commitment before and after. The original commitment pairs each half of the list with its own half of the generators. The shortened lists pair every half with every other half, because the inner product distributes over the sums. So the new point has the two original terms plus two extra ones, which are called cross terms, and they stand between the short lists and the original commitment.

Here we switch to the second list from the last chapter, three, one, eight, four, because the running list happens to have an upper-with-upper term equal to the identity point, which would hide the effect. The halves of three, one, eight, four add to eleven, five. Paired with the summed generators, that lands on the sum of all four pieces, which is a different point from the commitment.

The cross terms depend on the secret list, so the verifier cannot compute them, and the prover has to send them. The statement also carries the claimed value as a multiple of U, and if the public list is split and summed the same way, that inner product splits into the same four pieces. So the prover sends two points. The point L pairs the upper half of the secret list with the lower halves of the generators and the public list. The point R pairs the lower half with the upper halves. With plain sums, the shortened statement is the statement point plus L plus R.

This creates a new problem. The verifier cannot check how the prover built L and R, and in particular the prover is free to choose their U parts. If the way the halves are combined is fixed and known in advance, the prover can use this freedom to hide a false value.

Here is how, on the running example. A prover claims thirty-eight instead of thirty-seven, so its statement point carries one extra U. It sends the honest L, but in place of the honest R it sends R minus one U. The extra U and the missing U cancel, so the verifier ends up holding the same point it would hold for the true claim, and the prover continues honestly from there.

The prover could make this correction because it knew the weight on R before it sent R. The fix is to weight the halves with a random challenge that the verifier picks after the prover has sent the cross terms, so that the prover cannot tailor them to the weights.

Choosing the weights.

The weights must not disturb the two terms that make up the original commitment. Suppose we scale the upper half of the list by one scalar and the upper half of the generators by another. Expanding as before, the upper-with-upper term picks up the product of the two scalars. For that term to keep weight one, the two scalars must be inverses of each other.

This is why inverses of the challenge appear throughout the argument. One half of the pair goes on the scalars and the other goes on the generators, and they cancel exactly on the terms we want to keep.

Take the weight three, whose inverse modulo forty-three is twenty-nine. If we scale both upper halves by three and remove the cross pieces, the upper-with-upper term carries a nine, and we miss the commitment. If instead we scale the upper list by twenty-nine and the upper generators by three, removing the cross pieces leaves exactly the commitment.

The scale of each half is also free, as long as the product over a matching pair stays one. Like Bulletproofs and the Ragu Book, this chapter spreads a challenge over both halves. The secret list gets the challenge on its lower half and the inverse on its upper half, and the generators get the reverse. We use this symmetric form because the book uses it, and because a polynomial that later chapters rely on comes from it. Ragu's code runs a one-sided form, which we return to later.

One round of folding.

The public list pairs with the secret list the same way the generators do, so it needs the same weights as the generators. So a round works like this. The new secret list is the challenge times the lower half, plus the inverse times the upper half. The new public list and the new generators are each the inverse times the lower half, plus the challenge times the upper half.

Expanding the new commitment term by term gives four pieces. On lower-with-lower and upper-with-upper, the challenge and its inverse cancel, which leaves the original commitment. The lower list with the upper generators picks up the challenge squared, and the upper list with the lower generators picks up the inverse squared. The new inner product expands in exactly the same way.

Now form the new statement point and collect terms by their power of the challenge. The terms with no challenge give back the old statement point. The terms weighted by the inverse squared form exactly L, and the terms weighted by the square form exactly R. So the new statement point is the old one, plus the inverse square times L, plus the square times R.

The verifier can compute this once it has L and R, and it can fold the generators and the public list itself, because they are public. Only the new secret list stays with the prover. The result is a new claim with the same shape as the old one, at half the length. The order of the messages matters, so the prover sends L and R first, and only then does the verifier send the challenge.

A complete run on the toy curve.

A list of four entries takes two rounds. We pick the challenges by hand so that the numbers avoid accidental coincidences, while in a real proof Fiat-Shamir would derive them from a transcript, as chapter three describes.

In the first round, the prover computes the two cross inner products from the secret list. The upper half five, three against the lower powers one, nine gives thirty-two. The lower half seven, two against the upper powers gives four. The prover uses these to compute L and R, and sends them.

The verifier then sends the challenge fifteen. Its inverse is twenty-three, so L gets the weight thirteen and R gets the weight ten. The secret list folds to five, thirteen, and the public list folds to thirty-four, five. The verifier computes the new statement point from public values, and the prover reaches the same point from its folded list. The inner product changes as predicted, since thirty-seven plus thirteen times thirty-two plus ten times four is twenty modulo forty-three.

The second round repeats these steps with one entry per half. The cross inner products are twelve and twenty-five, and the challenge ten gives the weights forty and fourteen. The secret list folds to four, the public list folds to nineteen, and again both ways of computing the statement point agree.

All three lists now have one entry, which we'll call the final scalar, the final public entry, and the final generator. The claim becomes that the statement point equals the final scalar times the sum of the final generator and the final public entry times U. The prover sends the scalar four. The verifier already holds nineteen and the final generator from folding, checks this one equation, and accepts.

Unrolling the update rule puts the whole check in one line, which is how an implementation evaluates it. The verifier adds each round's weighted L and R to the starting statement point, and compares the result with the final scalar's side.

The whole proof here is four points and one scalar, against the four scalars of revealing the list. The saving only appears at larger lengths, because the proof grows with the logarithm of the length. This site counts rounds upward from one, while the Ragu Book and the code label rounds differently, and a later section lines up the labels.

Why cheating fails.

The verifier never sees the secret list, so we need a reason to believe that a false claim cannot pass. Start with a prover who claims thirty-eight and otherwise folds honestly. Across all one thousand seven hundred sixty-four pairs of nonzero challenges, the verifier rejects every time, because the extra copy of U stays in the folded point in every round.

A cheater can do more than this, because L and R can be any points it likes. To see the limits of that freedom, we track one number through the protocol.

The gap.

Assume for now that the prover builds every point from the public generators and knows the coefficients it used, and that nobody knows a relation among the generators. Then each point has one expression, a combination of the generators plus a coefficient on U. We define the gap of a point as its coefficient on U, minus the inner product of its generator coefficients with the public list.

The claim holds exactly when the statement point has gap zero. For the running example, the honest statement point has gap thirty-seven minus thirty-seven, which is zero. The false statement point for thirty-eight has the same generator coefficients, so its gap is one.

The gap is linear, which means the gap of a combination of points is the same combination of their gaps. Each generator's gap is minus its entry in the public list, and the gap of U is one.

Now look at the right side of the final check. By linearity, a folded generator has minus the folded public entry as its gap, in every round. So the final generator's gap is minus the final public entry, and adding the final public entry times U brings it back to zero. The right side therefore has gap zero, whatever scalar the prover sends.

With no relation among the generators, two equal points have equal coefficients, and so equal gaps. A passing proof therefore needs the left side to have gap zero as well. That means the statement point's gap, plus every weighted gap of each L and R, must sum to zero.

Counting the challenges a cheater survives.

Suppose a round starts with a nonzero gap. The prover fixes L and R, and then the verifier picks the challenge. Writing w for the challenge squared, the new gap is zero only if the old gap, plus L's gap divided by w, plus R's gap times w, comes to zero.

Multiplying through by w gives a quadratic in w whose middle coefficient is the old gap. That coefficient is nonzero, so the polynomial is not zero, whatever the prover chose. By the root count from chapter three, it has at most two roots, and each root has at most two square roots. So at most four challenges in a round can clear a nonzero gap, for every list length.

The gap argument can look abstract, so we check it against the final check itself in the smallest case, a list of length two with one round. Let the prover build the statement point, L, and R from any coefficients it likes. With no known relation among the two generators and U, the final check holds only if the coefficients match one generator at a time, which gives three equations.

The prover sends the final scalar after it sees the challenge, so it can pick that scalar to satisfy one equation. To see what remains, multiply each generator's equation by its public entry and add them. The right side of the sum equals the right side of the U equation, so subtracting the sum from the U equation removes the final scalar completely.

What is left is exactly the gap equation from before. Whatever scalar the prover sends, a passing challenge must solve it. The two generator equations must also agree on the scalar, which can only remove more challenges, so the gap equation is a necessary condition for passing.

Here is a numeric case. Take the secret list seven, two and the public list one, nine, whose true inner product is twenty-five, with the false claim twenty-six, so the statement point has gap one. With the honest generator parts in L and R, the two generator equations never conflict, and only the gap equation decides. The honest U parts give L and R gap zero, so the gap equation reads one equals zero, and no challenge passes.

A cheater can choose other U parts. With U parts one and four, the gaps of L and R are forty-two and twenty-seven modulo forty-three. The quadratic has the roots ten and twenty-five, and both are squares, with square roots five, fifteen, twenty-eight, and thirty-eight. Running the check for all forty-two nonzero challenges confirms that exactly these four pass. Across all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine choices of the two U parts, none passes more than four.

A false statement starts with a nonzero gap and must clear it in one of the rounds. The chance is at most four times the number of rounds, divided by the number of nonzero challenges. On the toy curve that is a large fraction, which is one more way the toy curve is insecure. For a list of two to the thirteen entries over a Pasta scalar field, which has about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, the bound is fifty-two divided by that size. That is below two to the minus two hundred forty-eight.

Why the challenge follows the cross terms.

The count relies on L and R being fixed before the challenge. If the prover sees the challenge first, it can solve the equation for the point it still has to send. This is the earlier fixed-weight attack, with the known weight of the challenge squared in place of the weight one.

Suppose the prover knows the first challenge is fifteen before it sends R, and it wants to pass with the false claim of thirty-eight. It subtracts the challenge's inverse square times U from the honest R. The weight on R turns that change into exactly minus one U, so the folded point comes out equal to the honest one, and the verifier accepts.

The same altered R does poorly when the challenge arrives afterwards. Across all forty-two nonzero challenges, the verifier accepts only at fifteen and twenty-eight, the guess and its negative, which share the same square. The prover wins with a chance of two in forty-two. The gap equation predicts exactly this. The statement point has gap one, the altered R has gap thirty, and L has gap zero, which forces the challenge's square to be ten.

In a non-interactive proof, this ordering becomes a requirement on the transcript. Every L and R must go into the hash before its challenge comes out, for the reason chapter three gives about omitting data from the transcript.

Why the generators need no relation.

The gap only makes sense if each point has one expression in the generators. On the toy curve every point is a known multiple of every other point, so the final scalar can absorb any error. Keep the false claim of thirty-eight and the honest L and R, with challenges fifteen and ten. Counting shows the folded left side is sixteen times the first generator, and the point the final scalar multiplies is thirty-five times it.

The prover divides these logs to get the final scalar forty-one, and the verifier accepts the false value. This is the attack the discrete log relation assumption rules out on a real curve.

So the argument rests on three assumptions. The discrete log relation assumption holds for the generators together with U. The prover builds its points from the generators with coefficients it knows. And the challenges are uniform and come after the points they weight, which in a non-interactive proof means treating the transcript hash as a random oracle. This reasoning is informal, and it is not a security proof.

Two limits of this argument.

The first limit is a real weakness of the textbook protocol, which accepts some false evaluations. The argument shows only that the statement point has the right form for some secret list. The last chapter showed that the textbook statement has a gap when the commitment itself contains a multiple of U, and the halving argument inherits that gap unchanged.

An honest commitment never contains a multiple of U, since it is built from the generators alone. But a cheating prover can hide some copies of U in its commitment and claim a value lower by the same amount. The statement point then has gap zero, so the protocol accepts every time, even though the polynomial behind the generator part does not take the claimed value. A later section runs this attack with numbers and shows how the code closes the hole.

The second limit concerns what sound means. The argument shows that a false statement fails. Recursion needs a stronger property, which chapter two called knowledge soundness, saying that a prover who passes actually knows a suitable secret list. This chapter assumes that property and does not argue it.

The verifier's linear cost.

The proof is short, but the verifier still has to compute the final generator and the final public entry. Folding the generators round by round costs about one scalar multiplication per generator in the first round, half that in the second, and so on. The verifier can do better by computing the final point in one step, if it knows each original generator's final weight.

For a list of four, substitute the first round into the second and multiply out. Each original generator ends up with a weight that has one factor per round. A round contributes its challenge if the generator sat in the upper half at that round, and the inverse if it sat in the lower half. Read a generator's position in binary, and the first round looks at the highest bit.

These products form what we'll call the weight list. Its inner product with the generators gives the final generator, and, because the public list folds with the same weights, its inner product with the public list gives the final public entry. In our run the weight list is forty-one, fifteen, twenty-three, twenty-one, and both results match the round-by-round folding.

Computing the final generator this way is one multi-scalar multiplication, written MSM, with one term per generator. It is cheaper than folding, because it can share work across its terms, but its cost still grows linearly with the length. No shortcut is available, because the generators are independent points with no structure to exploit.

The polynomial g of X.

The final public entry is cheaper, because the public list has structure that the generators lack. Its entries are powers of the evaluation point, so each upper entry equals the matching lower entry times the point raised to half the length. Substituting this into the fold shows that one round multiplies the lower half by a single factor, the challenge's inverse plus the challenge times that power.

In our run the first factor is twenty-three plus fifteen times thirty-eight, which is thirty-four, and thirty-four times one, nine gives the folded list thirty-four, five. The first entry starts as one, and each round multiplies it by that round's factor. So the final public entry is the product of the factors of all the rounds.

Replace the evaluation point with a variable, and this product defines a polynomial we'll call g of X. Each factor is a round's inverse challenge, plus that challenge times X raised to a power of two, and the final public entry is g evaluated at the point.

The coefficients of g are the entries of the weight list. To see this, expand the product by choosing one term from each factor. Choosing the challenge term adds a power of two to the exponent of X, and choosing the inverse term adds nothing. So the coefficient of each power of X comes from the single choice that matches its binary digits, which is the rule for the weight list.

With challenges fifteen and ten, g multiplies out to forty-one, fifteen, twenty-three, twenty-one. At nine its two factors are thirty-four and seventeen, and their product is nineteen, the final public entry. Ordering mistakes are easy to make here, so the script behind this chapter also checks three rounds with challenges three, five, and seven, and folding and g agree there too.

The Ragu Book numbers rounds downward, so its first challenge is our last one, and it calls the final generator G zero. This site avoids that name, because G zero already names the first public generator. After reversing the round order, the book's g and its listing of the weight list match ours.

This gives a compact statement of the verifier's costs, since the final generator is the Pedersen commitment to g. Take eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators. The final public entry takes twelve squarings and thirteen factor multiplications. The weight list takes about eight thousand one hundred ninety-two field multiplications, the final generator takes eight thousand one hundred ninety-two scalar multiplications, and combining the L and R points takes twenty-six.

Everything except the two tasks involving the whole weight list is logarithmic in the length. A recursive verifier cannot afford those two, because it would have to perform them inside a circuit. Chapter eleven, on accumulation, works from this exact point. It notices that the final generator is a claim about the commitment to a polynomial with a short description, namely the challenges, and it postpones checking that claim.

The code's one-sided fold.

The remaining sections compare this form with Ragu's implementation, which the code describes as adapted from Halo two. This section covers how the code folds, and the next covers what statement it folds.

Everything we say about the code in these sections is reconstructed from it at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The book uses the symmetric weights, while the prover code uses one-sided weights. This site teaches the symmetric form, because the book uses it and the g of X of later chapters comes from it. Where the question is what Ragu actually runs, the site follows the code.

Recall that the scale of each half is a free choice. The book spends this freedom on the symmetric split, while the code leaves the lower halves unweighted. To keep the conventions apart, we'll call its challenge the one-sided challenge. The new secret list is the lower half plus the inverse of the one-sided challenge times the upper half. The new public list and generators are the lower half plus the one-sided challenge times the upper half.

Repeating the expansion gives the same L and R, weighted by the inverse of the one-sided challenge and by the challenge itself. If the one-sided challenge is the square of a symmetric one, the update is identical. The folded lists then differ only by a scale factor, the symmetric challenge or its inverse, which cancels in both inner products.

Run the same statement with one-sided challenges ten and fourteen, the squares of fifteen and ten. The cross terms and the statement points after each round match the symmetric run, while the lists differ. The final scalar is thirty-five rather than four, and the final public entry is twelve rather than nineteen. The product of the symmetric challenges is twenty-one, and thirty-five times twenty-one is four modulo forty-three. Both forms pass their final checks.

The one-sided form also changes the polynomial. In the verifier code each factor is one plus the one-sided challenge times X raised to a power of two. For squared challenges, this product is the symmetric g times the product of the symmetric challenges. In our run its coefficients are one, fourteen, ten, eleven, and dividing them by twenty-one gives the coefficients of g.

The code's challenges are not literally squares, since a hash outputs them directly, and a uniform challenge could be a non-square. So the one-sided form is not a change of variables for every challenge, though the expansion holds for any nonzero challenge. The gap argument also carries over, with at most two surviving challenges per round.

The code's opening statement.

The book opens the commitment plus the claimed value times U directly. The code makes three changes to the statement before it starts folding.

The first change moves the value into the constant coefficient. The polynomial minus the claimed value has a root at the evaluation point exactly when the claim is true. Its commitment is the original commitment minus the value times the first generator, which the verifier can compute itself. The claim then says this commitment has inner product zero with the public list, so the starting point needs no U term.

The two forms are equivalent, because the verifier can move between them on its own. The code follows Halo two here, and the zero form has one practical advantage. Any other polynomial with a root at the point can be added to the claim without changing the claimed value, which stays zero.

The mask is such a polynomial, and it is the second change. The final scalar is a fixed linear combination of the coefficients, with weights the verifier can compute, so sending it reveals one piece of information about the secret list. To hide that combination, the prover picks a random polynomial with a root at the point, commits to it, and adds a random multiple of it.

For example, the random list eleven, four, nineteen, six takes the value twenty-six at nine. Subtracting twenty-six from the constant coefficient gives the mask twenty-eight, four, nineteen, six, which has a root at nine. The code uses the letter s for both this mask and the weight list, so we keep them apart by name.

After the prover commits to the mask, the verifier samples a mask challenge, and the combined list adds that many copies of the mask. Its inner product with the public list is the true value, plus nothing from the mask, minus the claimed value. So it is zero exactly when the claim is true. With a mask challenge of six, the combined list still evaluates to thirty-seven at nine, and after subtracting thirty-seven it has inner product zero.

The order matters for the same reason as before. If the mask did not vanish at the point, a prover who knew the mask challenge in advance could choose the mask to cover a false value. Suppose the prover claims thirty-eight and commits to the unadjusted mask, which takes the value twenty-six. It needs thirty-seven plus the challenge times twenty-six to equal thirty-eight, which takes a challenge of five. Across all forty-three challenges, only five accepts, a chance of one in forty-three.

The code's module documentation says the mask is retained for the folded coefficients. It also says the argument at this commit has no Pedersen blinding, so neither the commitment nor the L and R points carry a blinding term. The mask is therefore not a claim that the argument is zero knowledge. This matches the status recorded in chapter two, where the Ragu Book and code do not yet establish zero knowledge.

Scaling U by a challenge.

The third change closes the weakness from the end of the cheating section. A commitment with hidden copies of U can be opened to a false value when U has a fixed weight, because the hidden copies make up the difference between the true evaluation and the claim.

The code samples a scaling challenge after the commitment, the value, and the mask commitment are in the transcript, and it uses that challenge times U as the generator in every round. The hidden copies of U pass through the shifted and masked statement unchanged. The gap argument, run with the scaled U, then proves that the U coefficient of the starting point equals the scaling challenge times the inner product.

There are two cases. If the claim is true, the inner product is zero, so the hidden amount must be zero, and a hidden U term cannot help. If the claim is false, only one scaling challenge passes, the hidden amount divided by the inner product. The prover chose both before it saw the challenge, so it hits that value only by luck.

Here is the attack with numbers. The prover hides six copies of U in its commitment and claims thirty-one, which is thirty-seven minus six. The list behind the generator part still evaluates to thirty-seven, so after subtracting the claim the inner product is six. The spare six copies of U would supply the matching term if U carried weight one.

The formula predicts that only the scaling challenge one passes, and running all forty-two nonzero challenges confirms it. One is the case where the scaling does nothing. So a fixed, unscaled U would accept this claim every time, and a random scaling challenge accepts it with a chance of one in forty-two.

The code's comment says this challenge ensures the prover did not interfere with the U term in its commitments. The Halo two background page reaches the same goal by having the verifier select a random U before the argument begins. Ragu fixes U in its public parameters and randomizes its weight instead. The book's description does not mention this step, and the accumulation chapter uses the same idea with a challenge called tau.

A complete run of the code's form.

We can now run the code's protocol on the same claim, again with challenges chosen by hand. The code counts rounds upward from zero, uses G zero for the first public generator as this site does, and calls the final generator G prime zero.

The transcript already holds the commitment, the point nine, and the value thirty-seven. The prover sends the mask commitment, the verifier replies with a mask challenge of six and a scaling challenge of thirteen, and both compute the starting point.

In the first round the prover sends L and R, with the scaling challenge on U, and receives the one-sided challenge fourteen. In the second round it sends L and R again and receives seventeen, and its coefficient list folds down to the final scalar twenty-one, which it sends. The verifier skips the round-by-round fold. It computes the final public entry, thirty-eight, and the weight list, one, seventeen, fourteen, twenty-three, from the challenges alone, and one MSM gives the final generator.

The left side of the check is the starting point plus each L weighted by the inverse challenge and each R weighted by the challenge. The right side is the final scalar times the final generator, plus the final scalar times the final public entry times the scaling challenge, times U. Both sides come to the same point, so the verifier accepts.

At this commit the compression verifier computes the full linear MSM for the final generator, and the accumulation chapter covers the code's option to defer it.

The same run shows what the mask does to the revealed value. With the mask set to zero, the final scalar becomes thirty-three in place of twenty-one. Without the mask, that scalar is a fixed linear combination of the secret list, with weights anyone can compute from the public challenges. With the mask, it also depends on the mask's random entries, which the verifier never sees.

Comparing the two forms.

Both forms prove that a committed polynomial takes the claimed value at the point. The book uses a fixed generator Q and starts from an inner product equal to the claimed value. The code uses a challenge times a fixed U, and starts from a shifted, masked point with inner product zero. The book's weights are symmetric, and the code's are one-sided. The book's proof holds the L and R points and the final entry, and the code's proof also holds the mask commitment.

The Halo two background page uses yet another labeling, in which its L is this chapter's R. A reader comparing sources should check the pairing and not only the letter. Later chapters on this site use the symmetric g of X, and they point out the code's form where it matters.

Where this chapter leaves us.

Folding a list in half creates two cross terms, and weights that are inverses of each other keep the original commitment intact. One round sends L and R, receives a challenge, and produces a claim of the same shape at half the length. After a logarithmic number of rounds the prover sends one scalar.

A false claim has a nonzero gap, and each round lets at most four challenges clear it, provided the generators have no known relation and each challenge follows its cross terms. This proves the relation we started with, which matches the evaluation claim only when the commitment has no hidden U component.

The verifier's remaining cost is the final generator, an MSM the size of the list, while the final public entry takes only one factor per round. Ragu's code uses one-sided weights, moves the value into the constant coefficient, adds a mask, and scales U by a challenge, and it proves the same evaluation claim.

The inner product argument opens one polynomial at one point, and a proof in Ragu needs many openings. The next chapter, on batched openings, reduces many evaluation claims to a single one, so that one inner product argument can finish the job.
