Chapter seven. The inner product argument.

The last chapter reduced a polynomial evaluation to one statement about a secret list, and it finished the exchange by revealing that list. This chapter replaces the reveal with a proof whose size grows with the logarithm of the list's length. The idea is to turn a claim about a list into a claim of the same shape about a list half as long, and to repeat this until one scalar is left, which the prover can send.

We derive one halving round in full, run the argument by hand on the toy curve, and then look at what cheating would take and what the verifier pays. The last sections compare this textbook form with the version in Ragu's code, which differs in several visible ways while proving the same claim.

The claim to prove.

The prover claims to know a list such that the statement point equals that list's commitment, plus its inner product with the powers of the evaluation point, times the extra generator U. The halving step never uses the fact that the second list consists of powers, so until we need the powers again, we'll call it the public list. The verifier knows the generators, the public list, U, and the statement point. Only the prover knows the secret list. We keep the running example, with coefficients seven, two, five, three, the point nine, and the claimed value thirty-seven.

The general Bulletproofs relation has a secret second list with its own generators. The Ragu Book states that form and then sets those generators to zero for polynomial openings, because the powers are public. The book also writes f and Q where we use the secret list and U.

Halving produces cross terms.

To shorten the secret list, the prover has to combine its entries. A natural way is to split the list and the generators into lower and upper halves, and add the halves. Now compare the commitment before and after. The original pairs each half of the list with its own half of the generators. The shortened lists pair every half with every other half, because the inner product distributes over the sums. So the new point has the two original terms plus two extra ones, called cross terms, and they stand between the short lists and the original commitment.

For example, take the list three, one, eight, four, whose halves add to eleven, five. We use it here because the running list happens to have an upper-half term equal to the identity point, which would hide the effect. Its shortened lists land on the sum of all four pieces, a different point from the commitment.

The cross terms depend on the secret list, so the prover has to send them. That creates a new problem, because a prover who sends two points and then folds in a fixed, known way could pick them to make any claim look consistent. The fix is to weight the halves with a random challenge that the verifier picks after the prover has sent the cross terms, so the prover cannot tailor them to the weights.

Choosing the weights.

The weights must not disturb the two terms of the original commitment. Suppose we scale the upper half of the list by one scalar and the upper half of the generators by another. The upper-with-upper term picks up their product, so to keep weight one, they must be inverses of each other. This is why inverses of the challenge appear throughout the argument. One goes on the scalars and the other on the generators, and they cancel on the terms we want to keep.

Take the weight three, whose inverse modulo forty-three is twenty-nine. If we scale both upper halves by three and remove the cross pieces, the upper term carries a nine and we miss the commitment. If we scale the list by twenty-nine and the generators by three, removing the cross pieces leaves exactly the commitment.

The scale of each half is also free, as long as the product over a matching pair stays one. Bulletproofs and the Ragu Book spread the challenge over both halves, which puts the challenge's square and its inverse square on the cross terms and leads to a tidy formula later. Ragu's code leaves the lower halves unweighted, and we'll see that the two forms differ only by a rescaling.

One round of folding.

The public list pairs with the secret list the way the generators do, so it needs the same weights as the generators to keep the inner product intact. So a round works like this. The new secret list is the challenge times the lower half, plus the inverse times the upper half. The new public list and new generators are each the inverse times the lower half, plus the challenge times the upper half.

Expanding the new commitment gives four pieces. On lower-with-lower and upper-with-upper, the challenge and its inverse cancel, which leaves the original commitment. The lower list with the upper generators picks up the challenge squared, and the upper list with the lower generators picks up the inverse squared. The new inner product expands in the same way, because the public list has the same weights as the generators.

Now form the new statement point and collect terms by power of the challenge. The unweighted terms add up to the old statement point, and the other two brackets are the points the prover must send, which we name L and R. L combines the upper list with the lower generators, plus U times the upper list's inner product with the lower public entries. R does the same with the lower list and the upper halves.

This gives the update rule. The new statement point is the old one, plus the inverse of the challenge squared times L, plus the challenge squared times R. The verifier can compute this once it has L and R, and it can fold the public values itself, so only the new secret list stays with the prover. The result is a claim of the same shape at half the length. The prover must send L and R before it learns the challenge.

A complete run on the toy curve.

A list of four entries takes two rounds. We pick the challenges by hand so the numbers avoid accidental coincidences, and Fiat-Shamir would derive them from a transcript in a real proof.

In the first round, the two cross inner products are thirty-two and four, which the prover uses to compute and send L and R. The verifier sends the challenge fifteen, whose inverse is twenty-three, so L gets weight thirteen and R gets weight ten. The secret list folds to five, thirteen, and the public list folds to thirty-four, five. The verifier computes the new statement point from public values, and the prover reaches the same point from the folded list. The inner product changes as predicted, since thirty-seven plus thirteen times thirty-two plus ten times four is twenty modulo forty-three.

The second round repeats this with one entry per half. The cross inner products are twelve and twenty-five, and the challenge is ten, which gives weights forty and fourteen. The secret list folds to four, and the public list folds to nineteen. Again both computations of the statement point match.

All lists now have one entry, which we'll call the final scalar, the final public entry, and the final generator. The claim becomes that the statement point equals the final scalar times the sum of the final generator and the final public entry times U. The prover sends the scalar four. The verifier holds the other two values from folding, checks this one equation, and accepts. Unrolling the update rule puts the whole check in one line, as an implementation does, by adding each round's weighted L and R to the statement point.

The whole proof here is four points and one scalar, against the four scalars of a reveal. The saving appears only at larger lengths, because the proof grows with the logarithm.

The book and the code label these values differently. The Ragu Book numbers rounds downward and calls the final generator G zero. The code counts upward from zero and uses G zero for the first public generator. This site counts upward from one and doesn't call the final generator G zero, since that name already belongs to a toy curve point.

Why cheating fails.

The verifier never sees the secret list, so we need a reason to believe a false claim cannot pass. Start with a prover who claims thirty-eight and folds honestly. Across all one thousand seven hundred sixty-four pairs of nonzero challenges, the verifier always rejects, because the extra copy of U stays in the folded point. A cheater can do more, because L and R can be any points it likes. To see the limits of that freedom, we track one number through the protocol.

The gap.

Assume for now that the prover builds every point from the public generators with coefficients it knows, and that nobody knows a relation among the generators. Then each point has one expression, a combination of the generators plus a coefficient on U. Define the gap of a point as its coefficient on U, minus the inner product of its generator coefficients with the public list.

The statement is true exactly when the statement point has gap zero. The gap is linear, a generator's gap is minus its public entry, and the gap of U is one. By linearity, each folded generator has minus its folded public entry as its gap, so the final generator's gap is minus the final public entry. Adding the final public entry times U brings that to zero, whatever the final scalar is. So the right side of the final check always has gap zero.

With no relation among the generators, equal points have equal gaps. So a passing proof needs the left side to have gap zero as well, which means the statement point's gap plus every weighted gap of L and R must sum to zero.

Counting the challenges a cheater survives.

Suppose a round starts with a nonzero gap. The prover fixes L and R, and then the verifier picks the challenge. Writing w for the challenge squared, the new gap is zero only if the old gap, plus L's gap over w, plus R's gap times w, is zero. Multiplying through by w gives a quadratic in w whose middle coefficient is the old gap. That is nonzero, so the polynomial is not zero, whatever the prover chose. It has at most two roots, each with at most two square roots. So at most four challenges in a round can clear a nonzero gap, for every list length.

We can test this on the list seven, two with public list one, nine, where a false claim of twenty-six has gap one. Keep the honest generator coefficients in L and R, and try all one thousand eight hundred forty-nine choices of their U coefficients against all forty-two nonzero challenges. The most that ever pass is four, and the honest U coefficients pass none. A further five thousand six hundred twenty-five strategies with altered generator coefficients, taken from a grid, reach at most two.

A false statement must clear its gap in some round, so the chance is at most four times the round count, divided by the number of nonzero challenges. On the toy curve that is a large fraction, which is one more way the toy curve is insecure. For a length of two to the thirteen over a Pasta scalar field of about two to the two hundred fifty-four elements, the bound is below two to the minus two hundred forty-eight.

Why the challenge follows the cross terms.

The count relies on L and R being fixed before the challenge. Suppose the prover knows the first challenge is fifteen before sending R, and it wants to pass with the false claim of thirty-eight, whose point carries one extra U. It subtracts the challenge's inverse square times U from R. The weight on R turns that into exactly minus one U, so the folded point matches the honest one, and the verifier accepts.

The same altered R does poorly when the challenge comes afterward. Across all forty-two nonzero challenges, the verifier accepts only at fifteen and twenty-eight, the guess and its negative, which share a square. That is a chance of two in forty-two, and the gap equation predicts exactly those two challenges. In a non-interactive proof, this ordering becomes a transcript requirement, so every L and R must go into the hash before its challenge comes out.

Why generators must have no known relation.

The gap only makes sense if each point has one expression in the generators. On the toy curve every point is a known multiple of every other, so the final scalar can absorb any error. Keep the false claim of thirty-eight and the honest L and R. With challenges fifteen and ten, the prover divides discrete logs to get the final scalar forty-one, and the verifier accepts the false value. The discrete log relation assumption rules out this attack on a real curve.

So the argument rests on three assumptions. The discrete log relation assumption holds for the generators together with U. The prover builds its points from the generators with coefficients it knows. And the challenges are uniform and follow the points they weight, which in a non-interactive proof means treating the transcript hash as a random oracle. This reasoning is informal and is not a security proof.

The verifier's linear cost.

The proof is short, but the verifier still has to compute the final generator and final public entry. Folding the generators round by round costs about one scalar multiplication per generator in the first round, half that in the second, and so on. The verifier can do better by computing the final point in one step, which requires each original generator's final weight.

Substituting one round into the next shows the pattern. Each weight is a product with one factor per round. A round contributes its challenge if the generator sat in the upper half, and the inverse if it sat in the lower half. Read a generator's position in binary, and the first round looks at the highest bit. These products form what we'll call the weight list. Its inner product with the generators gives the final generator, and its inner product with the public list gives the final public entry. In our run the weight list is forty-one, fifteen, twenty-three, twenty-one, and both results match the folding.

This is one multi-scalar multiplication with one term per generator. It is cheaper than folding, but its cost still grows linearly with the length. No shortcut is available, because the generators are independent points with no structure to exploit.

The polynomial g of X.

The final public entry is cheaper, because the public list has structure the generators lack. Its entries are powers of the evaluation point, so each upper entry is a fixed power of the point times the matching lower entry. Substituting this into the fold shows that one round multiplies the lower half by a single factor, the challenge's inverse plus the challenge times that fixed power. In our run the first factor is thirty-four, and thirty-four times one, nine gives the folded list thirty-four, five.

Multiplying the factors of all the rounds gives the final public entry. Replace the point with a variable, and this product defines a polynomial we'll call g of X. Each factor is a round's inverse challenge, plus that challenge times X raised to a power of two.

The coefficients of g are the weight list. To see this, expand the product by choosing one term from each factor. Choosing the challenge term adds a power of two to the exponent, and choosing the inverse term adds nothing. So each power's coefficient comes from the single choice matching its binary digits, which is the rule for the weight list. With challenges fifteen and ten, g multiplies out to forty-one, fifteen, twenty-three, twenty-one. At nine, its factors are thirty-four and seventeen, whose product is nineteen. Ordering mistakes are easy to make here, so the script behind this chapter also confirms agreement for three rounds with challenges three, five, seven. The book's listing matches after relabeling rounds.

The final generator is therefore the Pedersen commitment to g, which gives a compact statement of the verifier's costs. Take eight thousand one hundred ninety-two generators. The final public entry takes twelve squarings and thirteen factor multiplications. The weight list takes about eight thousand one hundred ninety-two field multiplications, and the final generator takes eight thousand one hundred ninety-two scalar multiplications. Combining L and R takes twenty-six scalar multiplications.

Everything except the two tasks involving the whole weight list is logarithmic. A recursive verifier cannot afford those two, because it would have to perform them inside a circuit. The accumulation chapter works from this exact point. It notices that the final generator is a claim about the commitment to a polynomial with a short description, namely the challenges, and it postpones checking that claim.

The code's one-sided fold.

The remaining sections compare this form with Ragu's implementation, which the code describes as adapted from Halo two. Everything we say about the code here and in the next two sections is reconstructed from it at the commit this site pins, and the authors have not confirmed it. The Ragu Book uses symmetric weights and the prover code uses one-sided weights. This site teaches the symmetric form, because the book uses it and later chapters build on its g of X. Where the question is what Ragu actually runs, the site follows the code.

The code leaves the lower halves unweighted. The new secret list is the lower half plus the inverse of the one-sided challenge times the upper half. The new public list and generators are the lower half plus the one-sided challenge times the upper half. Repeating the expansion gives the same L and R, weighted by that challenge's inverse and by the challenge itself. If the one-sided challenge is the square of a symmetric one, the update is identical. The folded lists then differ only by a scale factor, which cancels in both inner products.

Run the same statement with one-sided challenges ten and fourteen, the squares of fifteen and ten. The cross terms and statement points match the symmetric run, while the lists differ. The final scalar is thirty-five rather than four, and the final public entry is twelve rather than nineteen. The product of the symmetric challenges is twenty-one, and thirty-five times twenty-one is four modulo forty-three. Both forms pass their final checks.

The one-sided form also changes the polynomial. The verifier code uses factors of one plus the one-sided challenge times X raised to a power of two. For squared challenges this equals the symmetric g times the product of the symmetric challenges, and in our run dividing by twenty-one recovers g's coefficients. The code's challenges are not literally squares, since a hash outputs them directly, and a uniform challenge could be a non-square. So the one-sided form is not a change of variables for every challenge, though the expansion holds for any nonzero challenge. The gap argument carries over, with at most two surviving challenges per round.

The code's opening statement.

The book opens the statement point directly. The code makes three changes before it starts folding, and each answers a specific need.

The first change moves the value into the constant coefficient. The polynomial minus the claimed value has a root at the point exactly when the claim is true. Its commitment is the original commitment minus the value times the first generator, which the verifier can compute. The claim now says this commitment has inner product zero with the public list, so the starting point needs no U term.

The second change adds a mask. The final scalar is a fixed linear combination of the coefficients, with weights the verifier can compute, so sending it reveals one piece of information. To hide that combination, the prover picks a random polynomial with a root at the point, commits to it, and adds a random multiple of it. For example, the random list eleven, four, nineteen, six takes the value twenty-six at nine, so subtracting twenty-six from its constant coefficient gives a root there. The code uses the letter s for this list and for the weight list, so a reader of the code should keep the two apart.

After the mask commitment arrives, a mask challenge sets the multiple. The folded statement is the original commitment, minus the value times the first generator, plus the mask challenge times the mask commitment. The new list's inner product with the public list is the true value, plus nothing from the mask, minus the claim, so it is zero exactly when the claim is true. With a mask challenge of six, direct evaluation confirms that the inner product is zero.

The order matters again. If the mask did not vanish at the point, it could shift the evaluation, and a prover who knew the mask challenge in advance could choose the mask to cover a false value. Suppose the prover claims thirty-eight and commits to the unadjusted mask, worth twenty-six at nine. It needs thirty-seven plus the challenge times twenty-six to equal thirty-eight, so it needs a challenge of five. Across all forty-three values the verifier accepts only at five, a chance of one in forty-three.

The mask changes the revealed value. With the same challenges and no mask, the final scalar is seven, a fixed combination of the coefficients. With the mask it is four, and it also depends on the random entries.

The module documentation says the mask is retained for the folded coefficients, and also that the argument at this commit has no Pedersen blinding. So neither the commitment nor L and R carry a blinding term, and the mask is not a claim of zero knowledge. This matches the status recorded in chapter two, where the book and code do not yet establish zero knowledge. The documentation also records a limitation of unblinded round commitments. A valid opening can produce an identity L or R, for example R with two coefficients at the point zero, and the transcript rejects identity points.

The third change scales U by a challenge, because a fixed U leaves a weakness. A prover could send a commitment with a hidden U component. That point commits to no polynomial under the generators alone, but it can still be opened, because the extra component makes up the difference between the true evaluation and a false claim.

So the code samples a scaling challenge after the commitment, the value, and the mask commitment are in the transcript, and it uses that challenge times U in every round. The final check now requires the hidden component to equal the scaling challenge times the inner product. The prover chose both before seeing the challenge, so a nonzero hidden component passes for only one challenge. For example, the prover adds six copies of U and claims thirty-one, which is thirty-seven minus six. Across all forty-two nonzero challenges, the verifier accepts only at the challenge value one, where the scaling does nothing. A fixed U would accept this claim every time, and a random challenge accepts it with probability one in forty-two.

The code's comment says this challenge ensures the prover did not interfere with the U term in its commitments. The Halo two background page reaches the same goal by having the verifier select a random U before the argument begins. Ragu fixes U in its public parameters and randomizes its weight instead, and the book's description of the relation does not mention this step.

A complete run of the code's form.

We can now run the code's protocol on the same claim, again with hand-picked challenges. The transcript already holds the commitment, the point nine, and the value thirty-seven. The prover sends the mask commitment, the verifier replies with a mask challenge of six and a scaling challenge of eleven, and both compute the starting point.

In the first round the prover sends L and R and receives nineteen, and the secret and public lists fold to thirteen, nineteen and thirty-five, fourteen. In the second round it receives seventeen, and the lists fold to four and fifteen. The prover sends the final scalar four.

The verifier skips round-by-round folding and computes the final public entry fifteen and the weight list from the challenges. Its left side is the starting point plus each weighted L and R. Its right side is the final scalar times the final generator, plus the final scalar times the final public entry times the scaling challenge times U. Both sides come to the same point.

At this commit the compression verifier checks this argument, and its call chain runs the full linear multi-scalar multiplication. The code's guard type also offers a path that accepts a claimed final generator and returns the challenges alongside it as an accumulator. No code in that crate outside this module calls that path at this commit.

Comparing the two forms.

Both forms prove the same evaluation claim, and their differences come down to a few choices. The book uses a fixed generator Q and starts from an inner product equal to the claimed value. The code uses a challenge times a fixed U and starts from a masked, shifted point with inner product zero. The book's weights are symmetric, and its proof holds the L and R points and the final entry. The code's weights are one-sided, and its proof also holds the mask commitment.

The Halo two background page uses a third labeling, in which its L is this chapter's R, so a reader comparing sources should check the pairing and not only the letter. Later chapters use the symmetric g of X, and they point out the code's form where it matters.

Where this leaves us.

Folding a list in half creates two cross terms, and inverse weights keep the original commitment intact. One round sends L and R, receives a challenge, and produces a claim of the same shape at half the length, so the proof ends up with two points per round and one scalar. A false claim has a nonzero gap, and each round lets at most four challenges clear it, provided the generators have no known relation and each challenge follows its cross terms.

The verifier's remaining cost is the final generator, a multi-scalar multiplication the size of the list, while the final public entry takes one factor per round. Ragu's code uses one-sided weights, moves the value into the constant coefficient, adds a mask, and scales U by a challenge, and it proves the same claim.

The argument opens one polynomial at one point, and a proof in Ragu needs many openings. The next chapter reduces many evaluation claims to a single one, so that one inner product argument can finish the job.
