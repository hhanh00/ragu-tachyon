# Writing listening scripts

These scripts turn the chapters in `docs/` into an audiobook read by a text-to-speech voice. The listener has only their ears, cannot look back at a formula, and may be walking or driving. The reference for voice, density, and technique is `audio/scripts/06-polynomial-commitments.txt`. Read it in full, alongside `docs/polynomial-commitments.md`, before writing anything.

## Replacing what can't be heard

Replace every equation and table with prose instead of dropping its content. Say what an equation does as a procedure or a relationship ("multiply each entry by the generator for its position, then add up the results"), and carry its logical role into the sentence that follows.

Narrate a table when it makes a point, as a short story of what changes and what stays the same. When a table or a run of numbers only confirms a result, state what it confirms in a sentence or two, keeping a number only if the listener can hold it in their head. Keep the worked examples that show something happening, such as a forgery succeeding, and tell them with small numbers.

Keep all the concepts, arguments, and reasons for each step, and keep the informal explanation of why cheating fails. Each protocol step should survive, though the listener needs the idea and its order rather than a term-by-term expansion.

Expect a script of roughly 75 to 80 percent of the chapter's non-math word count. Shorter is fine when a chapter's bulk is numeric, but don't remove arguments to reach a length.

## Naming things

Refer to objects by what they are. Use a role-based name ("the challenge point", "the claimed value", "the statement point") rather than a symbol. A letter is acceptable only when a chapter juggles several similar objects and a letter genuinely helps, and then introduce it in a sentence first ("we'll call it U").

Call vectors "lists" when introducing an idea, as the reference script does. Later chapters may say "vector" where that reads more naturally, once the listener has heard the term. Keep the site's technical terms (Pedersen commitment, accumulation, revdot, endoscalar, nullifier, and so on) and define each at its first appearance in the same way the chapter does.

Replace visual and positional references. "Section 4", "the table below", "the figure above", and "as shown" become "the binding argument from earlier" or "the chapter on batched openings". Drop links, and describe a diagram only if it carries an idea that the surrounding text doesn't.

Leave out code listings. Describe in a sentence what the code does when the chapter uses it to make a point.

## Accuracy

Everything in a script must come from its chapter. Add no new claims or numbers, and copy any number exactly from the chapter text, because the worked numbers are generated by scripts in `docs/worked/`.

Keep the site's provenance statements, because they are claims about what is known. When the chapter labels something "Reconstructed from code at commit X, not confirmed by the authors", say that it is reconstructed from the code and that the authors have not confirmed it, and say "at the commit this site pins" instead of reading out a hash. When the book and the code disagree, give both and keep the site's stated reading.

## Writing style

Follow the prose rules in `CLAUDE.md`. In particular, give sentences real subjects and active verbs, motivate a step before asserting it, avoid "simply" and "of course", and use plain declarative headings. Connect ideas with "because", "so", and "which means". Avoid colon- and dash-heavy sentences, since neither sounds like anything when spoken.

Listening adds a few requirements. Use mostly short and medium sentences, one idea each, and keep every sentence under 40 words. Signpost often, and say where the argument has got to before moving on. Restating an object's role when it returns after a gap is helpful here, because the listener can't scroll back, though the rule against repeating a whole point in different words still applies.

## File format

The text goes straight to the speech engine, so:

- Use plain text with paragraphs separated by one blank line and no line breaks inside a paragraph.
- Begin with `Chapter <number in words>. <Title>.` as its own paragraph, using the number given in your assignment.
- Put each spoken section heading in its own paragraph, ending with a period, at most 7 words. Every other paragraph must be longer than 7 words, because the renderer uses that word count to detect headings and adds pauses around them.
- Write all numbers in words ("forty-three", "two to the two hundred fifty-five"). Write out abbreviations you want spoken as words.
- Use only letters, digits-free words, and the punctuation `. , ; ' ? -` (hyphens only within compound words). Use no markdown, symbols, Greek letters, brackets, slashes, or parentheses. Spell out Greek letters and symbols by name if you must mention them.
- Write "Halo 2" as "Halo two".
- End the chapter with a short spoken wrap-up and a pointer to the next chapter, following the chapter's own closing.
